# Chapter 772

## The Twenty-Fifth Of The Twenty-First, And A Man Putting His Hands On A Valve Wheel In Front Of Everybody And Nothing At All Coming Back, And The Yard Going On Working Around Him While It Did Not

He was at the middle gate at about the seventh hour with his hands out before anybody had thought to look for him, and Nia Vale was at the gauge because she is always at the gauge, and Emrys Dole was at the wheel because he has been at the wheel every morning since the frost.

It is a small thing to do at that wheel. It is not a lever and it does not take a shoulder. You put the flat of your hand on the cold of the metal and you wait, and what comes back up the iron is where the water is going and how fast, and a man who has stood at that gate for nine years can do it with two fingers and has done it four times a morning in weather that would take the skin off.

He did it for about a minute and a half. Nothing came back. He took his hand off and put it on again lower down, where the pipe runs before it turns, and waited the length of a slow cup and nothing came back there either.

Nia Vale watched the gauge the whole time and read it twice and said both readings out loud because she has said them out loud every morning since the sixth hour on the day before yesterday, and neither reading moved.

**"Nothing on the gauge either,**" she said, to Emrys Dole, not to him. **"Two hundred and forty-six is where it was at the sixth hour and two hundred and forty-six is where it is now and the water has not stopped and has not gone anywhere.** She wrote it on the back of her own hand. **"I am saying that out loud for the four people who are standing about nine feet away and not saying it to anybody in particular, because in about a week there is going to be a story in this yard that something went wrong at that wheel this morning and there was nothing wrong at that wheel this morning except that a man put his hand on it."**

He put it on a third time, at the joint where the cold iron comes up out of the ground, which is the oldest iron in this holding and the only one he has ever been able to get anything at all out of, and he waited longer at that one than at the other two, and about nine people there watched him wait.

Then he took his hand off and put it behind his back, which is what he does with his hands when he does not want a yard to look at them.

Emrys Dole did not say anything. He had his mouth open at one point and he shut it again and turned the wheel a quarter and left it where it was, which was not a thing he needed to do at that hour.

Nobody there stopped him, and nobody explained it to him, and nobody said one word about it to him at any hour of that day, and nobody in the yard said sorry to him about it either. What happened is that the yard went on.

Nia Vale read the gauge a third time at the eighth hour. The boy who carries water carried water. The man who lays for three councils laid the fifth course he had said he would lay at the fourth hour and told nobody he had said anything about it the day before. A woman from the middle row put a barrow down at the middle table and took it up again. A mason from the third place changed his mind about a line on a sheet in his own hand and initialled it in his own hand and told nobody, and it was not the man at the middle table and it was not the third line.

What Marek Vale did there for the rest of that morning was watch people, and he did it well.

He knew at about nine feet off that the mason from the fourth place was going to say something to him and was going to decide not to, and he knew it about two seconds before the man decided, from the man's hands, and he let the man decide. He knew that the woman from the middle row had a question about the north end and had decided not to ask it, and he did not look at her again so that she could finish not asking it. He read a whole morning of about thirty people out of the corners of his eyes while not being able to get anything at all out of two fingers on cold iron, and about four of the people in front of him were read correctly and about four were not and one of the four was a woman who had come up from the second place with her sleeves wet and who was about to say something she had not planned.

That is not the same capacity, and this holding has no word for either of them and printed none.

Hanne Brack came up from the second place at the ninth hour and gave the weight and the morning of the run from the boards, in the order she has used all week, and put the chalk back in the tray.

**"One of them is nine hundred and nineteen and it is at the bottom, and the other is nine hundred and sixty-six and it is at the top, and forty-seven of masonry sits between them.** She wiped her hands down her front. **"What came down in the night was one thousand and four hundred and ten hundredweight, and that is the five hundred and thirty-fifth morning of the run, and it is down five, and I have said that number in this yard every morning this month and today I would like somebody in this yard to come and take it off me, because I am going to stop.** And I am not going to stop because of anything that happened at that gate this morning. I am going to stop because I have got a house to be in at the fifth hour and I am sixty-one and I have been up that lane since before the light nine days running."

Odile Vray wrote the two stones on the board at the seventh hour and then came down off the ladder and read the weight and the morning of the run out loud at the bottom of it, which she has never done, and about six people came and looked at the board to see whether she had got it right.

**"One thousand and four hundred and ten hundredweight in the night and it is down five, and the five hundred and thirty-fifth morning of it, and I wrote it on the board before I came down and it says the same thing as it says out loud, because I have not got a second figure in me and I am not going to pretend to one.** She put the chalk down. **"The near stone is nine hundred and nineteen and the top one is nine hundred and sixty-six and I put them on in that order and it is the order they go up in.** **"And I am not going to be a woman who reads out numbers off a wall. I am a woman who writes numbers on a wall. That is a different job and it is not a promotion and nobody has offered me one."**

The man who pushes the barrow gave the window at about the tenth hour from the middle of the yard with the shafts up over his shoulder, in the wrong order for the third morning running.

**"Five hundred and two of window, and two hundred and forty-six came this morning and two hundred and fifty-six stood. I know it is the wrong order because two men told me so this week, and I have said it the same way for eleven days because when I get to the wall I am thinking about whether I have eleven journeys left and not about which half is which.** He set the shafts down. **"I am the man who says eleven journeys every morning of this holding's record and I have said it every morning and I will say it tomorrow, and I am not a man for figures off a wall and I would like to be left alone about it."**

A woman from the middle row read the long sheet at about the tenth hour and did not wipe it, and made a point of saying that she had read it right.

**"Five hundred and thirty-third sits at the head of that sheet, five hundred and thirty-second under the rule, and five hundred and thirty-fourth above it, and those are three numbers and not one number in three places, which is the other thing that happens on that wall.** She stood back from the wall. **"I have read that wall since I was eleven years old and nobody in this holding has ever once asked me to.** **"And I will say the thing that four of us have been saying to each other on the middle road for a fortnight and that nobody has said in this yard. There is a third number that goes with those three and it is not on that wall and I have never seen it anywhere and my mother did not have it either.** And I am not asking for it. I am saying that I know it is missing and that four households at the end of my branch cannot read a line, and those two things have been sitting in the same place in my head for about a month and they are going to stay sitting there."

Sera Quill was not at the boards, because it was not the morning, and four or five people went to look for her and she was in the one room with the drawer shut and the key on its nail.

A man from the fourth place came up to the middle table at about the tenth hour and asked Tova Reed, in front of about nine people, for a date, and not for anything else, and he had been told by somebody that the correct answer was a season and he had written the season on a paper.

**"Second place. When does it get water.** He put the paper on the table. **"I have been told a season. I have written the season down. I would like to know whether I am going to have to scratch it out in front of my own household."**

Tova Reed did not come out from behind the trays. She stayed where she could see his mouth and about four other mouths, and she answered him in the yard in a voice that was not raised and was not slow.

**"The comfort is not standing, the father is not alive in the wood, and the day does not come back.** She did not move her hands. **"I have three things that do not come back and I have not said them in one breath at this door before and I am not saying them for you. You came to me with a season on a piece of paper and there is no season, and I am not going to tell you when, because I do not know when and because a date I have not got is worse than no date at all, and every person in this holding who has ever given you one was telling you the thing they wanted to be true.** She picked up the corner of her board. **"Your household should scratch it out. And when they ask you why, tell them the seed keeper will not put a date on it, and if they want to argue with me about it they can come and stand at this door at the sixth hour and I will be here with my sleeves rolled up and I will not be nice about it."**

Nobody said sorry to her about it and nobody argued with her about it and four or five people at the middle table read the three things on her face instead of on the paper.

She gave her four after that, from the step, in the order she has used for a week, and she wrote each one on the corner without being asked and without looking down at her own hand while she did it.

**"Three hundred and thirty-five on the charter, and it is the oldest of the four and it is the only one of the four that anybody has ever read out. Four hundred and twenty-three under Silling's second one, and blank, and it has been blank for nineteen days and it is going to be blank at four hundred and twenty-four tomorrow.** She got a thumb under the corner of the board and tipped it up against her hip. **"Four hundred and twenty-nine on the form. And the letter is at two hundred and thirty-two, and I have stopped saying anything about the letter, and I want that on the record from my own mouth, because I have said it in this yard six times this week to a yard that has never once written it down and I am not going to be a woman who keeps saying a thing into an empty yard.** And a table of results goes out on this wall in about a week and it is going to have everything in it that failed, and it is going to have my place on it, and I am not going to put it there myself because a thing I put up myself looks like an argument."

**"Who said the father was not alive in the wood."**

**"Nobody,"** Tova Reed said, **"and that is exactly why I said it. It is a thing that has been said in this holding for years and nobody in it knows who said it first, and you have just heard me say it, and now you know it was me, and it does not make it true or untrue and it never did.** She went back inside and shut the door behind her and did not shut it hard.

Kellan Rusk came out to the step with the register form under his arm and gave the figure from the step and went back in inside a minute, which is a minute shorter than the one he took yesterday.

**"Four hundred and twenty-nine on the form. Not entered. The key is on the nail.** He shut the door. **"And I have nothing to add to yesterday and I am not going to pretend to."**

By the time the light was properly on the wall the north board had four hundred and fifty-nine written under the rule on it and two hundred and fourteen written over, and nobody had been up that branch to see her do it except the chalk dust on her hands, and there was nobody at that trough.

The door nine hundred yards off was walked past twice before the light and once at the ninth hour and read on by nobody and offered to nobody.

A man of about seventy was not fetched that Thursday and no question of any kind was put to him at any hour of it, and the count of questions this holding has put to that man in four years remains not one.

The barrow went eleven journeys and stopped, and the man who pushes it said eleven out loud to nobody, and about four people in that yard heard him say it and about four did not.

And at about the eleventh hour a man who could read thirty people in a yard and could not get anything at all out of two fingers on cold iron was still at the middle gate, and had been for four hours, with his hands behind his back, and nobody had come and got him and nobody had sent him away and nobody in that yard had been told a single thing about what it meant.
