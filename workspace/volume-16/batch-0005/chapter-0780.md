# Chapter 780

## The Third Morning Of A New Calendar, And A Farmer Coming Up The Middle Road At The Ninth Hour To Ask Which Line Matters On His Own Field, And A Man Putting A Book Under His Arm And Saying Something Ordinary About A Pump

Roan Selk came up the low road before the light on the last of those mornings and got to the top of it at about the fourth hour, and there was a light coming up out of the ground along the high side of the road where the hedge is and the ground is bare under it, and it was not on the hedge and it was not on the water and it was not steady, and it was gone by about the seventh hour.

Not one person in this holding was on that road at any hour of that morning, and nobody here sent anybody onto it.

At about the same hour there was light lying along the surface of the water in the channel inside the gate, flat, moving when the water moved and stopping when the water stopped, and it was still on that water at the eleventh hour with nothing about it that anybody in that yard could have pointed at and named.

Nobody in that yard said the two of them were the same and nobody said they were different, and nobody had been asked about either one, and nobody was going to be.

The man who lays for three councils was up on the wall at about the sixth hour and gave two figures to about nine people at the foot of it without coming down.

**"What came down in the night was one thousand and four hundred and twenty-two hundredweight, which is down five on the morning behind it and puts the run at its five hundred and forty-third morning.** He set his hod down on the stack. **"I put four courses on yesterday and four of them are going on this morning before the light is off that wall, and I have not got a second opinion about it and nobody has asked me for one.** He picked the hod up. **"There is a thing in the water this morning that has been there two mornings now. I have said one word about it to two people and neither of them was you, and if you want to ask me about it you can ask me at the dark when I am putting my tools away."**

Nobody asked him at the dark either. He went down the middle road at about noon to a house of his own that has stood empty since the frost, and he would be back on that wall before the light in the morning, and everybody in that yard knew it and about four of them said so to each other on the way out of the gate.

A man came up the lane at about the sixth hour and a half and stood at the gate, and about nine people in that yard knew him and one or two did not, and he said his piece and said it in about four sentences.

**"There are two of us on the paper for our household and I am the second one, and I am telling this yard before I do anything else with my morning that we are stopping."** Nobody moved. **"I am not asking anybody for anything and I am not telling the man at the middle table first, because I told this yard first, and that is the way it is done now, and I have watched it done once this week.** He put his hand flat on the gate post for a moment and took it off again. **"Four households are on that paper with two signatures on each. There are four this morning and there were four yesterday and both of mine are still on it, and the one standing in your gate is me. There is no list in this yard with a line through anything on it and there is not going to be one.** About four people wanted to say something and about four of them said nothing, and nobody asked him why, and nobody asked him to wait, and nobody asked him to say it again tomorrow, and nobody said the word anybody would have used for it.

He went out of the gate and down the lane at about the seventh hour and about nine people watched him go and none of them followed him.

Tova Reed put her board on the end of the same wall at about half past six on the third morning since the calendar turned, and chalked a line on it with a place against it and nothing else, and then stood where she could see the mouths of about nine people and read it out.

**"That place has no water. It had none before the flood and it has none now and it will have none at the dark.** She did not point at it while she said it, which is not something she has ever done at that wall before. **"It is not apportioned. It is not dated. It is not held up as a thing that is going to be put right, because nobody in this holding has said that and I have not said it and there is no arrangement signed here that covers it.** She put the chalk down and then picked it up again. **"The comfort is not standing, the father is not alive in the wood, and the day does not come back.** She said them at the speed she says her four figures, and then she picked the chalk up again, and nobody in that yard said a word back to her. **"Nobody got them from me,**" she said, to the chalk.

Nobody laid a hand on her arm at any hour of that day, and about four people in that yard spent a good part of it standing where they could have done it and did not.

She gave her four from the step at about the ninth hour in an order she has used four times this month, and she wrote them on the corner one at a time, and then she wrote one more thing under them.

**"Four hundred and thirty-seven is the form. Three hundred and forty-three is the charter. Two hundred and forty is the letter. Four hundred and thirty-one under Silling's second one. That rule is empty this morning, I have not written on it this morning, and I am not going to be standing at that store door at the sixth hour tomorrow looking at it again.** She put the chalk in her apron and then took it out again and went back to the corner of the board and wrote two more figures underneath her own four, small and squared off, in the same chalk. **"That is all that is on that corner. I am not going to read them out at the trough and I am not going to read them out here, and nobody has asked me to and nobody is going to.** She wiped the corner with her thumb, and the two figures stayed. Under her own four, in the same chalk, small and squared off, two hundred and eighteen of four hundred and sixty-seven. Nobody said either of them out loud and nobody asked her what they were for.

A boy of about nine was waiting for his mother at that door for about a quarter of an hour and looked at that corner about nine times and did not ask her about it, and she did not tell him to go away, and about four people watched him not ask.

**"And six ruled rows are on that shelf with no seventh one cut under any of them, and four lines are ruled under two words with nothing on them, and the things this holding is keeping unanswered are the same number this morning that they were when I came here, and nothing has been taken off that list and nothing has been put on it.** She picked up the tray. **"And it is thirty-five in and thirty-five out and that is the only way anybody in this yard is going to hear it from me, and nobody in this yard is going to ask me what the thirty-five are, and if somebody did I would not tell them."**

The man from the middle row read the long sheet off the wall at about the tenth hour, having gone and stood in front of it by himself, and he got it right, and about six people stood near enough to hear him get it right.

**"Five hundred and forty-one at the head of that wall. Under the rule it says the five hundred and fortieth out of five hundred and forty-second, and the five hundred and forty-first is the one above the rule, and those three are a day apart and none of them is the head of it.** He came away from the wall. **"I have been trying to add three of those numbers together for four weeks and I have been stopped for it once and I am not going to try it this morning, because I was told the first time and I am not a fool, I am just a man who likes a total.** He wiped his hands on his coat. **"And I am going to ask the thing I have come up here to ask about nine times this month, and I am asking it one last time in this yard because I am not coming back up here for it after this morning.** He looked round at about nine people. **"Is that joint on."**

The woman who is the neighbour of the second place was standing about eleven feet off with her hands in front of her and she answered him without waiting for anybody else to.

**"The far end of a thing is held by two people and neither of them is me, and no instrument anywhere in four counties says whether it is on or off.** Nobody in that yard said yes and nobody said no. The woman with a barrow that is not the yard's came through the gate about four minutes later, looked at about nine people standing still, said nothing at all to any of them, and carried the barrow through to the far end.

Roan Selk read two figures off the boards at about the tenth hour and a half and gave them with a nail behind his ear and did not touch the wall.

**"Five hundred and ten between them. Two hundred and fifty came down this morning and two hundred and sixty stood, and those two are the highest that pair has stood at since this holding began putting them up a wall.** He put the nail in his pocket. **"I was up that low road before the light and I am going to be up it again before the light, and there is a thing at the top of it that I have not described to anybody and I am not going to describe it to you in a yard with a wall behind me, and you have all stopped asking me about it, which is the correct thing to have done.** He looked at the gate. **"And the answer that woman gave that man two minutes ago is the same answer I have given four times this season, and this morning is the last morning there is going to be one of these in this yard, and I am not going to make anything of that either."**

The girl of about seventeen from the sluice road came through with a chain and went to the boards and read two figures off a piece of paper in her coat and then put the paper away.

**"Nine hundred and twenty-seven at the near end and nine hundred and seventy-four at the top, and forty-seven of masonry between them, and that paper has been on a nail inside my mother's door since the middle of the summer and anybody on this road can go and read it.** She shifted the chain. **"Hanne Brack has not been on this road before the light in five days and she is sixty-one and she said she would stop and she stopped, and somebody else has to say those two numbers now and I am somebody else.** She did not say Hanne Brack's name again and nobody asked her about it.

At about the ninth hour a farmer came up the middle road from the low end and stood in that yard with his hands behind his back and asked his question in the middle of about twenty people, and he was not shy about it and he was not ashamed of it and he asked it in the voice of a man who has a field and cannot eat what is on it.

**"There is a light in two places on this road and I have seen both of them, one up where the hedge is and one lying on the water, and they have been there three mornings.** He looked at the man at the middle table. **"I am not asking anybody what either one is. I am asking which of the two matters on a field, because I have a field at the bottom of that road and I have got to put four days of work into it in the next week and I would like to do it in the right place.** He waited. **"That is the whole of my question and I have walked up here to ask it and I would like an answer in words and not a figure."**

Marek Vale had a book under his arm that nobody in that yard had ever asked him about and had carried for longer than anybody there had been counting.

He did not open it. He turned round and put it under the other arm and then took it back, and put it away somewhere against his side where his hands were free, and shut the cover of it with the flat of his hand as he did it, and nobody in that yard said a word about it and about four of them looked at the movement and then looked away.

**"The high one matters to you.** He said it at once and he said it in the voice he uses with a mason. **"Not because it is brighter. Because that road at the top of the lane runs above the level of the water and the one at the bottom of your field is in it, and one of those two goes in a hard week and the other one waits, and it is never the same week for both.** He took the gauge off its nail and carried it to the pump at the second house on the mill lane and read it in front of about nine people, twice, and read it a second time with the boy who carries water standing on the step holding the lamp.

**"Eleven feet of head on this pump and eleven feet of head an hour ago, and that is a pump with a hand on the handle twice a day and not a channel and not a plate and not anything anybody in this yard has written about this month.** He put the gauge back on its nail. **"Put your four days on the high side. Stay off the bottom. And I will walk your field myself this week and bring you a figure for it and it will not be a figure off a wall, it will be a figure off your ground.** He looked at the farmer. **"That is my answer and it is not about the two lights and I am not going to tell you what either of them is because I do not know, and a man who tells you he knows is selling you a morning."**

The farmer said that was a plain answer and he would take it, and he asked one more thing, which was whether the high side of that road was on somebody's paper, and Marek Vale told him it was on two hands of paper belonging to the households up there and not on anything of this holding's, and the farmer said he would go and read it on the way down, and he did not go and read it, and Marek Vale did not offer to take him.

Nia Vale read the gauge at the middle gate at the sixth hour and at the seventh and at the tenth and gave the three numbers out loud each time, and they were the same three numbers, and the water went down that channel at the rate it went down it on the morning before and the morning before that, and she said so out loud twice and nobody asked her to say it a third time.

A man from the fourth place asked him at about the eleventh hour to go and look at a branch three miles up and come back and tell him what was under it.

**"I will go,"** Marek Vale said. **"I am not going to be able to tell you what is under it and I would rather say that here, in this yard, at about the eleventh hour on this morning, than have you find it out alone in a field in a month's time.** He put his hands behind his back. **"I can stand in this yard and read about four of these people from across it and tell you what they are going to do before they do it. I have been able to do that since I was a boy. I have never once been able to do the other thing and I am not going to get better at it this month."**

Nobody said sorry to him about it and nobody said anything about it at all, and the farmer was still in the yard, and the woman with a barrow was at the far end of it, and the man who lays for three councils came in off the middle road about a quarter past twelve with his tools under his arm and asked nobody for anything.

At about the eleventh hour there were about twenty-one people in that yard and the water was going down the channel at the rate it goes down. The two figures chalked in the corner of a board at the end of a wall were under four other figures in the same chalk and had been under them since about the ninth hour. The farmer went out of the gate about a quarter past eleven and down the middle road and said nothing to anybody on his way past the boards, and about nine people watched him go and about four of them knew which field he was going to and the rest did not.

The long table in the one room held the same four pieces of paper it has held since four districts put them there, and not one of the four has been taken up and entered and not one has been handed back refused, and nobody cut a column under any of them to make room for what happened this morning. The sheet of terms out past the gate was left lying where the rain gets at it. The paper with a Chancellor's name at the head of it was still on the middle table, unpicked, undated, unfilled in, not withdrawn, and nobody looked at it once this morning except a woman who went round the table and did not stop at it.

And at about the fifth hour of the afternoon Marek Vale went out past the gate and down the middle road toward the low end of it, at the pace of a man going to look at ground, with a barrow and a line and a stub of chalk over his shoulder, and nobody was sent with him and nobody asked him to go, and about nine people watched him out of the gate and about four of them knew which field he was going to.
