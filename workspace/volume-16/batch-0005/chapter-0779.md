# Chapter 779

## The Second Morning Of A New Calendar, And Two Of Eleven Households Going Up A Lane In Two Hands And Stopping At The Head Of It, And A Woman At The Boards Saying A Weight Out Loud While Two People Read One Gauge Twice

The pump at the second house on the mill lane had to be primed again at about the fifth hour, and the boy who carries water primed it and said nothing about it to anybody, and it was the fourth morning in a row and there is no entry for it anywhere and there is not going to be one.

Two of the eleven households went up the lane at about the sixth hour with a mason from the fourth place, and they took the paper with them, and they stopped at the head of the lane where the paper says to stop, and nobody came down past a gate, because there is no gate on it.

> **THE LANE, AS IT IS NAMED ON THEIR OWN PAPER IN TWO HANDS. IT IS NOT ON A BOARD IN THIS HOLDING AND IT IS NOT ON ANY BOARD IN FOUR DAYS NORTH AND IT IS NOT PUT ON ONE.**
>
> **Out of the second place's road, up off the dry branch, and past the second bend. Then along the high side of the third place's road, above the wall, where the ground is above the level of the water and is not the ground the water is in.**
>
> **Then to the head of the lane, and there it stops. There is no gate at the head of it. There is no holding at the head of it. There is nothing kept at the head of it.**
>
> **That paper is in two hands. Both of those two came up the lane this morning, and one of them had walked it twice before and the other had not walked it at all.**
>
> **It does not come down again. It does not pass this gate and it is not counted with any other road anybody keeps a figure on, and nobody in this holding is to add it to anything.**
>
> **There is another sheet with two place names on it. It is not this one. It is in the inside pocket of a coat at this gate and it has not been taken out and nobody in this yard has argued for it since the households spoke.**

They were back down at about the eleventh hour, wet to the knee, and neither of them said a word to anybody about what they had found, and about nine people in that yard asked them and got nothing, and the mason from the fourth place said on the way past that the two of them would speak when they were ready and not before and that he was not going to be asked either.

Nia Vale read the gauge at about the seventh hour and then had it read again by a man from the middle row who wanted to be helpful, and the two of them agreed, and she read it a third time at the ninth hour for herself and did not say that number out loud.

Auret Sill came in from the pumps at about the eighth hour and went to the boards and put her hand flat on them and said one figure out loud in front of about twenty people, at the top of her voice, and waited.

**"There was one thousand and four hundred and twenty-seven hundredweight in that channel between the fourth hour and the fifth this morning.** She kept her hand on the board. **"Up eight. That is the five hundred and forty-second morning of the run and I have said that number out loud in six yards this month and this is the yard I have said it in most.** She wiped her hands on her apron and left the chalk where it was, because it was not hers. **"That water went past my pumps between the fourth hour and the fifth and it did not go into them, and that is the only sentence I have got about it and I am going to say it once and then I am going back to the pumps.** She went back to the pumps.

Nobody said anything about the figure. Two men at the wall looked at one another and one of them said the courses at the end were going on better this week than last, and the other one said something about a cart of lime, and that was all that either of them said, and about nine people went back to what they were doing.

The clerk who is paid by two councils between them came through the gate at about the ninth hour with two sheets of his own under his arm and did not stop at the boards first, which nobody had seen him do before.

**"There is a slip of paper in here that came up the lane from the fourth place last week with a copy of the window on it, and there is the wall behind me, and I would rather you watched me hold the two of them up side by side than took my word for it from a desk four days north.** He held both up. **"They are the same. Five hundred and nine between them, two hundred and sixty came this morning and two hundred and forty-nine stood, and on the slip it is the tall half that came and the short one that stood, which is right, and I have done the working twice because that is the only part of my employment anybody pays me for.** He put one sheet down and kept the other. **"I am paid by two councils between them and neither of them gains by either of those two numbers, and one of them would be pleased if one of them were different, and I am telling you that it is not and it is not going to be, and I would like the four people who have been asking me about that wall to stop."**

A mason from the fourth place read three figures off the wall at about the tenth hour and did it in the way a man reads three courses, with his thumb on the stone under each one.

**"Five hundred and thirty-ninth is under my hand at this moment and that one is the one that was set that morning and it has not moved and it is not going to move today.** He took his thumb off the stone. **"A hundred and eleven stones in the course under that number and I have laid every one of them and the twelfth of them out of line and I have never taken it out because it is not a fault, it is a stone.** He stepped back from the wall. **"Five hundred and forty is above the rule and five hundred and forty-first is at the head, and I am not going to tell you which of those three is the important one because I have laid three hundred yards of wall in my life and not one of them has ever had one important number on it."**

The crew man with his own book came down off the gate end about the tenth hour and gave two figures from the top of the wall where he had been laying a course since the eighth, in the same breath as the course, because he said a fortnight ago that he would rather have those two said in the same mouth as the work.

**"There are two figures on that wall and I have never had to think about either of them: the near one stands at nine hundred and twenty-six and the top one at nine hundred and seventy-three, and there is forty-seven of masonry between the two of them as there has been since I was a boy, and I laid four courses today and I am going to lay four tomorrow and I am not going to tell this yard how many courses I lay.** He came down two steps. **"And I said those two out loud because I laid the eleventh course at the near end myself eight mornings ago and I have not got over it, and because a figure on a wall ought to be said by somebody with his hand on the wall and not by somebody who keeps the book.** He went and picked his hod up.

Tova Reed came out from the store at about the eleventh hour with the tray going and gave her four from the step without the board and without stopping what she was doing, and two of the four were said while she was handing somebody a paper sack.

**"Four hundred and thirty-six is the form, three hundred and forty-two is the charter and two hundred and thirty-nine is the letter, and I am saying them in that order because I have got two of them crossed in my head this morning and I am not going to cross them twice.** She pushed a sack across the step. **"Four hundred and thirty under Silling's second one, and it is blank, and it will be blank at four hundred and thirty-one tomorrow, and I am not going to say anything else about that line on this morning.** She wiped her hands. **"Forty mornings running I have said that line was blank in four different yards, and the four of you who have heard me do it can now do it without me, and I am going to keep saying it anyway because the day I stop saying it is the day somebody puts something on it.** She looked down the lane. **"And two people went up that lane at six and two people came down at eleven and I know which two because one of them has a limp and everybody knows about that limp, and I am not going to say a word about it, and nobody in this yard is going to hear a word about it from me."**

Nobody thanked anybody on that Thursday.

A man of about seventy was not fetched, and nothing was asked of him at any hour of it, and the number of questions this holding has put to him in four years is still not one. The barrow went its eleven journeys and the man who pushes it set the shafts down where he sets them down.

Four terms and five short empty lines under them and nothing at the head of the paper and nothing at the foot of it, and it has been lying out in the weather past that gate since before the flood, and nobody in this yard has gone out and picked it up.

And at about the eleventh hour and a half there were about nine people standing at the boards reading a figure that was up eight, and not one of them said the word highest, and about nine other people were in the yard doing what they did, and the compost line is paid at thirty-one and has not been discharged in thirty-one or in anything, and the two people who had gone up the lane at six were at the store door at about a quarter past twelve with wet coats on and one of them had a limp that everybody in this yard knows about, and neither of them had said a word about the head of that lane to anybody, and the two of them were going to be at the gate again at six the next morning, and neither of them had said one word about the head of that lane, and there was nothing on the board about it and there was never going to be.
