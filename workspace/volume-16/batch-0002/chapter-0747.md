# Chapter 747

## The Thirtieth Of The Twentieth, And A Food Route Put Up Beside A Seed Route With One Place On It That Has No Water

It was a Sunday and the north door of the seed house was open before the lane was dry, and Tova Reed had the corner of the board out on the step with the seed route already on it in her own hand, and she was waiting for somebody to ask her for the rest of it.

Nobody asked her for the rest of it before about the seventh hour, so at the seventh hour she put the food side up herself.

She worked from the step outward with her back to the north door and her face to the yard, which is where she has worked since the frost week and which she has never explained to anybody, and a mason dictating from the far end of the boards had to walk round and stand in front of her to be heard at all, and she wrote down what he said the first time and did not ask him a second question about any of it.

The board went up in her own hand and this is what was on it:

> **THE SEED ROUTE AND THE FOOD ROUTE, TOGETHER, IN ONE HAND, UNDATED.**
>
> Seed house to the third place. Trays out on the days the trays are ready and not on a day anybody has named.
>
> Seed house to the fourth place. Same trays, same days, and Perrin Dae's cart goes when his cart goes.
>
> Third place to the four houses at the end of the second branch. Bread and seed in the same load, because a house that gets seed in the middle of the week and nothing to eat with it does not keep the seed dry.
>
> Seed house to the second place. It is on the route. There is no water at it. It goes on the route every week that the route goes, and it is carried to the door and it is not carried in.
>
> Fourth place to the sluice road. The fifth hour suits that road and not the rest of us and I have written it at the fifth hour and not at mine.
>
> Nothing on this board is put off. Nothing on this board is divided. There is no line through any place on it and there is no second column, and I have not cut one, and if anybody in this holding wants a second column under this I will take the board inside.

A woman stood at the north end of the boards for most of that hour who had not been inside this gate since the spring, and she had come up the lane on foot with nothing in her hands, and she was the woman whose house stands next door to the second place, and about nine people in this yard knew exactly whose house that was and not one of them said so out loud.

Nobody went over to her. Nobody asked her how she was and nobody asked her how she felt about the board and nobody told her that anybody was sorry about anything, and she stood about four feet off the corner of it and read all of it, and when she had read the line about the second place she put her hand flat against the wall behind her and left it there and did not say anything at all.

At about the eighth hour a man from the fourth place looked at that line and said that a route with a stop on it that nobody could water was half a route and not worth the carting.

**"It is not half a route,"** Tova Reed said, without turning round. **"It is the route with that place on it. It has been on my route since the flood and it is going to be on it on the morning after this one and it is not going to come off it because I cannot fill a can at the end of it. You may write the word later on it if you want it there. You may not write it as though the place is not on the board."**

**"I was not saying to take it off."**

**"You said half. Half is off with one end showing."**

And she said that once and went back to the trays, and the man did not raise it again, and the woman by the wall stayed where she was for another half hour and then walked out of the gate and down the lane on her own and nobody watched her go but about four people who pretended not to.

Tova Reed gave the other four at about the ninth hour to the mason from the third place, who had to come round the trays and stand square in front of her to be heard at all, and she said them once and did not write any of them on the corner.

**"Four hundred and four on the form. Three hundred and ten on the charter, which is the oldest of the four and which nobody has ever read. Three hundred and ninety-eight on the ruled line under Silling's first one, and it is blank, and I am telling you that it is blank because there is a woman standing four feet from me who has read a line on my board and I would rather she heard it from me than worked it out."**

**"Three hundred and ninety-eight."**

**"And two hundred and seven on the letter, which is the last of them, and it goes to two hundred and eight in the morning."**

Perrin Dae read the two he has the most use for off the boards with his finger on the frame and got them both at once, because he has never in his life read one without the other.

**"One thousand and three hundred and seventy-nine hundredweight came down in the night, and the run is on the five hundred and tenth. That is eight up on that and it has been eight up on every even morning since the spring. I have turned timber at my own cost twice this season off the back of a board and I am not going to say the two things out loud one after the other again."**

**"You are going to say them out loud."**

**"I am going to say them out loud and I am going to be quiet afterwards."**

Harlan Vetch came down far enough off the wall to be heard without going all the way to the ground, which he does perhaps twice in a season, and gave the one figure and would not give the halves.

**"Four hundred and seventy-seven of window. That is all I have got for you and I am not splitting it, because every time I split it four men ask me which half is which and then two of them tell the other one he has it backwards."**

**"Then I will tell you which half came,"** the boy who carries water said, from the sill with a can in each hand, **"and I will not be asked which half is which. Two hundred and forty-four came. Two hundred and thirty-three stood."**

Auret Sill came up the lane from the pumps with her slate under her arm and the near figure already on it, and gave it with a complaint attached, which is how she gives everything.

**"Eight hundred and ninety-four at the near board and nine hundred and forty-one at the top, and I will tell you what I have been shouting up the wall about for a week. There is a foot of silt under the near one on the channel side and a hand width of it has come off the face of the stone. I did not take it off. I have not had a barrow that was allowed out and I am not going to be the one who took it off."**

**"A foot of silt."**

**"And I have not measured it and I have not written it in anything, and I am telling you three people and not four, and if you write it down anywhere then it will have been measured."**

Soren Rill came at about the ninth hour and gave the long sheet without stopping on it and said, before he had finished, that he was not going to do this at this hour any more on a Sunday, and he went on doing it.

**"Five hundred and seventh underneath, five hundred and eight on the head of it, five hundred and ninth over the line. And that is my hand and my sheet and I have read three numbers at nine o'clock on a Sunday for twenty years and this morning is the last Sunday I am going to do it on until somebody asks me to."**

**"Then nobody asked you to."**

**"No. That is what I said and I am not withdrawing it."**

After the fourth hour nobody put a hand on the barrow. It stands in the middle of the yard wheel first with eleven journeys in the tray, and there is no twelfth in it for any reason including a good one.

The figure in front of paid on the board at the back of the tap house has not moved off thirty-one in a single morning of my working life, and the column beside it for what has been let out has been empty since before the spring.

Inside the one room there are two books and they have never been one book: fifty-three requests, fifty-three notes under the section-nine heading, no figure ever drawn from one and set against the other, and the two lists have never once matched. Above the long table on the wall are thirty-nine blanks, and the rule under them standing open and empty at the thirty-ninth time, and nobody walked in and looked at either of them on that Sunday.

The drawer was shut with its key on its nail at the sixth hour and again at the dark and at every hour in between. Nobody in that yard went near the bottom of the ladder at the tool house wall.

And the offer lay on the low board out past the gate where it has lain since before the flood, and nobody picked it up, and nobody dated it, and nobody filled in the space at the foot of it, and it did not go back to whoever left it there, and about four people in that yard knew that the woman who had come up the lane on foot had walked down it again without anyone at this gate having said one word to her about it.
