# Chapter 751

## The Fourth Of The Twenty-First, And A Woman Coming Back From Four Tanks With A Count In Her Own Book That She Did Not Read Out To Anybody

Nia Vale had gone out before the light and came back up the lane at about the eighth hour with her own book in her coat and mud to the knee and four places behind her instead of three, which is how anybody in this yard knew she had found a fourth one that would move.

She did not go to the boards. She went to the pumps, then to the tap house, then to the wall end of the wall, and she stood with Auret Sill and the crew man and gave them what she had in words and in no figures at all, and she gave it to them separately rather than all at once, and she wrote it down after each one while the person she had told was still standing there.

> **WHAT I SAW AT FOUR PLACES. MY OWN COUNTING, IN MY OWN HAND, AND IT IS NOT IN ANYTHING OF YOURS AND I AM NOT ENTERING IT.**
>
> The tank at the second place on the cart. It moves. It has been standing on that cart since the flood and it can go to any one of four houses at the end of the branch and it will go to whichever one asks first.
>
> The butt behind the third place. It moves and it is nearly out and it would take a fortnight of nothing before it would take anything.
>
> The pool at the wall end. It does not move and it is not going to and I have looked at the ground it sits on twice and I would not put a tank on it.
>
> The one I did not know about, at the top of the fourth road, under a lean-to with nobody living in it. It moves. It has been sitting under that lean-to with a full top and a sound bottom since before I was born and there is no reason in the world it has not gone to the second place every week since the flood, and it has not, and I am not going to say why and neither is going to be anybody else in this yard.
>
> So that is four that move and two that do not, and it is six places and not a figure, and it is mine, and the day it stops being mine is the day somebody else goes and looks.

The crew man asked her the standing question about the return while she was still writing.

**"Then what is a return for."**

**"It is for nothing that anybody in this yard can use,"** she said, without stopping the pencil. **"It moves no figure. It has not moved one in four years and it is not going to move one for me this morning, and there is no number on it and there is not going to be one. I have been asked that by a man at my gate, by a man who walked two hours up a road, and now by you, and I am going to give you the answer I have given the other two and it is the same one. What I think it is for is a thing about me and not about it."**

**"Then you have never known."**

**"I have never known it on a morning when knowing it would have helped anybody."**

Auret Sill had her hand on Nia Vale's arm at some point in that and took it off again without any appearance of having put it there, and nobody in this yard has ever said that it happened and it is not a thing anybody is going to say.

**"The one at the top of the fourth road,"** Auret Sill said. **"Two houses at that end have not had a full butt since the spring."**

**"They know about that tank. I am not going to be the one who tells two households in a yard of thirty people what is under a lean-to they walk past every week."**

**"You have just told me it is full."**

**"I have told you that it is full because you are the person who has to open a pump at six in the morning and you are entitled to know what a person can bring you,"** Nia Vale said. **"That is me telling the person who would carry it. That is not me standing in a yard telling thirty people, and if those two things get confused today then the lean-to is empty by Thursday and I will have done it."**

The barrow had gone eleven times by the ninth hour and the man who pushed it came in from the fourth road and put it down wheel first and would not take it out again, and when a man from the fourth place told him there was a fourth tank under a lean-to that would fill the barrow twice he told him that eleven is eleven and that a number does not become a different number because a woman has been out all morning.

**"You could go twice more and be back before the dark."**

**"I could,"** the man said, **"and I am not going to, and you may write that down as a man who will not be pushed, which is what it is, and which is not the same as a man who does not care."**

The boy who carries water gave the two off the sill with his hands on the cans and gave them the wrong way round first on purpose, because two men had been arguing about which of them mattered and he wanted to see whether they would catch him.

**"Five hundred and fourteenth morning of the run. One thousand and three hundred and eighty-five hundredweight came down in the night, up eight, because it is an even morning."**

**"You had it wrong round once already this week."**

**"I did, and you caught me, and now you know that you caught me,"** he said, **"which is more use to this yard than the figure was."**

Perrin Dae read the window off the ledge at about the ninth hour and could not tell anybody which half of it had come, and said so twice before anybody gave up asking him.

**"Four hundred and eighty-one of window, and I can read it as well as the next one and I will tell you honestly that I do not know which half came last night and neither does my son, and I have been reading figures off that ledge for nine years and I have never once known which half came."**

**"Nobody does."**

**"Then it wants saying that a man can read a figure and not know the thing about it, and I would have told you I knew if I had known."**

**"Two hundred and forty-six came and two hundred and thirty-five stood,"** Nia Vale said from the rack, and said it without any remark on the fact that a man had just stood in front of nine people and admitted in front of them that he did not know. **"I have watched him not know it every morning for nine years and I have never once offered, because the morning I offer it is the morning he stops admitting it. I am offering it now because four of them have not moved and six of them have, and a man who reads the tall number sends his cart to the wrong house."**

Ismay Rooke walked the length of the yard to read the near stone instead of reading it from the middle of it, which put him at the wrong end of forty-seven of masonry with the wall behind him, and he came back and gave both and gave the far one from memory and said which one he had walked to.

**"Eight hundred and ninety-eight at the near end. Nine hundred and forty-five at the top, and I did not walk up to that one, I am telling you where I got it from."**

**"You always read them the other way up."**

**"I read them the other way up because I read them from this end and from this end the near one is on the left, and I have been doing it for nine years and I am not starting again this morning."**

Soren Rill gave Odile Vray the long sheet at about the tenth hour and stood back and let her read it, which he has not done in twenty years, and she read it out to the yard in the ordinary voice she uses for a slate.

**"Five hundred and eleventh under the line, five hundred and twelve at the head, five hundred and thirteenth over."**

**"That is right."**

**"I have never read it before and I want it said that I do not know which end of it is the top."**

**"You have read it correctly and I am not going to explain it to you in a yard."**

Kellan Rusk came out to the step with the register form under his arm and gave his figure before anybody had asked him for it and then said something he has not said before.

**"Four hundred and eight on the form. And I will tell you the rest of it in my own time and not because anybody asked, because I have been sitting in there all morning with the drawer shut and the key on its nail and I have worked out that this is the only thing in this holding that I have ever been the only person who could say, and that is not the same as the only thing anybody wants to hear."**

He went back inside and shut the door, and the drawer was shut at every hour of that day including the dark.

Tova Reed gave her three to a man from the second place who had his back to her for about a minute and a half while she sorted trays, and she waited until he turned round and did not say anything at all while she waited, and she wrote them on the corner as she gave them.

**"Two hundred and eleven on the letter. Three hundred and fourteen on the charter, and that one is the oldest. And four hundred and two on the second of Silling's two ruled lines, which is blank, as it has been since she ruled it."**

**"Four hundred and two."**

**"It went past four hundred yesterday morning and it is still blank and that is all I am going to say about a number I carry for somebody else."**

On the wall inside the one room the blanks stand at thirty-nine and have stood at thirty-nine for four years, and the second line beneath them stands open and empty at the thirty-ninth time, and nobody has ever put anything under it. Seven sessions have been entered in this holding's book and no eighth has ever been entered. Two books sit on that shelf with a heading over each of them and no heading over the two of them together.

The drawer behind the door was shut at every hour of that Thursday and the key went back on its nail at the dark.

And at about the eleventh hour Nia Vale put her own book inside her coat and went out to the pumps again with the fourth place's man and did not come back into the yard, and about four people who had wanted to ask her a question about the lean-to did not ask it, and the tank under the lean-to at the top of the fourth road was still full on that morning and was still there the morning after that, and nobody in this holding has ever said out loud whether it moved.
