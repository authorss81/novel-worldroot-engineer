# Chapter 748

## The First Of The Twenty-First, And A Date Changed On A Slate At The Gate Before Anybody Had Done Anything

The date on the slate at the gate was wrong from the middle of the night until about the fifth hour, and that is the whole of what happened between one morning and this one.

The boy who carries water saw it first at about the fifth hour and said so to nobody, and then said so to the man who was filling the second can, and the man looked at it and said that it was a number and a number was a number, and the boy said that he had been told a date was not a figure and he was going to leave it alone.

Then somebody else came and looked, and by about the sixth hour there were about eleven people standing at the gate reading a slate.

Marek Vale came up the lane at the sixth hour and looked at it and did not touch it and came on into the yard.

**"It turned in the night. That is all it did."**

**"It is the first of something."**

**"It is the first of a different column on a slate by a gate that a boy fills in at the fifth hour, and it has not done one thing to the water in the channel or to the ring or to the five lines on that table, and I want it said in this yard this morning that nothing about the work has started again."**

**"Then what is a month for."**

**"It is for telling a man how long he has been at it,"** he said. **"It is not for telling him he has not been at it."**

A woman from the four houses at the end of the second branch was at the boards at about the seventh hour with a basket on her hip and said the thing that most of that yard was thinking and did it without any heat in it.

**"My bread does not change because a slate changed. I am not carrying less than I carried on Saturday and I am not going to go and tell my sister the slate says something new. So whatever it is that was supposed to happen this morning, it is going to have to happen without me noticing."**

**"Then it is not going to happen,"** a man said.

**"It is going to happen without me noticing,"** she said, **"which is not the same and you will find out which it is in about three weeks."**

The crew man had his book open on the sill and did not read out of it until he had the yard's back, which is what he does, and then he read his own page out at the boards in his own voice and would not hand it to anybody.

> **THE RING. NINE BEDS. WHAT EACH OF THEM DID. IN THE CREW'S OWN BOOK AND IN THE CREW'S OWN ORDER, AND THE TWO COLUMNS ARE TWO COUNTS OF TWO DIFFERENT THINGS.**
>
> One. Held the dry-season draw clean from the top to the bottom. Held the flood load. No mark on the face.
>
> Two. Held the draw. Held the load. Face scoured below the second stone since the flood and sound.
>
> Three. Held the draw. Held the load. Fine, and the best of them, and I am not going to be told which one I mean, because I have not written down which one I mean.
>
> Four. Held the draw clean. Would not take the load above what the face will carry, and there is a scour at the inside of the bend that will want watching in a hard season.
>
> Five. Held the draw. Would not hold the load. It let the load sit on the top of it until the top of it went soft, and that is the whole of what it did.
>
> Six. Did not hold the draw. Held the load, and held it well.
>
> Seven. Did not hold the draw. Held the load. It took the draw at the bottom end and let it go at the top end, and that is the whole of what it did.
>
> Eight. Soft at the north end. Took the draw clean at the top and let it go out at the far end of itself without being asked. Not a fault in the bed and not a fault in the man.
>
> Nine. Gone. Came up with the root on it on the morning it came up and it is not there again this season and we are not going to try to put that root back where it was.
>
> Six held the draw and five held the load. Those are two counts and I am not adding them and neither is anybody else, because a bed can hold one and not the other and that is the ordinary case.

He read the last line once and shut the book and stood with his thumb in it.

**"Nine beds. I have never said nine in a yard and I have just said it, and it is a count of holes in the ground and not a score and not a report on anybody, and I would like it left at that."**

A man from the fourth place did the sum out loud anyway, twice, both times correctly, and put his hand over his mouth the second time.

**"You have done it twice and got the same answer twice,"** the crew man said, **"and I am not going to argue with a man about arithmetic. I am going to tell you what it is for, so you stop. It is for me knowing which beds to put a load on in a hard season and which one to leave off, and I know, and it is in the book, and that is the end of the use of it."**

The man who lays for three councils came down the middle road with his hod and no chart and put the hod down by the sill and gave his two without being asked and went and picked the hod up again.

**"Five hundred and eleventh morning of the run. One thousand and three hundred and seventy-four hundredweight came down the channel in the night, down five, because it is an odd morning. I have said eight up three times this week and down five three times and I am going to say the pair and no reason for the pair and then I am going to lay."**

Odile Vray gave the two stones from memory with her back to the board, which she has never done on a morning when anybody was in the yard to check her.

**"Eight hundred and ninety-five at the near end and nine hundred and forty-two at the top. I did not look. I have had them in the same place I have the window and I have had them there since the wall went up, and if either of those two is out by a hundredweight this morning then I have carried it in my head since Saturday and that is a thing about me and not about the wall."**

The boy who carries water gave the window off the ledge as he went past with his two cans full and a third empty, and he gave it without stopping and in one breath, which is a new thing he has started doing.

**"Four hundred and seventy-eight. Two hundred and thirty-four came and two hundred and forty-four stood."**

**"You have been saying that the same two have come every other morning since I started,"** Nia Vale said. **"It is not every other morning. I have it on a slate four days a week and it does what it likes. The only thing I know about that pair is which of them moved, and I have never once known why, and I am the person who is supposed to know."**

At the seventh hour the girl from the second place wrote the pair on the north board under a frame that is a month nine months back and has been that frame for four years, two hundred and two over the rule and four hundred and thirty-five under it, and the frame did not move in the night and did not move when the date on the slate at the gate changed, and nobody read the two figures out and nobody asked when the reading at that trough would begin again.

Soren Rill came at the eighth hour and asked a man at the boards for a slate, and got one, and read the long sheet off the slate instead of off the wall, and handed the slate back.

**"Five hundred and eighth under the line, five hundred and nine at the head, five hundred and tenth over. And I read that off somebody else's slate because I did not bring mine, and I am not going to be told it is bad for the figures."**

**"It is bad for the figures."**

**"Then it is bad for the figures once a week and it is good for the two of us not having to stand shoulder to shoulder at the same board every morning of our lives."**

Tova Reed gave her four at three different moments of the morning to three different people and wrote each of them on the corner of the board as she said it, and she did not write them in the same order twice and she did not look up from the trays for any of them.

**"Three hundred and eleven on the charter."**

Then at about the ninth hour, to a man at the north door.

**"Two hundred and eight on the letter, and two hundred and nine will be tomorrow and two hundred and ten the day after, and if anybody in this holding thinks the letter stops at the end of a month then they have not been watching it."**

Then at about the tenth hour, to nobody in particular, standing with the corner of the board against her hip.

**"Four hundred and five on the form. And three hundred and ninety-nine on the ruled line under Silling's first one, and it is blank, and it is going to be blank at four hundred tomorrow morning and I would like it said in this yard that nobody is to help me by putting something on it."**

Nobody in this yard answered that, and about four people heard it, and one of them was the mason from the third place who was standing directly in front of her and had therefore heard all three of the four figures and had not been asked to remember any of them.

Kellan Rusk shut the drawer at the dark and the key went back on its nail, and it had been shut at every hour of that day as well as at the dark. There are seven sessions entered in this holding's book and there has been no eighth since the spring.

A man asked at the boards at about the ninth hour whether the compost line would ever turn, and Sera Quill told him that it was not hers and that she has never once known what would turn it. What is paid on it is thirty-one and the column that would take what has come out of it has been empty for four years.

At the back of the tap house a joint that has wept into a saucer every morning since before the flood wept once, and the saucer kept it. Nobody went near the ladder at the tool house wall, and the ground inside the ring behind it took no boot that morning.

Nobody fetched the man of about seventy and nobody put one question to him between the sixth hour and the dark, and there has never been a question put to him in the four years this holding has kept any count of it.

And the offer lay out past the gate on the low board with no date on it and nothing in the space at the foot of it, and the slate at the gate had a new day on it and the paper out past the gate had not changed at all, and about four people in that yard found that harder to explain to somebody else than they had expected to.
