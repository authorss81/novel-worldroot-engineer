# Chapter 718

## The First Of The Twentieth, And A Chalk Held In The Wrong Hand At The North Board

It was a Saturday and there were more people in the yard than there had been all week, and about half of them were standing about rather than doing anything, which Odile Vray said was the correct number for a Saturday and which she said out loud so that it would be on the record that she had said it.

The wind had gone round to the south in the night and come back north by the fourth hour, and the light on the wall was the bad kind, the kind that makes a stone look sound and a man look tired.

The boy who carries water came through with his two cans at about the seventh hour and stopped at the north board the way he does, and the chalk he picked up was in his left hand because his right one had gone cracked across the second joint in the frost week and he had said nothing about it to anybody and it was visible to everybody.

He wrote the pair on the board in the ordinary place, above the two lines that have been ruled under it for longer than he has been coming here.

**"Two hundred and eleven of four hundred and five."**

Then he put the chalk down and did not read it, and when a man at the boards asked him to read it out he gave the reason once and it was not long.

**"I am paid to carry it up and I am not paid to say it. Nobody has ever asked me to say it and I do not start on a morning when I have to write with the other hand."**

The heading above the pair was the heading that has always been above the pair, which is of a month nine months back, and the boy did not touch it and did not look at it, and a man who has been in this yard a long time looked at it, and then looked at it again, and then said the thing that half the yard was thinking.

**"That is up a month. The month turned in the night and that heading has not, and either it is wrong now or it was wrong last night, and one of those two is going to get found out by somebody with a reason."**

The girl from the second place was on the stones at the gate end when he said it, and she came down off them to look at the board, and she read the heading and then she read the pair underneath it, and then she took the chalk out of the boy's hand and wrote the heading out again exactly as it stood, in the same letters, and gave it back.

**"It is right,"** she said. **"It has said that since before I came up this road and it said it last night the same as it said it the night before. I have written it out three times now for people who did not want to look up."**

**"Then somebody wants a month off it."**

**"Somebody can want it. It is not going to get one."**

Nobody read the pair out. Nobody has read it out in this yard for a long time, and the boy was not thanked for writing it and the girl was not thanked for writing the heading out again, and the two of them went back to what they were doing without any further words about it.

The man who buys came up at about the ninth hour with a load of lime in a cart he was not going to unload until somebody had told him he was in the right place, and he got out of the cart and looked at the boards the way he always does, and he found what he was looking for, and then he found something he was not looking for.

**"I have got that form at my place,"** he said. **"Not one like it. That form. I have had it in a drawer at my place since the spring, and the count on mine runs two hundred and twelve and not three hundred and seventy-five, and I have never once compared it with anybody."**

Kellan Rusk came out and stood beside him and looked at the thing in the cart and not at the man.

**"It is the register form. Three hundred and seventy-five days. And it is in the rack in there and it has been in that rack longer than your cart has existed."**

**"That is not what I asked."**

**"No, it is not. The count on mine goes with the rack. If yours goes with your drawer then there are two of the form and one of us has a copy, and I have never seen your drawer and I cannot tell you which of us is right, and I am not going to guess at a thing with two answers in front of a man who has to pay for lime either."**

The man who buys put both hands on the side of the cart.

**"So it is no good to you."**

**"It is no good to either of us this morning and it was no good to you at the fourth hour as well. It is still a true figure about something. I have not said it is not true."**

**"Then I am unloading here,"** the man said. **"Unload here, I have got another place that will take it this week, and I will come back for the rest when somebody can tell me what the form is for."**

Odile Vray came off the boards and told him to put it down anywhere on the hard standing and not on the sill and not on the path, and he did that, and he did it in about nine minutes, and then he stood in the yard with his hands empty while eleven people watched a cart of lime that was going to be carried back out of the gate the same evening by somebody else.

Nobody in that yard told him he had done the wrong thing, and the figure in the rack was still three hundred and seventy-five when the cart had gone.

He stood in the middle of the hard standing with his hands hanging and looked up at the wall, because a man who buys anything in this valley looks at a wall before he looks at a person.

**"Two numbers on your stones,"** he said to Harlan Vetch. **"Nine hundred and twelve at the top and eight hundred and sixty-five at the gate, and I would call that a delivery and I would call the forty-seven of masonry a waste of the good stuff at the bottom."**

**"Forty-seven is the whole of what I have got,"** Harlan Vetch said. **"It is the same forty-seven every morning since the wall went up and it will be the same on the morning this comes down, and I have never once found a reason to make it anything else."**

He went up the ladder to the top course and came back down with a stone dust on him and gave the rest of it from up there, because a man who is on a wall talks down.

**"The channel is carrying one thousand and three hundred and twenty-nine hundredweight, which is five down on last night, and the run calls that one the four hundred and eighty-first."**

He moved a hand onto the same stone without changing his voice.

**"The window on this stone is four hundred and forty-eight. The two halves are two hundred and nineteen underneath and two hundred and twenty-nine over the top of it, and I have given you the low one first for once, and that is not an apology, it is the order the stone happens to be in."**

**"So the underneath went last night."**

**"The underneath went last night. It has gone on every one of the odd nights this month and it will go again before Sunday, and the half over the top will stand there and count what it likes."**

He looked down at the man with the empty cart.

**"You have been counting a delivery since you were fifteen. You counted that stone once, when it was made, and what I say up here counts it every morning it is still standing. Those are two numbers about the same lump of rock and one of us is going to sound like a fool to a man from an office."**

The man who buys said that the office had never once called him a fool to his face, and got a short laugh out of four people who were not listening.

Soren Rill came down off the boards at about the middle of the afternoon and gave the aggregate from the gate end and gave it last, as he has given it all week, except that today he gave it with a barrow going past in the middle of it and did not stop for the barrow.

**"Four hundred and seventy-nine at the head of that long sheet, the four hundred and seventy-eighth under the line, and the four hundred and eightieth over it. There is a fourth place on the same sheet that has not been filled since the spring."**

He set the barrow down on the other side when it had gone.

**"And I will say the thing I have said every day this week and have never once said better: the one at the head of it and the two under and over it are three separate things and anybody who adds them has built a number that is not on anybody's paper."**

Then a woman asked Kellan Rusk whether the sheet on the middle table was one of the four on the long table, and he came out and stood in the doorway and answered that and then answered three more things nobody had asked him about, which is what happens at that door on a Saturday.

**"The four on the long table are four sheets and not one of the four has been entered and not one has been refused and no column has been cut under any of them. The paper on the middle table is a fifth thing and it is not a fifth sheet."**

She looked at the door and then at the paper on the middle table, and he read the look and answered the other question before it was asked.

**"The charter is two hundred and eighty-one days,"** he said, before she had asked for it. **"Silling's book has a second line ruled under the first one and it is at three hundred and sixty-nine days, and it is the blankest object in this holding, and a man put a date on it once last week and I watched him not do it. And the letter is a hundred and seventy-eight days and has been dealt with by nobody since the spring."**

He shut the door behind him and stood on the step with his hand on the latch.

A woman came in about the middle of the morning with a pencil behind her ear and asked Odile Vray for a sheet, because she had heard there was a thing on the middle table with four written on it and no date at the top of it, and she said that a paper without a date is a paper nobody can find again.

Then she stopped with her hand on the middle table with the pencil still behind her ear, because the wind came round the corner of the one room and lifted the near corner of that sheet, and there was nothing under it to hold it, and Odile Vray was a hundred feet off at the boards.

**"Give me the pencil,"** the woman said, to the man nearest her, and then stopped again.

Kellan Rusk came out of the doorway and looked at the sheet and then at the pencil.

**"Put the date on it and it becomes a record,"** he said. **"It has four things in four hands on it and five lines at the foot of it and no name at the top of it, and the whole of what it is doing depends on that. A record gets an entry. An entry goes in the rack in there and then it is one of a number and it is not any more the thing four people wrote with their hands in an afternoon."**

**"And in four months nobody will know when it was written."**

**"Then in four months it will be four terms with no date on them and a woman who remembers the afternoon. That is a worse record and it is the one they wanted."**

She put the pencil back behind her ear and left the sheet where it was, and about ten minutes later the wind lifted it again and she stood and held the corner herself until it went down.

Then the man who lays for three councils came and stood in front of the sheet for a while with his thumb on the third of the four, and he answered a question nobody had put to him, which was whether anything about the four started this morning because the month had turned.

**"No,"** he said. **"It started when the four of us wrote it, and it is not going to be any more true or any less true on the first of a month than it was on the day before. A calendar does not lay a course. And I will tell you the one thing I am sure of about that paper and it is not a calendar thing at all: that paper does not do anything at all until somebody builds something out of it, and what gets built is what makes it true."**

He took his thumb off the third term, picked up a hod off the stack, and went down the wall toward the north end, where four courses had been finished and done since before the light.

The sheet stayed where it was, undated, with a woman holding one corner of it and a man six feet away laying stone that none of the four on that paper had asked him to lay.
