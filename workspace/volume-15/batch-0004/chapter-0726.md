# Chapter 726

## The Ninth Of The Twentieth, And A Boot Scraped In The Mud Where The Lane Turns At The Fourth Place

Two people came up the lane on foot on Sunday morning and got here at about the seventh hour with wet to the knee, and neither of them had been sent for and neither of them had asked anybody here for anything, and one of them had a sheet folded inside her coat and the other one had nothing at all.

The one with the sheet put it on the boards herself and turned it round so the yard could read it, and then she said what her household had decided and she said it in her own mouth and not in anybody else's, and the other one stood beside her and said nothing at all from beginning to end, which about four people in that yard noticed and did not comment on.

> From the lane above the fourth place, and this paper belongs to whoever is carrying it and not to this holding:
>
> **We are not standing on the middle road.**
>
> The route we will hold is this one. It comes up off the dry branch past the second bend, it goes along the high side of the third place's road above the wall, and it stops at the head of the lane and does not come down past a gate.
>
> **There is no gate on it. There is no holding on it. There is nobody standing on it that has not asked to be standing on it.**
>
> We are not asking this yard for one thing. We are not asking for a load, and we are not asking for a bridge, and we are not asking for a figure to be changed, and if every figure in your yard is altered tomorrow the route is the same route.
>
> This paper is ours and it is not a record of yours, and it is not to be entered anywhere.
>
> Two hands are on it.

Not one person in that yard said thank you, and not one of them said they were glad.

The two of them stayed. That was the thing that about nine people got wrong and that the two of them corrected about eleven, separately and in the same words, which the girl from the second place heard from both and said out loud twice.

**"We have come four miles and we are going to eat at your boards and we are not going up that lane again this month unless one of us is asked, and nobody has asked us, and the asking belongs to nobody in this yard."**

They sat down at the middle table at about the ninth hour with bread and a pot of something and they stayed about an hour, and the woman with the sheet did the talking for most of it and the other one ate and looked at the wall the whole time.

A man came at about the tenth hour and asked three things at once, which was whether anybody had asked up the lane what they would take, whether anybody had told them what they would get, and whether the engineer of record had put a figure in front of them. Odile Vray answered the first two and the mason from the third place, who was in the yard on his own business and was not asked, answered the third.

**"Nobody has asked them anything and that is not our business. And nobody has put a figure in front of anybody, and if he had I would have heard about it before he got to the boards."**

Marek Vale had the sheet with the line drawn on it in his own coat, the one that came down the middle road and past the gate end, and he had it out in his hand about four seconds after the woman had finished, and then he put it back in his coat and did not say a word about it.

A man at the gate said that the road the engineer of record had was the shorter one and the only one with stone on it, and he had got about nine words of that out before Soren Rill stopped him without any particular heat in it at all.

**"You have been in this yard forty minutes and you have not been up that lane in your life. That is not a reason against your sentence. It is a reason against you finishing it."**

Then Soren Rill took the paper out of Marek Vale's coat in front of everybody and held it up and did not read it out, and then he put it back in the coat and said the one thing he had come down for.

**"That is what I would have put in front of them and it is not the one they have named, and I told them neither on Saturday nor on any morning before it, and not one person in this yard is going to be asked to say that out loud, because it would be worth nothing."**

He said the other thing about his own two silences at about the fourth hour, standing at the boards with his back to the ladder, and he announced it rather than defending it.

**"There are two things I will not say up that lane about anybody in this holding, and I am telling this yard now so that it is on somebody's morning. One is what those people want. The other is what they would do if somebody offered them a thing. There will be no questions from me on either of those and no quotations of me on either, and if either gets said in this yard this morning it will have been said by a person who has never been up that lane."**

A man from the north row with the cough then said what the route had done to a figure, and he said it out loud in the middle of the yard, and he believed it.

**"Nine hundred and twenty at the top of that wall. And they have just told us the far end is not where they are going to stand, so that number is finished, and it will be a different number this afternoon, and I say that as a man who counts things and not as a man who has an opinion."**

Nia Vale told him it was not, and told him why in as many words as it takes.

**"Eight hundred and seventy-three at the gate and nine hundred and twenty at the top, and those are two stones and forty-seven of masonry and neither of them has moved since the wall went up. Eleven households up a lane have said where they will stand and it has not taken one stone out of this wall and it is not going to put one in. A figure does not go and come because somebody said a thing in a yard. If it did, half of them would be different every time a woman was cross with a man."**

Then Nia Vale went into the one room and stayed in there for an hour, and Kellan Rusk came out and closed the door behind her, and would not say what was happening in there and did not need to.

The man who buys asked for the charter and got it from the boards and read it out as if it settled something, and Odile Vray stopped him.

**"Two hundred and eighty-nine days. That is how old a paper is. It is not a route and it does not have a road on it and it is not going to tell anybody where to stand."**

**"Then what is it for."**

**"It is for writing on, and nothing has been written on it since the spring, and this yard should stop finding out what a figure is for by using it to close an argument."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the wall at about the sixth hour and gave the four in the order he gave them on the first day of that week, which he had not done all week, and nobody thought to ask him why.

**"The stone at the top of this wall is at nine hundred and twenty and the stone at the gate end is at eight hundred and seventy-three, and there are forty-seven of masonry between them, and I have been saying those numbers to this yard every morning this month and I have said them at nine in the morning in the rain and at six in the evening in the dark and I have never once said them at a time anybody asked me for."**

He came down two rungs and stopped.

**"The channel is down five again at one thousand and three hundred and forty-one hundredweight of it. That is the four hundred and eighty-ninth of the run, and a man who does not carry a hod is not going to get that number out of me."**

He wiped his hands and gave the last of it to the stones.

**"Four hundred and fifty-six of window. Two hundred and twenty-three underneath and that is the half that went, and two hundred and thirty-three over the top standing. It is the sixth odd morning in a row I have had that the right way round and somebody in this yard is going to check me and I would rather they did it on a wet morning."**

Kellan Rusk came out with the ages and the four sheets and gave them, and did not enter the paper on the boards, and did not say that he had not entered it, and nobody troubled to ask him.

**"Three hundred and eighty-three on the form, two hundred and eighty-nine on the charter, three hundred and seventy-seven on the line under Silling's first one with nothing on it ever, and a hundred and eighty-six days on the letter. Four figures, and I have said them all out loud on a Sunday, and I would like whoever is keeping count to put that down."**

He put the letter away in his coat and came back out with his hands empty, which nobody had asked him to do.

**"And there is nothing on the long table either,"** he said. **"Four sheets, not one of them entered, not one of them refused, and not one column cut under any of them since the spring. And there is a fifth paper on those boards and it is going home up a lane with the two hands that wrote on it, and it is going home up that lane with the two hands that wrote on it, and this doorway will not see it again."**

Soren Rill gave the long sheet from the boards about an hour later because he gives it every morning now whether anybody wants it or not.

**"Four hundred and eighty-seven at the head, the four hundred and eighty-sixth under the line, the four hundred and eighty-eighth over it. And it went up by one in the night, and it will go up tomorrow, and there is no column cut under it for a route and there is not going to be one."**

The boy was through with his two cans at about the seventh hour as usual and the pair went up on the north board above the two ruled lines, in his own hand, and he did not read it out and not one person asked him to, for the fifth morning running, and this time he did not even explain.

**"Two hundred and fifteen of four hundred and thirteen."**

At about the eighth hour a man came round the outside of the wall to find out whether the lane the household had named went past this gate, and a man went with him as far as the turn at the fourth place and showed him, and stood there while he worked out that it did not.

The light went about half an hour before that, and the scrape in the mud at the turn at the fourth place had dried out and gone to nothing by the time the two of them got back, and neither of them could show anybody where it had been.
