# Chapter 717

## The Thirtieth Of The Nineteenth, And A Hand Laid Flat On A Sheet At The Doorway Of The One Room

The wind came over the top of the wall before the light was properly on and went through the yard from the north side, and it lifted the corner of the sheet on the middle table twice before Odile Vray put a lump of stone on it. She said afterwards that she had done that on purpose and would take it off at the fourth hour, and that anybody who wanted to read the four could come and hold the corner themselves.

Nobody came and held the corner.

The clerk came up the lane at about the sixth hour, on foot, in his own boots, and a mile and a quarter of that lane had come with him. He said so to the first person he met and he did not wait to be asked.

**"I am going to be behind all day and I am telling you that at the start so that nobody in this yard builds a morning round me. I have to be back up that lane before the light goes, because there is a woman at the top of it who wants a pen and has been waiting two days for one, and if I do not get back she will keep it until next week and it will be her fault."**

Odile Vray told him that the man he wanted was on the sill and that the man he did not want was in the one room, and that she had not been asked to keep the lane.

Marek Vale had his schedule on his knee at the north end of the sill with the wind taking the corner of it, and he had it weighted with two stones he had taken off the seed benches without saying so, and Tova Reed, who was at the benches with her back to the wind and a slate on her knee, watched him do it, said nothing, and wrote it down.

A woman came across the yard about half an hour later with a question about the third tray from the end, which wanted turning out of the wind and could not go back where it had been because of a wet thing in the corner that had not been dry since the spring. She asked Marek, because he was the one nearest the path.

He said the seed keeper had it and would have it written down. The woman said she could not make the seed keeper hear from the path without shouting, and that the seed keeper had made it plain she would not be made to hear from the path. He said he would go and ask her himself, and she said that was all right but that she would be standing there.

He went. He came back about a quarter of an hour later and told the woman what Tova had written down, and the woman said the whole thing to him again in her own words, and he wrote it on the back of his hand because he had no paper on the sill, and then the woman went and did it herself. The third tray went out of the wind at about the third hour and not before, and two of the seeds in the second tray had gone over in the time it took, and Tova Reed marked them in her own book without telling anybody what they were and without asking anybody to help.

Nobody answered her and nobody came over. The two seeds stayed in the book under two marks and she did not tell anybody what the marks were.

Then the fourth line came round, and it came round in the doorway of the one room, the way it does, and Nia Vale was the one with her own sheet on the door frame and her own thumb on the fourth of the four.

There were about nine people in the yard within the reach of it. Harlan Vetch was on the wall at the far end and could see it and could not hear it. Odile Vray was at the boards with her back to the doorway. Kellan Rusk came out of the room about a minute after she had finished.

She said it once and she did not say it to anybody in particular.

**"A hundred and fifty-nine in force, twenty-nine taken, a hundred and thirty not. And the nine people are standing there because they always stand there on the morning it comes round, and one of them is a woman from the sluice who has not understood in four years what any of it is for."**

She put her thumb on the fourth line and left it there while she said the rest of it.

**"There is nothing in front of me this morning to rub out. The fourth is mine and it has been mine since I started keeping it, and I have never once rubbed out one of the others, and there is a man in this yard who has told three people in three years that I have."**

The woman from the sluice said she had not understood it in four years and was sorry for it, and Nia Vale told her she had not asked for that either.

Sera Quill had her slate out from under her arm by then. She gave one of the two and kept the other, and said so in the same breath.

**"One hundred and seventh, and ninety-fourth, thirteen apart. And the ninety-fourth is the one I carry. It has gone a step with the other because they step on the mornings they step on and on no others, and it is not on a wall and it is not on a sheet and it is not in the rack, and I have said that here more times than I am going to say it again."**

Kellan Rusk came out of the one room and stopped in the doorway beside her and waited until she had put the slate away before he said anything at all.

Then he gave the aggregate and he gave it in the voice of a man reading somebody else's paper, and he said whose it was first.

**"That is Nia Vale's sheet and not mine, and the head of it is four hundred and seventy-eight. Under the line on that sheet is the four hundred and seventy-seventh and over the line is the four hundred and seventy-ninth. And I am reading her paper and not certifying it. She keeps it. I keep the rack."**

He went through the doorway again and then came back out, which he does.

The clerk got his turn about the seventh hour, when the wind had dropped enough for a man to be heard at both ends of a yard.

He had a fold of paper in his inside pocket and he took it out and put it on the boards, and Odile Vray looked at it and did not move it.

**"That is a charter figure,"** he said. **"Two hundred and eighty days. And I have had it in my own hand since the spring, out of my own office, for the same document, and mine says three hundred and two. So either your yard has had thirty days that nobody told me about, or one of us has been reading the wrong line, and I have to fill a form in before the light goes and I would rather fill in one I can stand behind."**

Kellan Rusk stood in the doorway and did not come out.

**"No."**

The clerk turned round.

**"I am going to say that once and then I am going to go and fill in my own figure, because both of the figures on this board are somebody else's paper and neither of them is mine, and I will not put a number under my own name that I have got off a wall in a yard I do not work in. Yours is two hundred and eighty and it is Nia Vale's figure on a sheet she keeps, and the three hundred and two is whatever your office had in the spring, and one of those two is about to be older than the other and neither of them is going to change in the next four days."**

**"That is not an answer."**

**"It is the only answer there is this morning. There are two figures and one of me."**

The clerk stood there for a while with his hand flat on the boards, the way people do when they are about to lose an argument they have already won once.

**"Then I fill in three hundred and two,"** he said, **"and if that is wrong it is wrong in a building with a roof on it and not in a yard with a stone on its paperwork."**

**"It is both,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"I have been in both."**

Then the clerk did the other thing he had come up the lane for, which was to ask after the haulage arrangement out of the fourth place, and Odile Vray told him that it had not begun and would not begin until the fourth place had put a cart on a road it had not yet looked at, and he wrote that down, and then he asked her whether the fourth place would be looked at this week.

**"Not by me,"** she said. **"I have a wall."**

Then he asked whether the second place was on the same arrangement, and Odile Vray told him it was on nothing at all.

**"A place with no water in it is not a stop on a haulage road. That is not a complaint, it is a fact about a cart, and you can put it on your form in whatever words your office uses and it will come out the same."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the wall about half an hour before the light went and gave the channel and the run and the two stones, and gave them in an order of his own because he had been asked for nothing and had decided what the order was.

**"The far stone is nine hundred and eleven and the near one is eight hundred and sixty-four. There is forty-seven of masonry between those two stones and there has been forty-seven of masonry between those two stones since the wall went up."**

He wiped his hands down his thighs.

**"The channel has one thousand and three hundred and thirty-four hundredweight in it, which is eight on yesterday and the largest it has been in a week, and it is the four hundred and eightieth of a run that started before any of us stood in this yard."**

Then somebody at the boards asked him for the window, and he gave it, and then a man from the north row with the cough asked him which half of it had moved in the night, and got an answer he had clearly expected.

**"Four hundred and forty-seven of it. The one over the top is two hundred and twenty-nine and it is the one that went. Two hundred and eighteen underneath and it has not moved since yesterday morning, which was a day this yard did nothing at all to."**

The man said he had thought the other one went, and Harlan Vetch told him he would be right tomorrow morning and wrong again by noon, and that a figure that changes hands every night is not one anybody ought to have an opinion about over a barrow at eleven in the morning.

Then he went off down the wall with a hod, and the two of them left it there.

A woman at the gate then used one figure as an argument about a letter, which is the only use she has ever had for it.

**"A hundred and seventy-seven days,"** she said. **"And nothing has happened to it. Not a stamp and not a hand and not a scratch. It is the only thing in this holding that has been exactly where it was for as long as I have been standing at this gate, and I have stopped finding that reassuring and started finding it useful."**

She was not told she was wrong and she was not told she was right either, and the tap joint at the back of the tap house wept its one drop into the saucer and stopped, and had proved nothing about that morning or any other.

Kellan Rusk shut the rack at about the sixth hour, in the ordinary way, with the key turned and put back on its nail, and then gave the three ages to a man who had come up to the door to ask him whether a form he had at home was worth keeping.

**"Three hundred and seventy-four days on the register form. The charter is two hundred and eighty, and you have had that figure from two mouths this afternoon and one of them was a stranger. And Silling's own second ruled line is at three hundred and sixty-eight with nothing on it, which is the emptier of the two objects in that room and the one nobody ever asks after."**

He closed the rack and stood with his palm flat against it.

**"No field was taken this morning, nothing waited, and I am not going to tell anybody what for, because in nine years of standing in that doorway I have never once been able to find out what the fourth line is in force of."**

Marek Vale ruled an hour off the bottom of his own schedule at about the fifth hour of the afternoon, in the open, with three people watching, and gave the hour to the woman who keeps the common key at the second place, who took it without asking what it was for and who will use it for the road and not for the water.

The clerk went up the lane before the light went, with three hundred and two days in his own hand, and this holding's two hundred and eighty stayed on a sheet inside the one room where nobody outside it could reach it.
