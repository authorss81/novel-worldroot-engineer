# Chapter 721

## The Fourth Of The Twentieth, And A Rope Laid In A Circle On The Wet Ground And Moved Twice

The crews came up the middle road before the light with spades and a rope and eleven baskets of heartwood that had been cut and seasoned three miles away by people who were not in this holding and had put no name on any of it. Nobody in this yard asked them where the heartwood came from and two of the people who cut it had come up the road to see the ring go in and stood at the back and said nothing at all.

Marek Vale had a schedule with three hours ruled under it, the fourth having been given away on the first morning of the month, and his own terms on the back of it, which are not the crew's four and have nothing to do with them. He read his own out once to whoever was nearest and put them away again.

Odile Vray had the ring set out with a rope where the crews' man wanted it, which was the gate end of the wall and inside it, about nine paces across, and it took until the eighth hour to get it true, and twice it was moved and once somebody stood in it and had to be asked twice.

The mason from the third place watched the whole of that and then said the thing he had come to say, and he said it before anybody had put a spade in the ground.

**"Not there."**

He put his thumb down on the rope at the gate end.

**"That is where the wall throws its water. There is not a winter in four that this yard has not had standing water at the foot of that course, and a thing with rootwood in it will sit in it and go soft at the collar and you will lose four of every ten you put in and you will not find out which four for two years."**

The crews who had come up the road with the baskets had their own man, and he had put the rope where he wanted it before anybody in this yard had an opinion, which was the gate end and inside it because the branch comes out there and because nine paces was what his own people had measured on a different ground in a different month. He said so out loud to nobody in particular and then waited to see whether anybody in this holding would tell him otherwise.

Nobody told him otherwise. The mason from the third place told him the water, and the crew man looked at the mason for about a second and then said the thing he had come to say, and he said it as a man who has nine bodies and one morning.

**"He is right about the water and I am not moving it, and I am going to tell him why I am not moving it. We will lose four in ten. I have lost four in ten before on ground that looked better than this and I am not standing here in the rain pretending that is a reason to be somewhere else, and I would rather lose four in ten on ground I chose than eleven in ten on ground somebody else chose for me."**

Then he asked Marek Vale one question, which was how many hours were on the schedule, and Marek told him, and told him that the order inside them was his own and the terms were on the back of the sheet and that anybody could take one out and nobody had to.

And that is the whole of what Marek Vale said about it, and he stood at the top of the rope for the rest of the morning and gave no order of any kind, and about half way down it he sat down on a stone and got the seed trays moved out of the rain because Tova Reed was at the far end of the yard with a slate and the rain was coming across sideways and could not have found the trays if they had not been moved.

He did not ask her whether he should move them and she did not ask him whether he was going to, and afterwards nobody in that yard could have told you which of the two of them had done it.

The ground came up wet at the fourth hour and hard at the seventh, and the second half of the ring took three times as long as the first, and the crew man put that in his own book in his own hand and did not round it.

The mason from the third place stayed about an hour and then went home, and he had already said the one true thing he came to say, and he said a second thing on his way out that was not addressed to anybody in the yard.

**"The hours on that sheet of yours are one body short. I have counted nine and you have written for eight, and I have laid around eleven men for nineteen years and I will tell you what nine bodies do and what eight bodies do at the foot of a ring, and eight bodies will have that hole open at the fourth hour and it will not close before the light goes."**

Marek Vale looked at his schedule and then went and counted the bodies on the ring himself, in the rain, on his own, and came back and did not change the sheet.

**"It is one body short,"** he said. **"It is the ninth hour that is short, and the hour was on the schedule before you came up the road and nobody has taken it off it. You were right about the bodies and I did not choose the hour, and the hour is still not yours, because the hour is in the schedule and it was in the schedule before you came up the road."**

Then he did the thing that is his own contribution and the only one he has ever been able to give this holding, which was to write a set of terms of his own and put them on the rope where nine people could reach them.

**"Anybody may take one out. Nobody has to take one. Nobody is to be asked whether they want one, and anybody who asks anybody else in this yard is doing it wrong, and my four and the four on the middle table are the same four and they are two pieces of paper and not one, and there is not a fifth of anything in this yard this morning."**

Nia Vale came to the doorway with her own sheet and rubbed the fourth of the four off it in front of about nine people who had come over to see whether a ring could be planted in the rain.

**"A hundred and sixty in force, twenty-nine taken, a hundred and thirty-one not. And it is a wet morning and that is the last time I am going to say the numbers into a yard with a hole in it."**

The girl from the second place read the same figure off the rack inside the one room about half an hour later and got it right, which was twenty-five mornings into her counting in this yard, and she said so out loud because she had been keeping it and nobody else was.

**"A hundred and sixty, and it went up by one and not because of the ring. It goes up on its own mornings and I have watched it go up on three of them now and it has nothing to do with anything anybody does."**

Sera Quill had the slate out before the girl had finished and gave both of hers before anybody had asked her for either, which she has never done on a fourth-line morning before.

**"One hundred and eighth, and ninety-fifth, thirteen apart. They have gone a step since Friday and only because Friday was one of the mornings they go on. And the ninety-fifth is the one I carry, and it is not on a rack and it is not on a wall and it is not on a sheet. You may have that without the reason."**

Soren Rill came through the rain at about the fifth hour to get a look at whether a ring could be planted where it had been put, stood at the edge of the rope for about two minutes looking at it and not at the men, and then gave the long sheet from the boards to anybody who was nearest, which was a man with a spade.

**"Four hundred and eighty-two at the head. The four hundred and eighty-first under the line and the four hundred and eighty-third over it, and those are three separate figures and if anybody adds them together today I will take the sheet off the wall myself."**

The man with the spade asked whether the figure had gone up because they were planting, and Soren Rill said that it had gone up by one every morning for seven days without a single thing having been planted or laid or mended, and that he was tired of the question and that the question was fair.

Kellan Rusk came out of the one room into the rain with a form under his arm and stood on the step and did not go into it, and a woman came across out of the middle of the dig to ask him whether she was supposed to put something in it.

**"You are not supposed to do anything with it. That form stands at three hundred and seventy-eight days. In nine years of standing in this doorway I have never once found out what a body is meant to do with it, and I have watched people carry it about and hand it over and take it back and not one of them has ever written on it."**

**"Then I will leave it."**

**"Leave it. You want the other three and here they are. The charter is at two hundred and eighty-four days. The letter is at a hundred and eighty-one and nobody has been near it since the spring. And Silling's own second ruled line is at three hundred and seventy-two, which is the emptiest page in this holding and has been for as long as I have been standing in a doorway."**

He went back in and shut the door, and the form stayed under his arm inside and did not go to the dig.

Harlan Vetch came down off the wall in the rain at about the sixth hour and had to stop twice because of the digging.

**"Up at the top of it, nine hundred and fifteen. Down here at the gate end, eight hundred and sixty-eight. Those two have been forty-seven apart since the wall went up and you could set a rule on it, and it will be the same in the wet."**

A man at the boards asked him for the channel while he was on the ladder and he said he would give it when the ground came up. The ground came up about half past six.

He wiped his face with the back of his wrist and came down the last three rungs backwards.

**"Eight on yesterday, one thousand and three hundred and forty hundredweight of it, and the run calls that one the four hundred and eighty-fourth. And the window is four hundred and fifty-one, and before anybody asks me: the half over the top went in the night and it is two hundred and thirty-one, and the one underneath stood at two hundred and twenty."**

A woman came at that point to ask whether the terms were hers to take away, and he told her that any of them were and that nobody was to ask anybody else whether they wanted one, and she went off with one folded into her apron without anybody watching her do it.

Then he said the thing that made four people look at the ring.

**"Dig your uphill side first. That is what the two hundred and thirty-one is for. The ground on the top of that ring is still soft from the night and the ground on the bottom of it is already going, and if you have got nine bodies and not eight you will do it in the order the water did and not in the order you would have picked."**

The crew man put his spade in the uphill side first and did not say where he had got it from, and nine bodies dug until the light went and the ring closed at about the seventh hour of the evening with the last basket emptied and nobody standing in the middle of it.

A man at the gate asked Marek Vale whether a ring in the ground meant the four terms on the middle table were finished, and Marek Vale said no, and gave the reason the way he gives reasons, once, and then put his schedule away.

**"It means eleven baskets and nine bodies and a hole that closed and not one of the three is a figure anybody has given me. It does not mean a definition and it does not mean anybody has signed anything and it does not mean that thing at the branch head is any easier to read than it was on Sunday, and I would like this yard to stop hearing things in spades."**

Then the crew took their tools off the ring in the order they had dug it, nine of them, and went up the path together, and the last of them put the rope back on its nail where it had hung before there was a rope.
