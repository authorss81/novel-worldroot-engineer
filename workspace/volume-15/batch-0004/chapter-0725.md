# Chapter 725

## The Eighth Of The Twentieth, And A Stool Put Outside The Gate For Somebody Who Did Not Come

It was a Saturday and about twenty people were in the yard and none of them had come from up the lane, and the whole of the morning's business was a thing that happened between people who were not the ones it was about.

Marek Vale had a sheet folded in his coat with a line drawn on it from a place name to another place name and nothing else on it, and he took it out about the fifth hour and looked at it and put it away again twice before he took it out a third time.

Soren Rill saw him do it the third time and came over and stood with his back to the boards so that the yard could not read it, which is a thing he has done for other men before and has never done for himself.

**"Give it to me."**

**"It is a route. It is the one I would put in front of them."**

**"No it is not."**

He took it out of Marek Vale's hand in the plainest way anybody takes a piece of paper out of another person's hand, and folded it, and went and put it inside his own coat.

**"I will carry it as far as my own house and I will keep it in a drawer and I will not take it up that lane. And I want to say the reason in the yard and not in your coat, and the reason is not politics and it is not a fence I am sitting on."**

**"Then say it."**

**"That is a line between two place names drawn by a man who has never been to either of them in a wet week. And the thing that is going to have to hold up there is not a route. Nobody up that lane has ever in their lives been asked to hold up a route, and if you put a route in front of them this month you will get a yes or a no about the route, and either one of those is a wasted morning, and I am not prepared to spend a wasted morning of theirs on it."**

Somebody at the gate said that the engineer of record had a schedule and terms and no reason to be told he could not bring a sheet.

Soren Rill turned round and said it to the yard.

**"He can bring a sheet. He can bring eleven. He can write out every hour of every week of the rest of this season and put it on the middle table beside the four terms and none of it is a route and I will not carry it and he will get that once and no more."**

A man at the boards said that was convenient, and that a man who would not carry another man's sheet was not a bridge but a fence with an opinion, and that the whole of this holding was being held off from a lane by one man refusing to walk up it.

Soren Rill did not answer him quickly. He put his hand flat on the boards and left it there, and when he did speak he spoke to the boards rather than to the man, and he spoke for about half a minute before he said anything that anybody in that yard would remember afterwards.

**"I have walked that lane nine times since the middle of the year. I have been turned back at the second bend three times and let past once, and I could tell you which was which, and you will not get it out of me, because the answer is that I do not know what they are doing this month and neither do you."**

He turned round then and looked at the man.

**"If you want to say I am a fence, say it to my face, out loud, in front of these people. I will stand here and take it. And then you can go up that lane yourself this afternoon and be let back or not, and you will get the same answer I have got nine times."**

The man did not say it to his face. He said something else instead, about there being a holding here that had to be run, and Soren Rill let that go as well, and then he said the other half of it, and said it more quietly, and it was the part that mattered.

**"And nobody in this yard is going up that lane this morning and nobody is going to say one word about a choice up there. I have not asked anybody what they will hold up. I do not know what they will hold up. I have known for eleven days that they will choose something and I have no idea what, and there is not a person in this yard I could put in front of them who would be able to say it for them, including me."**

Then he went to the boards and gave the long sheet because he has given it every morning this week whether or not anybody wanted it.

**"Four hundred and eighty-six at the head, the four hundred and eighty-fifth under the line, the four hundred and eighty-seventh over it."**

A woman at the gate then said that the figure in force was a count of the people standing in the yard, and counted fourteen out loud, and had counted fourteen out loud before anybody had answered her.

The girl from the second place told her it was not and said so while she was still counting, and the woman said where she had got the idea, and the girl said it was not hers either and it came off a board in a room four counties back before either of them was born.

Nia Vale came to the doorway with her sheet about an hour before the light went and rubbed the fourth of her four off it in front of the Saturday crowd, and she did it late, and she said nothing whatever about the doing of it.

**"A hundred and sixty-one in force, twenty-nine taken, a hundred and thirty-two not. And I have had four people ask me this month what the count is a count of and I have told all four of them the same thing and it is this: it is a figure that somebody has kept in order for a long time, and I keep it because it was given to me, and I have never been able to make it smaller and I have never been able to make it mean anything."**

The girl read the same figure off the rack inside the one room a little later and got it right on her twenty-nine mornings in this yard, and did not say the number of her mornings out loud this time and nobody asked her to.

Sera Quill had the slate out from under her arm before the girl had finished reading the rack, and gave both of them at once, which is not what she usually does.

**"One hundred and ninth and ninety-sixth, thirteen apart. They have gone a step since Tuesday because Tuesday was one of the mornings they go on. And the ninety-sixth is the one I carry. It is not on a wall, it is not on a sheet and it is not in the rack, and there is a girl in this yard who counts how many mornings I say it on and I have stopped pretending I do not know her."**

Kellan Rusk came out with the ages and gave them in the usual order and then stopped in the doorway and said the part that is his and not the room's.

**"Four of them, and I am giving them to you on the step because it is raining. Three hundred and eighty-two, which is the form. Two hundred and eighty-eight, which is the charter and the oldest of the four by ninety days. Three hundred and seventy-six, which is the ruled line under Silling's first one and has never once been written on. And a hundred and eighty-five, which is a letter that has sat on a shelf since the spring."**

He put his hand on the frame.

**"There are four sheets on the long table. Not one of them has been entered, not one of them has been refused, and there is no column cut under any of them. And the paper in Soren Rill's coat this morning is not coming in here, and if it comes back up this lane with a date written on it I will say what I would have said before the date was on it."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the wall and gave the stones and then the channel and then the window, which is the reverse of the order he has used all week and which he announced.

**"Nine hundred and nineteen at the top, eight hundred and seventy-two at the gate, and forty-seven of masonry between them. I gave those three in a different order about a week ago and it made a difference to nobody except me."**

He put the hod down before he said the next of it.

**"That channel went up by eight in the night and stands at one thousand and three hundred and forty-six hundredweight of it. The run has it as the four hundred and eighty-eighth and the run has been right every time I have ever checked it."**

He looked at the gate while he said the last of it.

**"Four hundred and fifty-five of window and it is the one over the top that went in the night, two hundred and thirty-three, and two hundred and twenty-two underneath standing where it stood on Friday morning and has not moved since."**

Then somebody put a stool outside the gate at about the sixth hour, and put it out of the wind, and left it there, and about four people looked at it and understood what it was for and none of them said anything.

It had been done before. There was a reach of wall outside this gate where anybody sitting down could see four miles of the lane and could not be seen from the lane, and people had been putting stools on that reach of wall since before any of the four terms were written down, and on this morning somebody put one there because they had heard what had been said up the lane by a man with a sheet in his coat.

One stool was the whole of it. Not a second one came out of a house that morning.

The stool stayed empty all afternoon and it is still outside the gate, and nobody has brought it in.

The one person in this holding who could have signed the low board's offer put his hand flat on it at about the seventh hour and left it there for a while and did not take it down and did not sign it, and he gave the reason out loud to two people and to the yard.

**"I cannot sign a thing whose terms are not written anywhere. That is not virtue and it is not fright. There is a board at this gate with an offer on it and no date and no conditions, and I will not be one of two men on a thing with no conditions on it, and if the conditions were written I would read them twice and probably sign, and that is the whole of my interest in it."**

He put his hand down and went home up the middle road, and the offer on the board was where it had been all month and had not been taken down by anybody and had not been given a date by anybody.
