# Chapter 722

## The Fifth Of The Twentieth, And A Quarter Turn Of A Sluice And A Cart Of Lime Going Soft Beside It

The draw started at the fifth hour with nine bodies and a tin measure and it was a real one, which means it was slow, and which means everything that happened to it happened at the speed that a thing of that size happens.

The crew that planted the ring a day ago ran the draw, because the ring is theirs and the crews in this holding have never once been told to stand somewhere and they were not told it on this morning either. They had a book of their own with their own columns in it and a man at the head of it with a wet sleeve.

Auret Sill came with them at about the seventh hour because the sluice at this end and the sluice four miles off are the same water and she is the one who knows which is which, and nobody asked her to come.

The quarter turn went in at about the ninth hour.

The man who buys had a cart of lime standing in the yard going soft in the wet and he came down the path to the sluice to see whether there was enough running in it to set the lime, and the sluice was half a turn open because the draw had it there, and he put his hand on the wheel and opened it a further quarter turn without asking anybody, and stood there with his hand on it.

It took about forty minutes before anybody noticed and it took another hour after that to find out how much of it had gone where nobody was measuring.

Odile Vray found it first and she did not shout, which everybody in that yard found harder to take than shouting.

**"Take your hand off it."**

The man took his hand off it and said he had not known it was being drawn and she said she knew he had not, and then she said the other thing.

**"You have taken an hour out of a book nine people have been filling in since the fifth hour and there is no way of putting that hour back in it, and nobody in this yard gets to say afterward that it was spoiled. It was not spoiled. It was had."**

The man who buys said he would pay for the hour and she told him there is no currency that buys an hour in a book.

Then he asked whether the lime had set.

**"No,"** said Odile Vray.

They went and looked at the lime. It had gone about two inches at the bottom of the cart and the rest of it was still good and the cart could be moved under cover and there would be a loss of about a third of a load and nothing else, and the man who buys stood beside the cart afterwards with his hands in his pockets and said nothing about it.

The crew man at the head of their own book wrote the hour down where it had happened and did not write a reason and did not write a name, and when the mason from the third place came back an hour later with lime for the drainage that was his own and not out of the man who buys's cart, he asked which of them had done it and was told and then told what it was going to cost, and he looked at the man who buys for about a second and then looked away and took the lime to where it was wanted.

The draw went on after that for another three hours with the same nine bodies and nothing whatever different about it, which is the part that made the loss real, and the crew said so themselves in their own book on the page after the hour, in a man's hand, without being asked to say anything about it.

Auret Sill read the record at about the third hour of the afternoon and found a minute in it that had been entered against the wrong hour, and took her own slate out, and would not have it changed.

**"It is in at the minute it was in at. I will not have a reading moved because it makes a column look tidier and I have watched a place lose a season's water to tidier columns and that book is nine men's."**

The crew man agreed with her out loud, in front of the rest of them, and then did not change it either.

Soren Rill came through at the fourth hour and asked, as he has asked all week, whether the ring had gone into anything.

**"It has not gone into the long sheet and it is not going to. The head of that sheet is four hundred and eighty-three. Under the line on it is the four hundred and eighty-second and over the line is the four hundred and eighty-fourth, and those are three figures and none of them adds to another."**

**"It is in the ground a day."**

**"It is in the ground a day. It is not on that sheet and no column has been cut for it and I would not cut one if this yard asked me to with a paper and a witness."**

The boy who carries water came at about the seventh hour and wrote the pair on the north board and put the chalk down on the ledge and then picked it up again and put it in his pocket, which is a thing he has not done before.

**"Two hundred and thirteen of four hundred and nine."**

A man at the boards said the boy had better read it out for the yard and the boy said he could not and would not and gave a third reason.

**"I cannot read them off the board from where I am stood without turning my head round, and I would have to turn my head round with a hod coming past. And I have heard a man read them out wrong twice this month who could read them."**

Nobody in that yard read it out, and nobody asked him a second time, and nobody said thank you.

The man who buys was still on the boards at about the fourth hour with the barrow of lime he had gone out for at the ninth hour, standing beside it while it was unloaded, and he asked, once, whether anybody in the crew would tell him whether the drainage would have saved the lime.

The crew man said that it would have saved about half of it, and then stopped, and then said that it would not have saved the hour in his book either, and that the man was owed both facts and would get them both and was not getting an apology with them.

Harlan Vetch came down off the wall towards the end of the day and gave the four figures that go with the stones and the channel and the window, and gave the channel last, which he has not done on any other morning this week.

**"The window is four hundred and fifty-two and it is two hundred and twenty-one underneath that went and two hundred and thirty-one over the top that stood."**

He leaned the hod against the wall so that he could turn round to it.

**"Eight hundred and sixty-nine is the stone at the gate and nine hundred and sixteen is the stone at the top, and there are forty-seven of masonry in between them, and the forty-seven is the only one of those three figures that has never once moved."**

**"Nine hundred and sixteen at the top,"** somebody said, **"and that is the end the crew are working from, because that is where the ground was soft this morning."**

**"Then it is a figure doing a day's work for once,"** Harlan Vetch said, **"and I have been giving it out for a month telling nobody what it was for."**

He put his thumb on the wall and gave the channel last, because the rain had come back and he was about to go up and the channel is at the low end of the wall where you can talk over it.

**"One thousand and three hundred and thirty-five hundredweight in it, five down on yesterday, and the four hundred and eighty-fifth of the run. That is the count and I have never once known what it is a count of and I would rather work it out on my own in the rain than hand it to you as a guess."**

Then he went up the ladder and left the yard below him to make of that what it liked.

The man who buys went past the compost board on his way out with his cart and read the paid column out loud without meaning anything by it.

**"Paid at thirty-one. That is a dung account. That is thirty-one loads of something and never got rid of, and whoever is keeping that board has been sitting on it since the spring."**

Odile Vray told him what it was in a flat voice, which is that the paid column stands at thirty-one and the other column is empty and is not discharged in thirty-one or in anything, and he stopped there and did not ask her another question, and she never told anybody in that yard what the rest of that board is for.

Kellan Rusk came out of the one room at dusk with the four ages and gave them standing in the doorway with the rain coming across the step behind him, and gave the register form first and then would not give the other three until somebody had explained why the register form was the first one.

**"Where the register form stands this morning is three hundred and seventy-nine days. The charter is two hundred and eighty-five, which is the newer of the two by a long way, and I have told you the two of them in the order they are stacked on the shelf behind me, which is not the order you asked for. Silling's own second ruled line is at three hundred and seventy-three and is empty. And the letter is at a hundred and eighty-two days, which makes it the only piece of paper in this holding that has never once been read out in a yard, and that includes today, when there has been more to say than there has been all month."**

The book on the crew's own table in the one room was still open at the hour it was lost in, with about a third of a day's figures in it, and it was not read out, and it was not tidied, and the man who bought the lime was still standing beside his cart at the gate with a third of a load gone soft in the bottom of it, and no figure anywhere in this yard recording what that was worth.
