# Chapter 719

## The Second Of The Twentieth, And A Man Standing At The Head Of The Branch With His Back To The Yard

Sunday put about nine people in this yard, and seven of them were on stone, and the eighth was the boy with his cans and the ninth was a mason from the third place who had come to look at a sill nobody had asked him to look at and who stayed for the whole of it.

The light came late and went early and the middle of the day was a bad hour to be standing in a yard in, and about four people took that hour off without saying so and were not missed.

The two who were going to the head of the branch came down the middle road together at about the fourth hour because the hours at the head of the branch are their hours now and they had come to find out where they were. One of them laid stone for three councils and had a hod and no chart. The other kept the common key at the second place and had come four miles for it and had a fold of paper in her coat with something on it that she could not find.

Neither of them was Marek Vale and neither of them had been asked.

The man with the hod was a long way from home and wet through and had not eaten, and he sat down on the second step with the hod between his knees and asked the woman with the key whether the branch head was running.

She said she did not know.

Then she asked him whether he knew, and he said he had been four miles that morning and had not gone near it, and that the two of them had come down this road on the strength of a line somebody had drawn and not one of them had read anything off the pipe since they got here.

A man at the boards said that there was a gauge at this end and that it could be read by anybody who wanted to read it, and the woman with the key said that she had read it in the spring and that it had told her nothing at the time and that she had not been back to look at it since.

Marek Vale was about eight feet away and did not say anything, and about four people in that yard looked at him while he did not say it, and about four people in that yard noticed the silence and none of them said what it was about.

The mason from the third place came up behind them at the far end of the wall and asked the question that had nothing to do with either of them, which was which half of the window had gone in the night.

He had been at that sill about an hour before he said anything about it and he had said a good deal about it, mostly to himself and to the man holding the other end of the thing.

**"There is a stone in the third course of this sill that came off a different part of this yard and I would not have laid it,"** he said. **"It is a good stone. That is not the argument. It is a stone that came out of ground that had been wet all the winter and it is going to sit in a wet joint and in four years it will be the first one anybody looks at."**

**"You laid it,"** Harlan Vetch said.

**"I laid it,"** the mason agreed, **"because I was the one here and it was the one that would go, and I have been waiting a month for somebody to ask me about it and nobody asked me, so I am telling a yard."**

The man who buys said that a stone was a stone, and the mason told him that a stone was a stone and that the water in this valley was not interested in that, and went back to it.

He stayed until about the third hour and then went home, and nobody in that yard said one word about the stone again.

The woman with the key said the one over the top had gone, and the man with the hod said it had not gone at all and that it was three days since either of them had gone, and the mason from the third place said it had gone because a figure that does not go up does not go down either.

Marek Vale was at the head of the branch when all three of them came and asked him to settle it, and he stood with his hand on the pipe and looked at the place where the water was and could not tell them.

He said it in one sentence and did not try to make it longer.

**"I cannot tell you from in here. I would be guessing and you would both hear it."**

Then he took his hand off the pipe and said that Harlan Vetch was on the wall and had the stones and would say it in a different voice.

Harlan Vetch said it from forty feet up and did not come down for it.

**"Four hundred and forty-nine of it. Two hundred and thirty over the top, two hundred and nineteen underneath. It is the one over the top that went, and it is the eighth morning running that one of the two has gone every single night, and it will be the eighth on Wednesday, and it is the only thing in this yard that happens the same way twice in a week."**

The man with the hod said he had lost a morning's argument over it, and the woman with the key said she had lost the same one, and neither of them said anything at all about the sentence that had come out of the branch end before they went up it.

Then they went up the branch together to find out how far back from the end they were meant to stand, and Marek Vale stayed at the head of it and let them go.

He had the far end of it in front of him and the two of them walking away up a wet path toward it, and he said the thing out loud to nobody, once, and then took his hand off the pipe and left it there.

**"The far end of a thing is held by two people and neither of them is me, and no instrument anywhere in four counties says whether it is on or off."**

He put his hand back on the pipe and left it there for a while and did not say anything else about it before the light went.

Kellan Rusk came out to the branch end at about the sixth hour because somebody at the yard had told him there were people standing at a pipe who were not on any paper.

**"I have been told there are two of you at the head of the branch. There are two of you at the head of the branch. I cannot tell you what the branch head is in force of, and I have not got a paper with it on, and if you want one you will have to do without it, and I am telling you that now instead of at the sixth hour of the evening."**

The woman with the key took the fold of paper out of her coat and looked at it and put it back.

**"The charter on mine says two hundred and eighty-two days and it is the only number on the sheet and I have been looking for the other one since I got off the road. So we are going up there with one figure and not two and that is what we have."**

**"The rack has three hundred and seventy-six days on the register form and a hundred and seventy-nine on the letter, and the ruled line in Silling's own book is three hundred and seventy and there is nothing written on it, and none of those three is what you are missing either. I have looked."**

She said that was the answer she expected and that it was worth having.

The man with the hod had spent the afternoon doing what he does best, which is putting a piece of the fourth course back in at the gate end and telling somebody why it cannot go any other way. He put the falling half of the window into a sentence about it.

**"Two hundred and nineteen underneath, and that is a night's water off this valley and there is a point at which the underside of that course stops being a joint and starts being a crack, and it is not the number of stones. It is how much came down while you were asleep, and I have used it twice now and it has told me the truth both times."**

Soren Rill came through at the gate end with the girl from the second place behind him and gave the long sheet from the boards, and gave it before she asked for it, which he has done once a day all week.

**"Four hundred and eighty at the head, the four hundred and seventy-ninth under the line, the four hundred and eighty-first over it."**

The girl wrote it on the far stone in chalk and asked him whether the fourth column was ever going to be filled, and he said that in nine years of standing at that gate he had never once seen anybody fill it and that he had stopped asking.

Then she read the two stones off the wall and gave them in the wrong order on purpose, far before near, and Harlan Vetch told her that forty-seven of masonry did not care which end she started at and that he had watched two people be beaten by that this week.

**"Eight hundred and sixty-six at the gate and nine hundred and thirteen at the top. And the channel is one thousand and three hundred and thirty-seven hundredweight, which is eight on yesterday, and it is the four hundred and eighty-second of the run and that is the largest that weight has been since the wall went up."**

The man of the north row with the cough caught Marek Vale at the gate end on his way past about the seventh hour and asked him the question that everybody in that yard had been carrying about since the pipe changed hands and nobody had asked it yet.

**"Are you still coming up that branch in the mornings, or is it off your list now."**

**"It is not off my list. There is a schedule in my coat with three hours on it and none of them are that hour, and an hour does not arrive because two people came and asked me for it in the rain."**

The man looked at him for a moment and then said something that was nearly kind, and Odile Vray was two feet away and cut across it before it finished being said.

**"Do not,"** she told him. **"He has just given away the only thing he had on that branch and you are not going to thank him for it in my yard."**

**"I was going to say he should sit down."**

**"Then say it to the wall."**

Marek Vale went out through the gate and shut it behind him and did not stand at it.

At dusk the two of them were still up at the head of the branch with the light failing on the last two hundred yards of wet path behind them, and one of them called down the branch and asked him whether it was to be read at the sixth hour or the seventh.

He said it was theirs now and they could read it whenever they liked and he was not going to come up and tell them.
