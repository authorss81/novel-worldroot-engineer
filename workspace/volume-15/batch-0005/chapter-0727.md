# Chapter 727

## The Tenth Of The Twentieth, And A Tin Of Cold Tea Left Standing On The Coping Since Sunday

Monday came in wet and stayed wet, and about eleven bodies were in this yard before the light was properly up, and the ring at the gate end had taken Saturday's rain down into itself without anybody standing in it looking.

There was a tin of cold tea standing on the coping at the north end where somebody had put it down on Sunday morning and nobody had drunk and nobody had washed out, and about four people stepped round it all day without going near it.

Marek Vale put two things on the boards before he had said good morning to anybody, which is not how he had done it for eleven weeks. He said nothing about the first of them. He said what the second one was.

**"Those are terms. They are not a contract and they are not a promise, and nothing in this holding has agreed to them, including me. There are four of them and each one can be read out and each one can be argued with, and I have set them down so that a man who does not like one of them can say so without losing a place."**

Odile Vray read the sheet standing up, in about the time it takes to drink a cup of anything, and handed the fourth of the four back to him word for word.

**"That one says what this holding will keep and not what it will be held to. If anybody at this gate reads it as the other thing then they are half right and half wrong, and I would rather it was not on the paper in that shape at all."**

**"It is not the other shape and I am not changing it."** He turned the sheet so that the yard could see the back of it, which was empty. **"If it were the other shape then it would be a promise. A promise with four hours standing on it is a thing that can be held against a man, and I have come eleven weeks down this lane and I will not be the reason a body of people gets held against."**

Then he read the schedule, and it took about nine seconds, and it was the shortest thing that has gone on those boards in eleven weeks.

**"Four hours. That is the whole of it. The four kinds of work are set to happen at the same time and these are the hours they have told me they will happen at, and every one of them was given to me by the people who are going to do it and not by me. If a set of people tells me it cannot be done at their hour then that hour comes off and I write another one in its place. If nobody tells me anything then it stands, and I stand the consequence of it standing."**

Harlan Vetch had come down off the wall to hear that, which he does, and he answered it from the bottom of the ladder with his hod still in his hand and the run before anything else in it.

**"It has been the four hundred and ninetieth of the run since Friday morning and the channel came into the yard this morning at one thousand and three hundred and forty-nine hundredweight of it. Neither of those two has changed because a man has been down this lane talking."**

Somebody at the gate then asked what the run was for.

**"It is the count of mornings since the third launder came in. It is not a load and it is not an order and it is not a figure anybody can be asked to do a thing with."**

**"Then why is it kept."**

**"Because it was kept."** Harlan Vetch wiped his hands down his thigh. **"I have never had a reason for it and I have never once had a day when somebody put it in front of me and asked me to do a thing with it."**

**"Four hours,"** Perrin Dae said, coming the length of the boards with two fingers on the far edge of the sheet, **"and there is a figure against the old charter that has been sitting in this yard since the spring. Two hundred and ninety days. I read that on Friday as the day this yard stops being answerable to anybody, and I have had a carter on the road with two loads of timber standing on the fourth place's books since Saturday evening."**

Nobody said anything for about four seconds, which in this yard is a considerable time.

**"It is an age,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"Four of them, and none of them is a date, and the oldest of the four is the one a carter was given in your own order. Three hundred and eighty-four is how long that form has lain in the drawer with the key on it. Two hundred and ninety is the charter, and there is nothing in it that anybody in this yard could read as a year. The ruled line under Silling's first one is at three hundred and seventy-eight and has never had a mark on it in its life. A hundred and eighty-seven days is a letter lying on a shelf. That is the four of them, and you may write them down in whatever order you want them in, because I am not handing you one."**

**"I will get the timber turned and I will get it turned at my own cost and not out of the fourth place, and I will not be taught arithmetic at this gate by a man who keeps a book in a drawer with the key on a nail."** Perrin Dae put his thumb on the sheet one more time, on the window, and left it there. **"And while I am here, nine hundred and twenty-one hundredweight is the figure at the top of that wall. I have been asked for stone against it twice this week by two men who are not from this holding, and both of them were told the same thing, which is that it is a figure at a wall and not a quantity anybody sells."**

**"It was me that told them that,"** Harlan Vetch said, **"and I will tell them again at the same price. Eight hundred and seventy-four hundredweight at the gate end has been standing on the same two stones since the wall went up, and it is not mine and it is not yours and it is not for sale."**

Ismay Rooke, who is a mason of the third place and one of the five that came down the middle road on their own feet and were not fetched, had been reading the schedule at the sill end for about a minute without touching it. He came over and stood in front of it and counted something on his own fingers under his breath and then said it out loud.

**"I am not on that, and it is not an hour."**

Marek Vale did not ask him which of the four he was not on, which cost him something to do without asking and about nine people in that yard could see it cost him.

**"Say the hour and say what is in the way."**

**"There is no hour in the way. There is a wall in the way and the wall is at a hundred and fifty-one courses and the courses are not days, and I will not be scheduled by the course."**

**"The fourth of the four hours is at the fourth hour of the afternoon and the sill is on it."**

**"Then the sill is not at the fourth hour of the afternoon, and I have told you why, and you can put that down in that shape."**

Nobody argued with him after that and nobody thanked him either, and two people at the gate looked at one another about it in the way people do.

Odile Vray took the schedule off the boards and turned it round, and the window was on the back of it in somebody else's pencil and not in Marek Vale's, with the half that moves on the even days underlined by the hand that had written it.

**"Two hundred and thirty-four over the top standing and two hundred and twenty-three underneath, and four hundred and fifty-seven the whole of it. That is not the order I would have written them in and it is the right pair."**

**"It is a hole that has to go in on the low side of that ring this morning,"** the crew man said from the gate, where he had been standing with his own book under his arm and his spade in his hand. **"That is the only use I have for a pair of figures and I will not pretend to you that there is another one."**

**"There is another use and you know what it is."** He turned round. **"Nine hundred and twenty-nine will be the top of that ring before the season turns and there is not one of those beds standing today."**

**"I have not written that on my board and it is not on yours either, and it is a figure about a future, and I have been in this yard nine years and I have watched about forty of those come at us."** He turned a page of his book over with his thumb. **"Two hundred and thirty-four tells me where to put a spade. That is the whole of what it tells me, and I have got nine bodies to put in a hole before the light goes."**

Soren Rill came through about the eighth hour and gave the long sheet because he gives it every morning now whether anybody wants it or not, and he read it the other way round this morning.

**"Four hundred and eighty-ninth over the line and four hundred and eighty-seventh under it, and four hundred and eighty-eight at the head. It stands a day higher than it stood on Friday and nobody in this yard has ever asked it what for, and there is no column cut under it for a route, or for a paper, or for anything else a person might have to answer for."**

**"Is that the pair at the gate and the top?"** the man from the fourth place's road asked him.

**"That is three of the four figures at that head and the fourth is on a board at the wall and it is not mine and I do not read it."**

Then it began to rain again and about half the yard went to the sill end at once, and Marek Vale stood at the boards with the four terms on his knee and the schedule gone from under his thumb, and Nia Vale came out of the one room at the fourth hour with her cloth and her own board and put herself on the far side of the tables from him without any arrangement being made about it.

He wanted one figure off the rack to go in the corner of his own sheet, and it was the only thing he asked anybody for all morning, and he had to ask his sister for it across eleven feet of wet stone in front of about twenty people.

**"There is nothing on that rack for you today,"** she said, **"and nothing will be invented at the fourth hour so that you can write it down. If you need a figure out of that room this week then come and stand in it and ask me at the hour I keep, and not on the boards with the rain going."**

She wrote her own three on the seed board afterwards and turned it round and went back inside without one word about it, and Marek Vale left the corner of his sheet empty, and the cost of that was the girl from the second place finishing the rack on her own with nobody to check a line against, and she got to thirty-one mornings in this yard before the light went and did not say the number out loud and nobody asked her.

The tap joint at the back of the tap house was weeping when they went in at the ninth hour and it was still weeping when they came out at the tenth, and it had not proved anything about any morning of the week and did not prove anything this one.

Tova Reed had the four ages on the corner of the seed board in lead pencil before the light went, letter first, in the order she was given them, and she had not asked for one of them twice and did not say out loud that she had written them down.

**"A hundred and eighty-seven, three hundred and seventy-eight, three hundred and eighty-four, two hundred and ninety,"** somebody read off the board at about the tenth hour, and Tova Reed said it was her hand and that she had been asked and that she was not going to be asked again because it was already there.

And a clerk from up a lane stood at the gate for about half an hour reading that board from the wrong side of the rain, and then went out and down the lane without saying what he thought of the four of them, which is the first time in a fortnight that he has gone away from a number without saying.

Nobody read the pair of boards aloud. The boards were where they were with the drawer's key on its nail beside them, and the two figures on them had not been read out by anybody in this yard for eleven weeks and were not read out today.

At dusk Ismay Rooke came back through the gate, looked at the schedule for about as long as it takes to count a sill twice, and took a piece of chalk out of his own pocket and drew a line through the fourth hour of the four, and said nothing to anybody while he did it.

Then he put the chalk back in his pocket and went up the middle road, and Marek Vale looked at the sheet, which had two of the four hours crossed through in two hands and the third crossed through in chalk, and did not fill in the name column and did not say why.
