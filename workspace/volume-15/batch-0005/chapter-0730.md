# Chapter 730

## The Thirteenth Of The Twentieth, And The Ink On The Boards Still Wet At Dusk Because Nobody Had Anything To Put Over It

The sheet was still on the north boards on Thursday morning, and about nine people were in the yard by the second hour and there was a man standing in front of it with his hat in his hands before the light was properly up, so the crew man came out of the tap house and looked at him and then went back in and got his book.

He did not take the sheet down and he did not put anything over it, and by dusk the chalk on it had gone shiny with rain and there was still no cover over it anywhere in this holding, because nobody had come with a board and nobody had thought to.

The man who wanted to read the sheet out was let. Nobody stopped him and nobody helped him.

He got to the fourth line of it inside a minute and stopped and stood back from it and said the thing that about four other people in that yard had also come to say.

**"Five of nine came through. That is most of them. You are telling me a dry-season draw and a flood load went over a ring of nine and five of the nine stood it."**

**"I am telling you six stood the draw and five stood the load,"** the crew man said, **"and there is a difference in that and you have just walked over it. One of the six let the load out at the far end of itself, so that one held once and failed once. The one at the top came up in the second hour with the root on it and it is not there and it will not be there again this season. Nine beds dug, six, five, one, one. That is the arithmetic of it and you can do it on your fingers and you have just not done it."**

**"And the eleven households up the north branch."**

**"Say what you are about to say."**

The man looked at the boards for a while.

**"That if they had taken it there would not be a hole in the middle of it."**

**"There would not be a hole in the middle of it and there would also be eleven households under a load they told us in three hands they would not stand,"** the crew man said. **"And I have written neither of those on this sheet because I have got the one that happened and not the one that might have. You are welcome to say the second thing out loud in this yard and I would ask you to do it about as often as you like, and it will still be a thing that did not happen, and you will still not be able to stand in front of a board and point at it."**

Nobody took the man's name off anything, because his name was not on anything, and he did not apologise and was not asked to, and he stayed in the yard until about the ninth hour looking at the two lines that were not good.

Auret Sill came in at the third hour with a basket on her hip and did not go to the boards at all. She went to the seed board, and there was already chalk dust on the end of her sleeve when she came out of the store, and she said the two halves of the window once and in the order she found them and did not say which one of them had moved, which is the one thing about the window that everybody in this yard says and she does not.

**"Four hundred and sixty the whole of it. Two hundred and thirty-five and two hundred and twenty-five."**

**"Which of them went overnight?"** somebody asked her.

**"One of them did and it is not my figure and it is not on my board because I was not asked for it. I have a pump to open at the second place in two hours."**

And she was gone before anybody could ask her a second thing, which is what she does, and it was Tova Reed who had written the pair on the corner of the seed board in chalk and had been told to and had not asked twice, and a man from the fourth place's road came along the table at about the fourth hour and put his thumb on the two numbers and asked whose hand that was.

**"It is hers and she was asked and she is not going to be asked again,"** somebody told him. **"If you want a figure written on a board in this yard you will have to ask the person who keeps the board, and that is the whole of what there is to say about it."**

**"I will not keep a second one,"** the man said, **"my own copy, off somebody else's board, because I have got the pair of them chalked on my own wall and the chalk says eight hundred and seventy-seven hundredweight and nine hundred and twenty-four hundredweight, and I would sooner have the chalk than my paper."**

Ismay Rooke came in from the third place at about the fourth hour and got as far as the sill end before somebody stopped him and made him look at the boards, and he read the sheet with his hands behind his back and gave the two figures off it in the order a man gives figures when he is thinking about what is on top of what.

**"One thousand and three hundred and forty-seven hundredweight in the channel and that is the four hundred and ninety-third of the run. That is the fifth time it has come down since Friday and every time it has come down it has come down five and not eight, and there is a reason for that somewhere in a book in a town four counties off and none of us will ever see the book."**

**"You have said that the run is not a weight,"** somebody told him.

**"It is not a weight and it is the number of mornings, and I count mornings for a living and I am telling you the two of them together because they are the two numbers I had in my head when I came up the road."**

Odile Vray came through about the fifth hour and gave the head of the long sheet in a shape nobody had heard her use before, standing with her back to the boards so that the forty-odd people of yesterday could not all read it off her at once.

**"Four hundred and ninety-one at the head. Four hundred and ninety-second over the line, four hundred and ninetieth under it."** She stopped. **"And before any of you start, that is a count of days and not a count of anything else, and I do not know what it is for and neither does the man who keeps it, and I have known him eleven weeks and I have never once heard him say what it is for either."**

**"It is not for anybody,"** Kellan Rusk said from the one room door. **"It goes up by one in the night. It has gone up every night since the spring and nobody has ever written down what causes the one."**

**"Then it is a figure nobody has looked after."**

**"It is a figure nobody has looked after and it is the oldest of the four I keep and I keep it because it was here before I was."**

A woman came in from the middle of the four places at about the sixth hour, and she had seen the boards on Wednesday and had heard the pair of numbers being written on the seed board that morning, and she put them together out loud in the middle of the yard, in front of about eleven people, and she was wrong.

**"Two hundred and seventeen of four hundred and seventeen, that is what your board says this morning, and that is a proportion. That is nearly half of them stood it and the other half of them did not, and that is the ring."**

The crew man put his thumb on the two of them on the board and did not raise his voice.

**"Four hundred and seventeen is not beds and it never was beds. It is a count of mornings measured against something on a board in a room four counties back, and it goes up by one on every other morning whatever happens in a ring or a hole or a yard, and if it stood for anything at all then this holding would have failed every other day since the spring and stopped doing anything."**

**"Then why is it written up in the same hand as everything else."**

**"Because it is written up. That is all that is. It is written and nobody reads it out and that is the condition of the thing and it is not mine."**

**"It is a figure about what was held,"** the woman said, **"and two hundred and seventeen out of four hundred and seventeen is the best thing on either board in this yard."**

**"Then take it as that,"** the crew man said, **"and take the ring as nine, and six, and five, and one bed gone with the root on it and one end soft, and put the two side by side in your own head for as long as you like. One of those is a count and one of those is a hole in the ground and they do not touch, and anybody who puts them together has done it because it sounds better and not because it is true."**

She said that was fair, in about one word, and did not use his name, and went and stood at the boards with the rest.

The compost board was read out at about the seventh hour by a man who had been asked what the thirty-one was for.

**"Paid at thirty-one,"** Nia Vale said, **"and not discharged in thirty-one or in thirty or in anything at all. And it is not a count of this year and it is not a count of this yard and it is not a dung account and there is no number in this holding that goes with it."**

**"Then what is it."**

**"It is a thing that is paid. I have been saying that sentence in this yard for eleven weeks and you have all heard it and none of you have asked me what a thing is, and I will not answer it this morning either."**

Nobody went near the compost board after that and the man went and stood in front of it for a while with his hands behind his back, the way a man stands in front of a thing he has been told is not his business.

Tova Reed had the other two ages on the corner of the seed board in lead pencil by then, in her own order and not anybody else's, and nobody read them out loud and about four people read them with their backs to the yard. Silling's ruled line stands at three hundred and eighty-one and has been empty since the day she ruled it, and a letter has sat at a hundred and ninety days since before any of us were in this yard.

The man who lays for three councils was at the gate for about half an hour in the middle of the day and read two of the four ages off the boards and would not read the other two, and gave the reason in four words.

**"Two hundred and ninety-three on the charter and three hundred and eighty-seven on the form. Those two are on a board and anybody may have them. The other two are not mine to read and I have been in this yard four times this week and I will not become the man who says the rest of it out loud."**

**"You are one of two people who hold the far end,"** somebody said, and he did not answer that and went up the middle road at about the eleventh hour.

The light went about half an hour before the end and the ink was still wet on the boards, and the crew man came out of the tap house and looked at it and did not cover it, and there was nothing in this holding to cover it with and nobody thought to go and find something.

At dusk the woman who had made the proportion out loud in the middle of the yard came back through the gate with her slate under her arm and asked him for the three numbers off the board.

He wrote nine, and six, and five, on the flat of the slate, in that order, and gave it back to her without one word about them.

Then she put the slate under her arm the other way up, so that the two she had said out loud that morning were against her ribs and could not be read by anybody standing in front of her, and went out of the gate and down the middle road.
