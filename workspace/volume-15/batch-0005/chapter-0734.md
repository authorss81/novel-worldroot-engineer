# Chapter 734

## The Seventeenth Of The Twentieth, And A Barrow Left Standing Wheel First In The Mud Because There Was Nowhere To Put It

Monday was an ordinary morning and nobody in this yard said so. There was nothing on the boards except figures, and a barrow that would not go anywhere.

The barrow came out of the gate end at about the second hour and went round the wall twice and came back and stood in the middle of the yard wheel first in the wet, and it stood there until the light went.

There was nowhere to put it. There is a wall on one side and a boards on the other and a tap house behind that, and the ground between them is the ground people stand on, and four different people in the course of the day considered moving it and moved nothing.

The mason from the third place was on the sill at the fourth course from about the second hour and worked at it until about the seventh without stopping once, and at about the fifth hour somebody asked him whether he was going to finish the course.

**"Not this morning. One thousand and three hundred and fifty-three hundredweight went into the channel in the night, and that is the four hundred and ninety-seventh of the run, and every one of those mornings since the spring has cost me about the same and I have stopped pretending I do not know what my own back is worth."**

**"That is not an answer, it is a complaint."**

**"It is a complaint and it is also the answer, and I have not got a second one."**

The drawer in the one room was opened at about the sixth hour and shut again inside two minutes, and the key went back on its nail, and about eleven people watched that happen because it happens every morning and there is not much else to do in a yard on a Monday.

Kellan Rusk did not do it behind a door. He came out into the light with the drawer open behind him in his own hand and stood there and shut it where about nine people could see him do it.

**"In that drawer are the two figures this holding is not allowed to forget. The near stone reads eight hundred and eighty-one hundredweight. The stone at the top of the wall reads nine hundred and twenty-eight hundredweight. Neither of them is a copy of the other. That is the only reason that drawer shuts at the sixth hour every morning and it is not because there is anything in it worth taking."**

**"There is a form in it."**

**"There is a form in it and there is another form four miles up this lane and I have told four people this month that I do not know which of the two is the copy, and I will not find out on a Monday morning by opening both of them in a yard."**

He put the key on its nail and the nail is by the frame and the key has been on that nail every morning since the spring.

Nia Vale and the girl from the second place were in the one room together from about the fourth hour until about the eighth, and the girl wrote, and neither of them read anything out.

**"Four hundred and ninety-five at the head,"** Nia Vale said, and the girl wrote it, and nothing was said out loud in that room for about a quarter of an hour except the two of them agreeing who was going to say a thing and who was going to write it, and then Nia Vale said **"four hundred and ninety-fourth underneath, and four hundred and ninety-sixth on top of it"** and the girl wrote that too, and neither of them looked at it again.

Harlan Vetch came down off the wall at about the seventh hour and gave the window in one sentence and did not stop walking while he said it.

**"Four hundred and sixty-four is the whole of it. Two hundred and twenty-seven underneath is the half that went in the night, and two hundred and thirty-seven over the top has not moved since Saturday morning."**

**"You never say them in the same order."**

**"No. And you have never once written them down, so it has not cost anybody anything."**

Auret Sill came in at about the eighth hour with nothing on her hip, and she gave the four ages off the corner of the seed board in the order of a woman who is going somewhere afterwards.

**"Three hundred and ninety-one on the form. The line Silling ruled under her own first one stands at three hundred and eighty-five and it is blank, and it was blank on Friday and it will be blank on Sunday. A hundred and ninety-four on the letter. I have read the age of it in three yards now and not one of the three could tell me what is inside it. And two hundred and ninety-seven on the charter last, because I am the one who is signing it and I have said all week that I would put it last."**

**"There are two hands on that paper and one of them is yours."**

**"One of them is mine and the other is a mason's, and neither of them has been asked to carry a place with no water in it, and if a third hand appears this week then somebody has found a way round that and I would like to be told about it before it is signed."**

The boy who carries water was told at about the sixth hour to put the pair up on the north board and did not, and said one thing about it and then carried on with his two cans.

**"The water came late. I will not stand at that board with a wet sleeve over a figure I did not have time for, and somebody else will have to write it and it will not be me."**

The girl from the second place wrote it at about the seventh hour instead, in her own hand, on the north board and above the two ruled lines, and neither she nor anybody else said it out loud afterwards, it was not read, and the boy was not thanked for not writing it.

**"Two hundred and nineteen of four hundred and twenty-one,"** the man from the fourth place's road said to the boards about an hour later, and was told by four separate people that he was not to do that, and stopped.

Nobody asked him why he had done it and nobody told him, and it is not known why any of the four said what they said.

The compost board was looked at by about six people on this Monday and the same thing was said about it by one person.

**"Paid at thirty-one. It has not been discharged and it is not going to be, and thirty-one is not a count of this yard and it is not a count of this year, and I have answered that question in four yards this month and I will not improve the answer for anybody."**

A man asked the old man at the end of the north row, in the ordinary way, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, whether he wanted anything, and the old man said no, and it was the ninth morning running that a person in this yard had put anything to him at any hour, and not one thing else was put to him after that, and the count of questions this holding has put to him since the spring is not a number anybody here keeps.

There were twenty-nine fetchings against him and none of them fetched and none of them asked, and he was at his own door at dusk with a stick in his hand, and nobody in this holding crossed the yard to look at him.

The use of the things in this yard was written up on a page at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, in a hand that is not a clerk's hand and has not been for a year.

> **Kept by the woman who counts bodies for her trade, and not entered in anything else:**
>
> **Barrow out and back, eleven. Eleven is the number and there has never been a twelfth line on this page and none is going to be cut.**
>
> **The barrow at the gate end has not gone anywhere since the second hour and it is standing wheel first because there is nowhere in this yard to put a barrow down.**
>
> **Sessions entered, seven. There is no eighth row under this and there was never going to be one.**
>
> **Things asked for and not known, six. Six lines, and there is not going to be a seventh for a ring or a load or a charter or a paper in a tray four miles away or a road that nobody has walked.**
>
> **The rule under these six is open and there is nothing in it.**
>
> **Nobody has signed this page and nobody is going to be asked to.**

Nobody said that this was a settled morning and nobody said that it was an unsettled one, and at dusk the mason from the third place pulled his own line along the top of the fourth course and it came out straight, and a boy of about nine asked him whether that was the end of the wall.

**"That is the end of what I have been asked for,"** the mason said. **"The wall is not finished and I have not been told that it is finished and I would not go on it if I was, because a man who goes past what he has been asked for ends up doing it twice."**
