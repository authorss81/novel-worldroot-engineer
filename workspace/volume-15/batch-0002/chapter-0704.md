# Chapter 704

## The Seventeenth Of The Nineteenth, And One Question Put Twice To Two People At Two Different Hours Of One Morning, And Two Answers That Were Both True And Were Not Reconciled

The council heard the engineer of record first, in the tool house at about the sixth hour, before the yard was full, with the door shut and no clerk and no book.

Nobody in this holding was told anything about it at the time. The sill crew came on at the fifth hour and worked at the sill and could hear nothing through a stone wall, and that was not arranged by anybody.

Marek did not bring the paper in. He said that he wanted to be asked rather than to read, and Perrin Dae said that was fair, and asked him.

He gave them one case in about eleven minutes and he did not make it smaller and he did not make it larger.

**"The gauge on the far side of that crossing is on the list of five. It is the third item on the list of five and it is the only one that cannot be read from this holding's own ground in a high week. On the twenty-second morning of building there is no hour in the day when a person can be on the far bank and at the seventh hour gauge here, and that is not a moral question and it is not a feeling. It is a distance and an hour."**

He put his hands flat on the tool bench.

**"I have been eleven days into a schedule that I did not write and I have watched a list of five get built out of a piece of paper in a yard, and the fifth hour of it is real and the seventh hour of it is real and the tenth hour of it is real, and I have watched four of the five work this month while I carried a bucket. I want the span and I am not going to pretend that wanting it is the same as needing it, because it is not. It would be the same on a Tuesday in a wet year as it is on this morning."**

Auret Sill asked him one question and it was the only one anybody in that room asked him.

**"If we do not build it, what does the gauge do in a high week."**

**"It does not get read by anybody from this holding. The woman at the second place who reads it reads it from the near bank and she reads it wrong by about a foot in a high week and she will go on reading it wrong because it is the only reading there is. I have not found out from anybody whether she knows. I have not asked her, because asking her is a different conversation and it is not mine to have had."**

Then Emrys Dole asked whether the span would survive a flood off the branch, and Marek said it would not, and said it in the same breath and without any difficulty at all.

**"It would not, and I have that written on my own paper and I did not put it in a small hand. A loaded barrow at full is the whole of what will ever go across it. If the branch comes up in the spring and goes over that thing it takes it, and I would have spent ninety-four feet of stone and eleven mornings of people to find that out in the wet."**

Then Hanne Brack asked the last one, and it is the one that made this morning.

**"And who decides."**

**"You do. That is the whole answer and I would rather give it in ten words than have somebody in this yard spend a week finding out that I think otherwise."**

Nobody in the tool house said anything else, and the council walked back down to the yard.

They did not hear the crew until about the second hour of the afternoon, and they heard all five of them, and they heard them in the yard at the boards because a crew that has been asked a question will answer it where it works.

Odile Vray did not start. Four of the five sat down on the boards, which none of them had done all month.

The fourth place's man read the launder at about the seventh hour, at the far end of the wall, in the ordinary voice, and Nia Vale read it after him from the same stone because he asked her to and she said yes without any question about it, and the whole yard heard both readings.

**"One thousand and three hundred and eight hundredweight, and light by five, because today is one of the odd ones. It is the four hundred and sixty-seventh of the run.**"

**"Window four hundred and thirty-four, made of two hundred and twelve of falling and two hundred and twenty-two of rising. The lower figure is the one that moved in the night. The upper has not shifted since Friday. And it will be light again on Monday, because that is what it does and I am not going to apologise to anybody for it."**

Then Nia Vale read it from the same stone the way she reads her own figures.

**"One thousand and three hundred and eight hundredweight, and it is five below the morning behind, because this morning is an odd morning and I have been getting it the wrong way round twice this week and I am not going to get it wrong again in front of five strangers who came down a road to hear a man."**

He looked at her for a second.

**"That is the first time in eight days that a person has read that stone in this yard and got the parity right without being told. I would say welcome and I am not going to, because welcome on that stone is not mine to give."**

Then she read the two cut figures off the same end of the wall without being asked, which is her own ground and always has been.

**"Eight hundred and fifty-one where it begins and eight hundred and ninety-eight where the far one is. Forty-seven of masonry, and that is the largest the far one has ever been and it has got there one stone at a time and nobody in this yard has decided anything about it."**

Then Odile Vray stood up off the boards and did not sit down again.

**"You have asked me twice this week what a span at ninety-four feet would do to me and I have told you twice in my own figures and I have not moved a figure of it. I am going to say the rest of it this afternoon and I am going to say it once."**

She sat down on the boards and did not look at the five of them.

She stood up again, because she could not say that part sitting down.

**"The first thing is that you did not ask me which question I meant. There are two questions and they have been in the same sentence all month and nobody has ever separated them. One of them is can a person get there. The other one is can a person build there. Those are two different questions about one piece of ground and every mouth in this yard, including mine, has answered only the one it could answer easily."**

She counted them on her fingers without looking at her hand.

**"The second thing is that the man in the tool house just told you the gauge is read wrong by a foot in a high week and that he has not asked the woman who reads it, because that conversation is not his to have had. He is right about that and I am one of the women he could have asked and he did not ask me, and I want that on this paper as much as the span is."**

Emrys Dole said that the answer was not going to be told them by the yard, they had come for the span.

**"Then here is the third thing and it is for you and not for him,"** she said. **"Nine of the sixteen live at the far end of a branch that has run dry since the spring and four miles of that is dry ground that a loaded barrow cannot cross after rain. You can say a bridge carries a loaded barrow at full. A loaded barrow at full cannot get to the bridge in the wet, because there is a mile of it with no water in it and a woman carrying a barrow would have to carry it instead of push it."**

She looked down the wall at the north end while she said it, at two courses of laid sill and a bar across them.

Nobody argued with her about any of it.

**"And the fourth thing, and then I am finished. On Wednesday a stone came up at the third course of the north sill at about the sixth hour and four of my five were standing in a wet face by six twenty because I was awake and I heard it. And if I had not been awake, the water would have gone under another four courses before the man at the tool house end finished his hour, and I do not know that, and nobody in this yard does, and that is not a complaint about the sill."**

She turned her head towards the north end while she said it, at two courses of sill bedded back in and a bar still lying across them.

**"I am saying that a span four miles away does not put a stone back under that sill, and I would like it written in whatever this council writes that it does not, so that when you go up that road somebody in the second place cannot be told later that we asked for it."**

Then she sat down, and the four who had sat down got up, and the hearing was over, and the five of them did not stay.

Nobody in that yard reconciled anything.

Soren Rill read the long sheet out at about the fifth hour in the voice of a man reading somebody else's paper.

**"Four hundred and sixty-five at the head, four hundred and sixty-fourth beneath, four hundred and sixty-sixth above, fourth not due. The rest of what I keep: a ruled column on another sheet with no heading over it, four ruled lines under two words with nothing on them, and four sheets on that table with nothing entered, nothing refused and no column cut under any of them. The youngest of those four is a hundred and sixty-four days. The letter is a hundred and sixty-four days. They are not one figure and this morning two accounts came out of a tool house and a set of boards and somebody is going to want them to be."**

He shut his coat over nothing.

**"And I have not been asked to choose between the two accounts this afternoon and I am not going to, because I have listened to both of them and they do not contradict each other. They are two answers to two questions, and the reason there was only one question is that nobody in this yard has been willing to pay for the second one."**

Kellan Rusk read the three ages off the rack at about the sixth hour and read them the way a man reads a thing he has read four hundred times and has stopped noticing, which is exactly what a reader wants and nobody else.

**"Register form three hundred and sixty-one days. Second ruled line in Silling's book, three hundred and fifty-five, with nothing on it. Charter two hundred and sixty-seven. Three hundred and sixty-one, three hundred and fifty-five, two hundred and sixty-seven, and I have said that order twice this week and I am going to say it again."**

Then, from the step, in the middle of the day's standing, with his thumb where the thumb goes.

**"The thing on the low board by the gate has no day on it and has not been picked up and has not been taken back. I have walked past it every morning for a fortnight. This morning I looked at it properly for the first time, because five people sat in this yard asking other people hard questions and I thought I would ask a hard question of a piece of wood. I did not get an answer and I am going to leave it alone again now."**

Harlan Vetch said the two that are his, on his way to the tool house, without stopping.

**"The barrow is eleven and has been eleven every day this month, and the log on the boards inside is fifteen lines with a clean space above the fifteenth, and I have read that number over somebody's shoulder four times this week and never once asked what it is for.**"

Then Kellan Rusk.

**"The board in this room stands at thirty-nine, and the rule under those blanks stands open and empty at the thirty-ninth time. Six ruled rows on the shelf and nobody has cut a seventh for anything, including a hearing. Seven sessions and no eighth. The two books on the lower shelf are fifty-three and fifty-three, and neither of them took one word of what was said in the tool house this morning or at the boards this afternoon, and I would like whoever is keeping score to notice that a holding's own records did not hear a thing that happened inside the holding."**

He looked up.

**"And the man at the end of the north row: twenty-nine fetchings, unfetched. Five people asked me a great many questions today and not one of them was about him. I have been standing on this step for nine years and today was the first time the subject never came up at all, and I am not certain that is an improvement."**

The girl from the second place read the compost line off the board at the gate end at about the sixth hour and asked nobody what it meant and told nobody what it meant.

**"Paid at thirty-one,"** she said. **"And not discharged. I do not know what either of those words is doing on a board and I have been in this yard eleven days and I have stopped expecting to be told."**

Sera Quill wrote the two figures on the slate at the north end at about the seventh hour, in her own hand, under the two she carries.

**"Two hundred and four of three hundred and ninety-one,"** she said to the slate, and then she put the slate back on the boards.

For about an hour there were three figures on one board in that yard, which has never happened in four years, and not one of the three was entered in anything.

Then Renn Ashby came up the north row to fill a bucket at the trough and stopped, which he has not done in this yard since the first day after the days turned, and said one sentence to the boards and to nobody.

**"There are two things I am not going to say and I am not going to say them to him first, and that is the whole of what I have come up here for."**

He filled the bucket and went back down the row and nobody in that yard asked him a second question, and he has not said anything since, and nobody thanked him for it.

At dusk there were two accounts of a span at ninety-four feet in this yard, one from a man in a stone room before light and one from a woman at a set of boards in the afternoon, and both of them were true, and neither of them had been corrected, and the council had gone back up the middle road with two accounts and no arithmetic and had said that it would do that next, and the yard went on working.
