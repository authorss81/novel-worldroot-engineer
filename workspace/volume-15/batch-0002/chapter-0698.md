# Chapter 698

## The Eleventh Of The Nineteenth, And An Arithmetic Done Out Loud By The Five Who Will Have To Live In It, And A Clause On A Sheet That Cannot Be Complied With In One Place And Is Not Taken Off The Sheet For That

The crew was out at the fifth hour because a crew is out at the fifth hour and not because anybody told it to be, and the frost had gone into the ground overnight and came up out of it again about an hour later, which is the whole of what Sunday is.

The girl from the second place was at the boards at about the seventh hour with the chalk in her hand and Kellan Rusk came out of the one room and gave it to her without a word and went back inside.

He had done it deliberately and he did not say that he had, and what he was thinking, if anybody had asked him, was that he had been watching her read that board backwards for eight days from the gate and that a person who can read a board backwards can write it forwards.

She wrote the two figures under the heading, which is of a month nine months back and which had not moved.

**"Two hundred and one of three hundred and eighty-five,"** she said, not out loud to the yard but to the board, in the voice of somebody checking.

Then she put the chalk down on the sill and stepped back and did not read it, and did not read it this morning or any morning after it, and nobody in that yard asked her to and nobody thanked her for it.

Odile Vray did the arithmetic at about the eighth hour and did it out loud and against the wall, with her own seed sack and a stub of charcoal, and about twenty people heard it and none of them wrote any of it down.

**"Five bodies. Four jobs. That is the whole of my morning and it has been the whole of my morning for four days."**

She wrote on the sack while she said it, and she said the figures as she wrote them, which is a habit she has and which two people in that yard have complained about.

**"The sill face, the sluice, and the chain with the bucket, and me on whichever of those three is going to be left at four. Two on the sill face is one body for four hours. The sluice is one body for four hours and the pin is out and home twice and counted both times. The chain is one body for four hours and the chain is the slowest of the three because the chain is wet."**

She turned the sack round.

**"Five bodies at four hours is twenty hours of work and it is done by four bodies, so one of the five is doing something else, and today I am the one doing something else, and on Friday one of the other five was doing something else and it was not me. So over a week of six mornings the fifth hour has been done by thirty-two hours of work that four people did, and that is not a complaint, it is a number, and I would like it written somewhere that is not on a sack."**

Somebody at the gate said that it belonged in the book.

**"It does not belong in that book and I am not going to say that book is not a good book,"** she said. **"It belongs in a box, and I have a box, and there have been one in my own box for two years. If it goes up that lane it stops being my crew's hours and starts being a claim, and a claim gets argued with, and the first man who argues with it will not be the man who has to be on the sill at the fifth hour."**

At about the ninth hour a boy came down the middle road from one of the four households with two signatures at the foot of the message and asked, as he had been asked to ask, what the four households were being asked for and in which month.

Odile Vray told him and told him the road.

**"You are the mile and a half one and I have been in your kitchen twice this week before light and I will be there again tomorrow, and here is the thing I want said back to whoever wrote that paper. I am not asking you for an hour. I am asking you to be at the fifth hour, and if you cannot be at the fifth hour then write the hour you were, on your own paper, in the space, and the reason does not go on the paper."**

Then a second boy came up from the same place, and he was nine miles off, and he had been on a cart, and he asked the same question and got the same answer with the roads swapped round, and he did not argue about it, and that surprised the people watching.

The third household was four miles off and could only be reached on a day there was a cart going that way, and there was no cart going that way this morning, and nobody went.

And the fourth household is at the far end of the branch.

Odile Vray said so out loud at about the tenth hour, standing at the boards with her back to the water trough and looking at nobody, and she said it once.

**"There is no cart that goes there and I have not been and I am not going to pretend to anybody that I have. That branch ran dry when the reach went out as far as it went, and it ran dry in a week, and nobody apportioned it and nobody softened it and nobody has come up this lane since with a way round it. I go up that road four times a year to somebody's mother's house and I stand in a yard with no water in it and I take a message back down."**

Somebody at the middle table said that if the council got its way there would be water for it.

**"There is no council and there is no way and there is a woman at the end of that branch who has had no water since the spring,"** she said. **"I am not going to let a thing that has not happened stand in front of that in my own yard. And I am not blaming anybody and I am not asking anybody to be blamed. It is a cost and it is on the record and it is not a wound that anybody has to be sorry for."**

She picked up her chain and went back to the sluice and did not say anything else about it that day, and no hand went on her arm, and nobody in any room said one word to her about her hearing, and the seed work stayed where it was.

A boy ran down the middle road at about the eleventh hour with the figures off the stone written on the back of somebody's hand, and Odile Vray read them at the boards while she worked, in the order of a woman who is doing two things and has not stopped doing the first.

**"One thousand and two hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight, and it is five down on this morning because this morning is an odd one. That is the four hundred and sixty-first of the run.**"

**"Now the window, which is four hundred and twenty-eight, and I will give you the halves the other way round from how I gave them yesterday. Two hundred and nine of it moved in the night and that is the falling half, and two hundred and nineteen of it has not moved since Saturday, and that is the rising half. On an odd morning the lower one goes. I had that backwards twice this week and I am not going to be the man who keeps count."**

She put the hand back on the sluice and the water went two inches and then an inch and then two.

Harlan Vetch did the wall at about the second hour of the afternoon and did the boards and the drawer at the same time because he was the only person in that yard who could say both without stopping.

**"Eight hundred and forty-five where it begins and eight hundred and ninety-two where I am, forty-seven of masonry, and that is not a figure of speech and it is not going to be one. Paid at thirty-one and not discharged in thirty-one or in thirty or in anything. Behind this end of the wall is a drawer, and it is shut, and the key has been hanging on its nail since the sixth hour, and there is nothing inside it that anybody in this holding could lay a hand to, and that is not an opinion. It is a drawer."**

He stood the hod against the wall beside him.

**"And the joint at the back of the tap house came away wet again this morning and the pipe behind it has been standing where it was standing since the wall went up, and a joint that weeps and holds has proved nothing about any day of the week and I am going to keep saying so until it proves something or until I stop talking."**

Soren Rill read the long sheet out in the yard at about the third hour, at Hesta Lyle's request and in her order, because she was inside with a man who had a paper.

**"Four hundred and fifty-eight at the head, four hundred and fifty-seventh under the line and four hundred and fifty-ninth over it, and the fourth figure is not due. She has told me to say those three and not to explain them and I am not going to explain them."**

Then he read the fourth thing on the sheet, which was not one of the three, and which he had not been asked to read and read anyway because it was on the sheet in the same hand.

**"And the fourth ruled line under the two words says that a person who turns up late writes the hour they turned up at, in the space, on their own paper. That is the clause, and I want to say it out loud in this yard and then I want to say the other thing about it."**

He put his finger on the line and did not pick the paper up.

**"That clause cannot be complied with in one place. In this holding a body lives a mile and a half from the sill and can be at the sill at the fifth hour. Nine miles off, on a road that is a road and not a path, a body that leaves at the fourth hour is at the sill at the sixth hour and it is not late and it is nobody's fault and it will be the sixth hour every morning of the season, and it is going to write the sixth hour in that space every morning until the frost goes."**

He took his finger off the line.

**"I have watched five people this month write a figure onto that wall or a sack or a slate and I have watched every one of them think about the fifth hour, and there is one place where the fifth hour is not a thing that anybody can be held to and I am not going to take that line off the sheet because it is inconvenient to a mason in a yard where everybody can see him. It stays. It is correct. What it does is not carry a fault, and that is the whole of the argument and I have had it."**

Marek said nothing about the clause at the time, and later in the day, at about the sixth hour, he said one sentence about it and it was this.

**"I have been the engineer of record on a schedule for nine days and the clause is right and it is the only clause on that sheet that is about people, and I have nothing to add to it and I am not going to stand here and improve it."**

Kellan Rusk came out at about the seventh hour with the rack behind him and the key already in his hand, because locking that room at the end of a day is the last thing he does and he does it whether or not there is anything worth locking.

**"Silling's own line first, three hundred and forty-nine, and nothing on it and nothing above the word. The charter is two hundred and sixty-one days and the register form is three hundred and fifty-five days, and I have said them in three orders this month and the order has never once changed a single one of them, which is the entire argument for having an order at all."**

He closed it and put the key into his pocket rather than back on the nail, which is not the arrangement, and he noticed himself not doing it and left it.

**"And the letter is a hundred and fifty-eight days. The four on the long table are not that figure and were never that figure, and I have explained the difference twice this week and explaining it twice made it worse, so from this morning I am going to hold my tongue about it and let anybody who wants to join two figures up do it in front of me."**

The girl from the second place was still at the gate at dusk and she had not gone anywhere all day and she had eaten at the boards, and when the yard began to say the day's standing she listened to it without writing anything, and she did not go up the road with the message because there was no cart and the message was not the kind that waits.

At dusk the whole of the holding's standing came out of the one room in one breath and in the reverse of the order it usually comes out in, because a Sunday is long and a man standing in a doorway at dusk on a Sunday will try something.

**"The window is four hundred and twenty-eight, and the halves of it are two hundred and nineteen and two hundred and nine. The aggregate over this morning is four hundred and fifty-nine, and under the line four hundred and fifty-eighth, over the line four hundred and sixtieth. Boards, eight hundred and forty-five of eight hundred and ninety-two. Register form three hundred and fifty-five days, charter two hundred and sixty-one, and Silling's ruled line three hundred and forty-nine with nothing on it. The letter, a hundred and fifty-eight days."**

He leaned on the door frame.

**"And the rest of it, which I have said to you every day this month and am saying now for the last time in this order: blanks, thirty-nine, and the rule under them open at the thirty-ninth time. A barrow, eleven. A log, fifteen, and a clean space above the fifteenth. Seven sessions. Two books on the lower shelf, fifty-three and fifty-three. And the channel came down this morning at one thousand and two hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight, which is the four hundred and sixty-first of the run, and I put that one last because it is the only one of the lot of them that anybody in this yard actually wants."**

The door stayed where it was.

**"And the man at the end of the north row. Twenty-nine fetchings. He has not been fetched and nothing has been put to him, not about a clause and not about a road, and I have said that out loud every morning this month and I am not going to stop on a Sunday."**

The yard went on working. The four households with two signatures at the foot of them were still four, and one of them has not been reached, and one of them has no water in it. There was a line ruled under two words on a wall in chalk that one place on the map cannot keep, and nobody took it off the sheet because it was inconvenient.
