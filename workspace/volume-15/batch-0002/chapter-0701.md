# Chapter 701

## The Fourteenth Of The Nineteenth, And A Stone Lifting At The Third Course Of A Sill Nobody Was Watching At The Sixth Hour, Which Is The First Thing The Definition Has Ever Been Asked To Do And Not A Demonstration Of It

The stone came up at the third course from the far side at about the sixth hour and nobody saw it happen, and the woman on the sill face found it with the side of her thumb.

There was a noise like a man clearing his throat under a floor and then a stripe of clean silt about the length of a hand along the joint, and then nothing for about forty minutes while everybody in that part of the yard carried on, because four hundred people do not stop for a noise under a floor unless they have heard it before.

Odile Vray had heard it. She put her hand flat on the sill face and waited, and when the second one came she said one word, and the other three stopped.

**"Down."**

They went down onto the wet face and got the third course up inside about as long as it takes to say this with a bar and two hands each, and the stone under it was dry on the top and running on the bottom, and it came up sideways.

Somebody at the gate said that the water had got in from the far side.

**"The water got in from the far side of a joint from under the sill face, and there is a fall under here from the far side to the near side that has been in the ground since before the wall went up, and nobody has ever dug it up and I am not going to pretend to know where it comes out,"** Odile Vray said. **"It came in under a stone in a hard week and it lifted a stone in a hard week, and that is not a mystery and it is not anybody being careless. It is water."**

Then she did the arithmetic, and she did it fast, because it was before light and there was no time to be careful.

**"We are four bodies on this face today and we were going to be four on the face and two on the sluice and me on the chain, and now we are all four on the face and the sluice is shut and the chain is not going anywhere this morning. So the third hour's work is not going to happen and it is going to happen tomorrow or it is not going to happen in this weather."**

She stood up and looked along the sill.

**"And it is not going to happen tomorrow either, because we take that course up, we dry it, we bed it and we go over the four above it, and then we have to come back and check the six above that, and that is two days. Not one day. Two. And on the second of those days the sluice is shut again."**

Somebody said that the fourth house had asked about the fifth hour this week and that the answer had been that the fifth hour was the sill.

**"The answer is that the fifth hour is the sill and the sill is lifted at the third course, and I am not going to go up four miles and say anything else this morning, because the answer to that house is the same as it was on Saturday and the sill has changed since Saturday and that is not anybody's fault including the water's."**

The rotation came round at the seventh hour and it did not wait for the repair, and nobody in that yard expected it to, and the book came out onto the step while four women were standing in the wet with their sleeves to the elbow.

**"A hundred and fifty-five in force,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"One on. One on this morning, and this morning four bodies have been standing in a wet face since before light and neither of those two things has anything to do with the other one and I am not going to stand on this step and pretend it is a lesson."**

He slid a finger down under the line and left it there.

**"Twenty-nine went out at their own hour. A hundred and twenty-six are on the books at this hour, and there is a woman at the end of the north row in that number and a woman at the face of a broken sill in that number and both of them are on the books because they are both still in this holding."**

He closed the cover without putting the thumb in it.

**"No field was taken this morning. Nobody has been asked to choose anything this morning. If anybody in this yard wants to be angry about the sill they should be angry about the sill."**

Sera Quill came up the middle road with the slate and put it on the boards and opened it and said two figures and stopped.

**"One hundred and third and ninetieth. Thirteen apart, and I have not said the rest of it this morning."**

Somebody at the gate said that she had said it every fourth-line morning for a week.

**"I have said it for a week and I said it on Tuesday and I stopped saying it on Wednesday of last week and this is the first fourth-line morning since, and a yard does not get an explanation from me for free,"** she said, and closed the slate and carried it back down the middle road.

The mason from the third place read the stone at about the seventh hour, in the order of a man who has watched somebody do it four times and has counted the words.

**"It came down at one thousand and three hundred and ten hundredweight, which is eight on, because this is an even morning. The four hundred and sixty-fourth of the run. The window is four hundred and thirty-one, and the rising half is two hundred and twenty-one and that is the half that moved in the night, and the falling half is two hundred and ten and has not moved since Tuesday."**

He ran his hand along the cut below it and left his palm flat on the stone above.

**"I have stood at this gate seven days and watched four people read that stone and I have said nothing to any of them, and this morning I am the one reading it, and I am not going to say what I think a thousand and three hundred hundredweight means because I have been in this yard seven days and I do not know what anything here means except that stone."**

Then, before anybody had asked him, he said the other thing.

**"There are two things I am not going to say while I am standing at this gate, and I am saying that I am not going to say them, and neither of them has anything to do with the sill or the span or the council and I would be obliged if nobody in this yard decided I had said something by saying that."**

Harlan Vetch had the wall down to three courses at about the tenth hour and was on the sill face at about the seventh hour and was on both of them at about the ninth, and he came down at about the fourth hour of the afternoon having done the following and said it out loud in the order he had done it.

**"I laid four courses at the top of the tool house end this morning before I went to the sill, and I did not lay the four at the bottom that were due, and I did not lay them because I was standing in a wet face holding a bar for two hours. The four are still to lay and they will be laid this week if the frost lets anybody, and the wall at the gate end says eight hundred and forty-eight and the tool house end says eight hundred and ninety-five, forty-seven apart, and neither of those two figures moved this morning because I did not lay a stone above the line and I did not take one up."**

He set the bar down.

**"There is a joint at the back of the tap house and it comes away wet around the second hour, and behind that joint is a pipe that has not shifted an inch since the wall went up. Nothing goes through either of them that has not gone through them every morning of this month, and I mention it at noon on a day when a stone came out of a sill because a man who only mentions it when nothing else has happened is not watching a joint, he is keeping a record of it."**

Harlan Vetch then went and read the compost line off the board at the gate end, which he has said out loud every day of this month without ever once reading it off the stone.

**"Paid at thirty-one,"** he said. **"And not discharged. I have been saying that sentence in my own words for ten mornings and this morning I have gone and looked at it, and it says what I have been saying, and I would like it noted that a man who finally goes and looks usually finds something else."**

Odile Vray read the two cut figures off the wall herself at about the third hour, because she was standing at the near end with a bar in her hand.

**"Near end, eight hundred and forty-eight. Far end, eight hundred and ninety-five. Forty-seven of masonry sits between those two numbers and it has sat between them since the wall went up, and a span ninety-four feet long over a channel four miles off will not shift either of them by one chip."**

She wiped her hands on her coat.

**"The drawer behind that end is shut and the key is on its nail, and it went on at the sixth hour before any of this happened, and there is nothing in that drawer, and I have now been in this yard long enough to say that out loud and to be believed, which I could not have done a month ago."**

Kellan Rusk read the three ages off the rack at about the sixth hour in an order that put the two that people argue about between two that they do not.

**"Charter two hundred and sixty-four days. Register form three hundred and fifty-eight. Second ruled line in Silling's own book, three hundred and fifty-two, and nothing written on it and nothing above the word, and I am not going to say the fourth time this month that it is not a mistake, because a thing said four times is a thing somebody in this yard has decided to believe."**

Then he stood in the doorway with the door shut and the key in his hand and said the standing of the holding, and the sill was still lying open at the north end with a bar across it.

**"That is all of it today and I am not going to give you the rest of it, because a man does not need the whole standing of a holding in his head to know that a stone came out of a sill before light this morning. Thirty-nine blanks. The rule under them open and empty at the thirty-ninth time. Fifteen lines, and the space above the fifteenth clean. And a stone out of a sill is not a fortieth blank, and I have decided that today, and nobody asked me to."**

He put his hand on the frame of the one room door.

**"The barrow is eleven, the sessions are seven, the rows are six, the two lower books are fifty-three and fifty-three, and I have said none of those out loud today because they have not changed, and I am not going to stand in a doorway and present a thing which has not changed as though it were news. They will all be exactly where they are at the sixth hour tomorrow."**

He turned and looked down the wet sill.

**"And there is a man at the end of the north row. Twenty-nine fetchings, and not one of them today, and nothing has been asked of him at any hour. I did not hand him a stone. I did not ask him to take a bar. I have known him nine years and I am not starting that on the morning when four people are in the water and the whole yard is looking at me."**

Hesta Lyle came out at about the seventh hour with her sheet squared and read it in the order of a woman who has been interrupted and is not going to say so.

**"Four hundred and sixty-first under the line, four hundred and sixty-two at the head of it, four hundred and sixty-third over it, and the fourth not due. And a man came up this lane on Saturday with a line about a span and he has not been in my room and he is not going to be, and my sheet has three figures on it and a fourth that is not due and that is the whole of my sheet."**

Then the girl from the second place asked about the low board by the gate, and nobody had prepared for it and nobody answered her for about a minute and a half.

She said it was a piece of wood with something on it that had no day written on it and had been there for longer than she had been at this gate.

Somebody said that it was not for her.

**"I did not ask whether it was for me,"** she said. **"I asked what it is. And there is a ladder against the wall that nobody has gone up, and a ring of ground nobody has walked, and a board with no day on it, and I have been in this yard nine days and I am not asking anybody to give me any of them. I am saying that three people have now told me in three different days that I do not get asked anything, and I would like somebody in this yard to notice that that is three."**

Nia Vale did the four sheets at the long table at about the sixth hour with her hand flat on the end one.

**"Four sheets on that table. Not entered this morning, not refused this morning, no column cut under any of them. The youngest of the four is a hundred and sixty-one days. The letter is not going to be answered this month and neither is the sheet, and those are two separate figures that have had nothing to do with each other in four years and I am tired of pretending otherwise."**

She came away from the table.

**"The ladder has no rung climbed and no figure against either of the two people it belongs to, and the ring is unwalked and unmeasured and unpriced, and I have said those three things every day this month and this morning a young woman asked me a question instead, and I am not going to pretend that was not an event."**

At dusk the sill face at the north end had two courses down and a bar across it and the third course was lying on the boards with its underside wet, and the wall at the tool house end had three courses on it and a fourth unlaid, and a hundred and fifty-five was in force on the books at the step, and the yard went on working.
