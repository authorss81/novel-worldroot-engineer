# Chapter 706

## The Nineteenth Of The Nineteenth, And A Stone At The Far End Of The Wall With Nothing On The End Of It At All, And A Neighbour Putting A Figure On What This Holding Is Worth To It

Nia Vale found it at about the sixth hour with a cloth in her hand and she stopped in the middle of the wall and did not say anything for a while.

**"Nine hundred."**

She wiped it once and it did not come off, and then she wiped it again, because she has done this before with a different figure on a different stone.

**"It has no tail. There is nothing after the nine hundred and there is four inches of blank stone above it that has been blank since the wall went up. It said eight hundred and ninety-nine on Sunday morning and it says nine hundred today, and I do not know whether that was laid this morning or Friday or whether it was there all along and we have been wrong for a week."**

She put her hand flat on the stone and left it there.

**"Nearest the gate it says eight hundred and fifty-three and that one has not lost anything and it never will, and forty-seven of masonry is the whole of the difference between that and this and it has been the whole of the difference every morning of this month. Forty-seven is not a round figure and it never has been one, and this one has just come round to a round figure with no tail on it, and both of those are on the same wall and I have laid both halves of it."**

Harlan Vetch was at the far end at about the ninth hour and said the other figures there without stopping, because he has forty minutes of wall and a hod.

**"Eight hundred and fifty-three at the gate end, nine hundred at this end, and the top four courses are the only ones I have laid since Wednesday, and I laid them on Wednesday and Thursday and Friday and Saturday and Sunday, and that is not a round number of courses and I am not going to give it one."**

He shifted the hod.

**"That joint at the back of the tap house has come away wet at the second hour and the pipe behind it has not shifted since the wall went up, and it has proved nothing about any day of the week. Twelve mornings I have said that. Let somebody in this holding write it down so that I can stop."**

The boy from the second place came up the middle road at about the eighth hour and this time he did give the message to somebody, and he gave it to Odile Vray at the boards and he stood there while she read it.

It was four lines and it had two signatures at the foot of it and a third below those, and it took her about a minute to read and about four minutes to stand still afterwards.

**"The second place will not put nine bodies on that crossing."**

She said it out loud at the boards and then she said it again at the gate so that people at both ends heard it, and she did not editorialise it and she did not stand on it.

**"That is the message and that is all of it. It is four lines long and there are three signatures at the foot of it and I am not going to read the other three out because they are not about the bridge and they are about the branch and I have said enough about the branch this week."**

Somebody at the gate asked what it was worth.

Odile Vray did the arithmetic out loud and against the boards with her hand, not on a sack, because it was not hers to write down.

**"Sixteen bodies for twenty-two mornings, and nine of them are not coming. That is seven. And seven is two on the sill face and one on the sluice and one on the wall at the tool house end and me and the chain, and two on the sill face is half of what a sill face takes, so it is one and a half on the sill face and it is a broken day every day for twenty-two mornings."**

She took her hand off the boards.

**"That is the price. It is not a figure about standing and it is not a figure about anybody's opinion of anybody. It is seven bodies and twenty-two mornings and a mile of dry ground, and there is no road on which those three become nine."**

Then somebody asked what the second place had got out of this holding, and Odile Vray answered that in a way that made the yard go quiet.

**"Ask them. Go up the road and ask them, and it is a fair question and it is a question I have asked at four houses in a week and three of them gave me a straight answer, which is that this holding has given them five women on a sill at the fifth hour every morning since the days turned, and a bucket, and a mason who laid a sill face in the wet in the dark in the week the frost came."**

She put her hand flat on the boards and took it off again without looking at it.

**"And that the third of those five went up the road on Thursday and has not come back, and that the second place has not had a bucket of its own since the spring, and that the branch above it is dry because a reach went out as far as it went. None of that has been softened and none of it is going to be, and if anybody in this holding has been waiting for me to say that the span will pay for the water, they have been waiting eleven days and they can stop."**

Nobody thanked anybody.

The book came back onto its shelf at about the seventh hour, because a book comes back onto that shelf every ninth day whether or not anybody wants it to, and this yard knows the interval the way it knows the hour a frost lifts.

Kellan Rusk came out and stopped on the step and said it in the standing answer because there was no better one in this holding and he had been asked for one a week ago by a young woman at the gate and had told her so.

**"It came back and it moved nothing. The board in that room stands at thirty-nine and the rule under those blanks is standing open and empty at the thirty-ninth time, and thirty-nine is what it has read for four years. It is not a fortieth anything this morning. A wall with nine hundred cut on the end of it did not open that rule. A span of ninety-four feet did not open it. A woman from the second place saying no to nine bodies did not open it."**

He went in without closing the book.

**"There is no ordinal to a return and there is no weight and there is no allowance, and I have said that twice now to two people and I am going to keep saying it until somebody gives me a reason to say anything else."**

The girl from the second place walked down to the far end of the wall at about the seventh hour and read the stone, which nobody had asked her to do and which she had done every morning she had been here except the two when the sill was up.

**"One thousand and three hundred and eleven hundredweight, down five, odd morning. Four hundred and sixty-ninth of the run.**"

**"Window four hundred and thirty-six. Two hundred and thirteen of it is the lower half and it fell in the night. Two hundred and twenty-three is the upper and it has not moved since Saturday. Near board eight hundred and fifty-three. Far board nine hundred. Forty-seven of masonry between them, and I am the first person to say that number today and I have said it four times this week."**

The fourth place's man was standing at the near end and he listened to all of it without saying anything at all, which from him is a speech.

Then she wrote the two figures on the board under the heading, which is of a month nine months back and has not moved.

**"Two hundred and five of three hundred and ninety-three."**

She put the chalk down and wiped her hands and did not read them out, and nobody asked her to, and she had been at that gate for ten days and had carried two papers up that road and had asked this holding for nothing at all until the morning she wrote them herself.

Harlan Vetch came down at about the second hour of the afternoon and did the compost line and the drawer, in the order of a man who has done the far end and is now doing the near end.

**"Paid at thirty-one, and nothing has gone out of that line in thirty-one or in thirty or in anything. And the far stone on this wall has come round to a round figure with no tail on it. I would like anybody who has been making remarks about that stone to go and look at the other one, which is eight hundred and fifty-three, has not moved, has no tail on it, and never wanted one."**

He put the key on its nail at the sixth hour and stood looking at the drawer for a moment.

**"Shut. Empty. Nail. Three things and three facts and I have never once known what is in it, and neither has anybody in this holding, and that is the arrangement and I am not changing it."**

Soren Rill read the long sheet and the four sheets in one walk at about the third hour, in the order of a man who has been asked to be precise this week by people from four places.

**"Under the line four hundred and sixty-sixth, over it four hundred and sixty-eighth, and the head of the sheet four hundred and sixty-seven, and the fourth of the four is not due. And four sheets on this table, none entered, none refused, no column cut under any of them, and a piece of paper on the middle table with nine hundred feet on it has not gone near any of these four and neither has a council of five."**

He put his hand flat on the end sheet without touching it.

He buttoned his coat over the paper he was carrying, which told the yard nothing and him something.

**"The letter is a hundred and sixty-six days and it is a different figure from every one of these and has been for four years, and on the morning this holding turns a round number on its own wall I am going to say the one thing I have been not saying for a fortnight and then go and walk the inside edge."**

He turned to face the gate while he said it.

**"There are two things I am not going to say. And I am not going to tell him first, and I have said that in this yard twice already and I am going to go on saying it until one of us stops believing it."**

Then he walked the inside edge of the eleven acres with the gate shut behind him and did not go near the ring, which is bare, and did not go near the ladder, which has no rung climbed on it this month, and did not go near the low board, which has no day on it and has not been picked up and has not been taken back.

The girl from the second place got her bundle from under the wall at about the fifth hour of the afternoon and came to the middle table and put two papers down on it and left them there, and they were the second place's four lines with three signatures and a second paper out of a house with two signatures, and the first of them was going up the road with her.

Odile Vray said that the papers could not go in the rack and did not go on the long table.

**"Those go up the road with her and they go nowhere in here,"** she said. **"And that is not because they are not worth entering, it is because a paper that gets entered in this holding stops being the second place's paper and starts being this holding's record of what the second place said, and I will not do that to four lines and three signatures."**

Harlan Vetch said that the four sheets had now been untouched by six separate things in three days, and that was the most anybody had ever managed in a week.

He turned and looked along the length of the shelf before he said the rest of it.

The yard went on working. At dusk the three ages went out of the one room first, out loud in the doorway, in the order of a man who has put the newest thing at the end for nine years and has decided to stop.

**"The charter is two hundred and sixty-nine days, and that is the oldest of the three and the nearest of the three to stopping being a plan. Then Silling's ruled line at three hundred and fifty-seven, with nothing on it and nothing above the word. Then the register form at three hundred and sixty-three days. That is the order they come out in tonight, oldest first, and they will come out in the other order tomorrow, because I have nine years of habit and it is not going to be beaten by a mood."**

He turned round in the doorway.

**"And the rest in one breath, because a stone on a wall reached a round figure today and somebody is going to go through this holding hunting for round things. Thirty-nine blanks, rule underneath open at the thirty-ninth time, on a morning the book came round, a stone came round and a neighbour came down a road saying no. Fifteen in the log. Eleven on the barrow. Seven sessions. Six rows. Fifty-three and fifty-three on the two lower books and never the same list once."**

He left the rack standing the way he found it and took his own book out under his arm.

**"And the old man at the end of the north row stands at twenty-nine fetchings, unfetched, and nothing was put to him at any hour of this day, and a young woman carried two papers up the road past his row this morning without stopping and I noticed that and I have no reason to tell anybody except that I have been keeping a list for a fortnight."**

Then he pulled the door to, and left it where he had left every other door this month.

The far board stood at nine hundred with nothing on the end of it, and the near board stood at eight hundred and fifty-three, and seven bodies were going to be on a sill somewhere tomorrow instead of nine, and a girl from the second place was walking up a road with four lines and three signatures in her bundle, and somewhere above this holding four places were counting what it was worth to them and had not finished, and the yard went on working.
