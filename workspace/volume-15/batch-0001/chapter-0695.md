# Chapter 695

## The Eighth Of The Nineteenth, And A Number On A Stone That Has Not Been On That Stone Before, Read Out In The Same Voice As Every Other Morning

The fourth place's man read the launder at the seventh hour in the same voice he has used all week and did not change his tone anywhere in it.

**"Four hundred and fifty-eighth of the run, at one thousand and three hundred and one hundredweight, which is eight on because this morning is an even morning."**

He wrote it on the copy sheet with the pencil behind his ear and then read the rest of it off the stone.

**"A window of four hundred and twenty-five, made of a rising half on two hundred and eighteen and a falling half on two hundred and seven. The upper half took its point in the night and the lower half has stood since Wednesday morning."**

He wiped under the line and put his hand flat on the stone above it.

**"Nine mornings I have read that stone this week and that is the ninth, and I have read the weight out loud first every morning this week because I worked out on the second day which end of the sentence a man would otherwise leave out."**

Somebody at the gate said the number twice, out loud, and got it wrong the second time.

**"Say it again,"** the fourth place's man said, **"and say it the way I said it, and if you cannot say it the way I said it then do not say it at all this morning."**

The somebody at the gate said it again and got it right the second time and went back to the boards.

Nobody in that yard said anything else about the weight. Nobody in that yard asked what it meant, and nobody was told, and there was no moment in that morning when anybody looked at anybody else because of it.

Hesta Lyle was at the long sheet at about the eighth hour with the sheet squared and her hand on the head of it, and the fourth place's man came and read the weight out loud within thirty feet of where she was standing, and she went on with the three figures under the top of it and did not turn round.

**"Four hundred and fifty-six at the head,"** she said. **"Four hundred and fifty-fifth under it and four hundred and fifty-seventh over it. Three and a head of four hundred, and the fourth is not due."**

The lady who keeps that sheet then said the thing she had said the day before and had said she would say once and then leave alone, which was that there is a rule in a book four days north about that channel, and that a rule in a book is worth nothing and worth everything depending on whether anybody walks down the lane and looks at the stone, and she said it in the same voice as the three figures and without looking up the middle road at all.

Then she said a third thing, which was about the run and not about the rule.

**"The run has not started again. The days turned a week ago and it did not start again then either. It has gone up and down all week on whatever it has been doing since it began and it is doing the same thing it was doing on Tuesday and I can see that from here without going near it."**

Then she stopped talking about it and went inside.

The sill crew were out at the fifth hour and were on the stones before Marek came down the north end, and the fifth hour of the wall was done by four people instead of five, because one of the five had gone up the road to the second place at the fourth hour and had not come back.

Odile Vray did the chain herself and did the sluice herself and put one of the women from the second place on the sill face in the morning and moved her to the sluice at about the seventh hour and said out loud, without being asked and without stopping, what the arithmetic of that was.

**"Four bodies and two jobs this morning, so it is two and one and me and my chain. On the day she comes back it is five bodies and it goes back to two and two and me, and it goes back without anybody deciding that it goes back, because it goes back when the body comes."**

Marek was given the chain at about the eighth hour and carried the bucket for an hour and ten minutes and did not go near the sluice pin and did not go near the gauge board.

At the gauge board the woman on the sluice read it out to him because he was standing in front of it.

**"It is at the notch and it has been at the notch all week and it moved a hair on Thursday and came back by the afternoon. I am telling you and you can write it down in words, and I am not going to stand here and read a number off a stick for anybody's convenience."**

He wrote it in words on his own sheet.

Harlan Vetch did the wall at about the tenth hour and had the hod down for a shorter time than usual because a mason from the third place had come up the road and was waiting at the gate and was allowed to stand at the gate and watch him, and did not come past it.

**"Eight hundred and forty-two at the gate end and eight hundred and eighty-nine at this end, forty-seven of masonry, and the joint at the back of the tap house came away wet again this morning. That is a morning's work and I have not kept a figure on it and I am not going to start, and a man from the third place can stand at that gate all day and he will not hear a number from me about it."**

He set the hod down and went and got a drink of water for the man from the third place, who had been standing there three hours, and brought it back out to the gate and did not take it inside.

Then the mason asked him a question about the wall, and Harlan answered it, and it was about the top eleven courses and not about the forty-seven, and the two men were still talking about courses at the end of the afternoon.

Kellan Rusk came out at the middle of the day and read the three ages off the rack with the door of the one room open behind him and the yard able to hear the whole of it.

**"Register form three hundred and fifty-two days. Charter two hundred and fifty-eight. And in the second ruled line in Silling's own book there is three hundred and forty-six and not a word on it anywhere, and the space above that word is clean, and I have said it ten mornings running and I am not going to change my order because a mason has a visitor at that gate."**

He shut the book on his thumb.

**"And on that long table the nearest of those four sheets has been lying a hundred and fifty-five days, which is the fifth figure I have read out this week and the only one of the five that nobody in this holding wrote."**

Then the thing that changed the day happened at about the third hour, in the middle of the yard, and it was that Marek said he would not write the terms.

The girl from the second place had been at the gate for eight days with her bundle under her arm and she had carried two papers up that road and had not asked this holding for anything, and on this afternoon she asked.

**"Is there going to be something written down before the morning comes that says what this holding will do? Not the five on the sheet. Something with our hours in it."**

Marek did not say no for about ten seconds, and then he said something that made two people at the boards look up.

**"No, and I want to be exact about why, because you have carried two things up that road for me and I am not going to give you a short answer. I can write a schedule. I can write a set of terms about what the network has to do and when. Both of those are a list of obligations and I am the right man to write them because I know what the things are."**

He put his hands flat on the middle table.

**"I am not the right man to write this. Every hour on that wall was put up by a mason or read out by a woman holding a gauge or counted out loud by a man at a stone, and if the engineer of record drafts the terms then in a season's time this yard will have handed its own work to a man from an office and the man from the office will not be the one who did it, and that will be true by the third season and nobody will be able to remember when it happened."**

**"Then who writes them?"** she said.

**"I do not know,"** Marek said. **"And I am not going to appoint anybody in a yard at the third hour on a Thursday, because a man who is appointed in a yard is a man who cannot be argued with in a yard afterwards."**

Nia Vale came along the wall at about the fifth hour and did the gate line and the two cut figures and then stood at the long table for a while with her cloth doubled and did not go near any of the four.

**"Thirty-one paid on that line, and not one load has ever been let out of it in thirty-one or in thirty or in anything at all. Between the two figures cut in that wall there is forty-seven of masonry and nothing else, and this holding's book has no column cut under any of the four sheets, and not one of those four has been entered or refused, and the nearest of them has been lying on that table a hundred and fifty-five days."**

She came to the end of the table and looked at the shape and then at the wall with the four hours on it.

**"The second place is without water and has been since a reach gave it away and said so out loud at a gate post, and nobody has found a way round it and nobody is going to find one in the three weeks left of this month. I am not going to say it once a week as though saying it were a way of doing something about it, and I am saying it today because a sheet with four hours on it went up on that wall this morning and every hour on it is an hour somebody is standing here."**

She picked the cloth up off the wood.

**"There is going to be a paper about hours and it is not going to be written by a man who was not here at the fifth hour, and I have not got anything else to say about it and I am not going to say it twice."**

Harlan Vetch was asked at about the sixth hour whether the mason from the third place might be allowed to go to the sill.

He said that the sill was five women's work and that a man from a third place standing on it would be in the way, and that when the mason from the third place came back in a season's time and knew the fifth hour, he could be asked.

The mason from the third place heard all of that from the gate and nodded and went back up the road at about the seventh hour, and the boy went with him, and nobody in that yard asked him to stay.

Soren Rill walked the eleven acres at dusk with his coat buttoned over a sheet with two columns on it and did not go near the ring.

**"Nothing went on the ring, nobody went up the ladder, and the thing on the low board has no day and no name and has not been picked up and has not been taken back. And nobody in this yard has been asked to choose anything today, and I am including the man who came up that road and went back down it."**

**"And there is a piece of work in this yard this evening that belongs to somebody, and the man who is the most qualified to do it has just told the yard he will not do it, and I have not got a better answer than he has and I am not going to pretend I have."**

Kellan Rusk turned the rack key at dusk, with the door of the one room open about a foot, and said what he had to say from inside it.

**"Thirty-nine blanks and the second rule open and empty at the thirty-ninth time. Nothing is entered in this holding's book on a Thursday. The two other books are at fifty-three and fifty-three and they have never been the same list and neither of them took anything off that road this morning. Eleven journeys and no twelfth. Fifteen lines and a clean space above the fifteenth."**

He shut the rack and then stood with his hand on the door.

**"And for the old man at the end of the north row, twenty-nine fetchings, unfetched, with nothing put to him at any hour of any day this month. That is eleven mornings. I have said it eleven times, and I have not once had anybody ask me why I keep saying it, and if anybody asks me why I keep saying it I am going to have to think about it."**

He shut the rack and put his hand on the door.

A man of about fifty was still inside the doorway of the one room at dusk with his hand on the frame where he has put it every evening since the middle of the month, and the sill crew had gone up the road, and a mason from the third place had gone down it, and a stone at the north end of a holding of four hundred people carried a weight that had not been on it before, and nobody in that yard had said a word about what it meant, and the yard went on working.
