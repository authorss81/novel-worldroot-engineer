# Chapter 691

## The Fourth Of The Nineteenth, And A Morning Of Work Done By Five People Who Are Not Being Watched, And A Gauge Board Read Out Loud By The Woman Holding It

The sill crew was on the stones before the frost was off them and Marek was not in the yard when they started, and nobody had told them when to start.

Odile Vray had the work cut into four by herself while the two lamps were still burning. Two on the sill face, two on the sluice, and herself on the chain with the bucket, and she wrote the four on the back of a seed sack with a piece of charcoal because it was the only thing to hand and because she had already decided that if the hours were not written down anywhere then the hours would be a thing somebody could later have an opinion about.

The gauge board was propped where it has been propped for four years and the float was at the notch and the notch was wet, and Marek came down the north end at about the seventh hour and stood in front of it for a while with his hands behind his back.

Odile Vray told him what it said.

**"It is at the notch and it has been at the notch since before you got here, and it went down a hair on Tuesday and came back on Wednesday and it is not telling you anything you could get a book out of. I have been reading it every morning this week for five of us and I am telling you what it says because you are standing in front of it and I would say the same to any man in this yard."**

He wrote that down on his own sheet, the words and not the figure, and he did not ask her a second time and he did not ask somebody else in front of her.

Then she went back to the chain and he was given a bucket, which is what a man who is not giving orders is given.

For two hours and a half the only people in that part of the yard were five women from the second place and a man carrying a bucket, and none of them was watched by anybody and none of them was waiting for the engineer of record to have an opinion.

The arithmetic was done out loud all morning without anybody deciding to do it. Sluice pin out and sluice pin home and count of forty on the pin. Plank off and the new one in and the wedges four and the wedges two. Bucket chain dry and oiled, which took longer than the plank and which nobody had written down anywhere before. Sill face scraped to sound, which is the whole of it and which takes an hour and cannot be hurried by anybody.

Then the ladle out and the top stones lifted and the second course bedded and the second course checked by the woman on the sill face with her thumb along the joint, and the woman on the sluice calling the water depth across to her in a flat voice the whole time.

**"Two inches,"** she called. **"One and a half. Two. Down to an inch, hold. Up. Two."**

Nobody shouted it. Nobody wrote it down. It went on like that for forty minutes and it was the whole of the morning's work and it is not in any book in this holding.

At about the tenth hour Odile Vray turned the seed sack round and showed it to Marek, and it had four lines on it and a fifth line with the hours against it, and the fifth line had a gap in it where a person was to write when they arrived.

**"There. Five names and five hours and a space to say what they actually turned up at, because a crew that is not watched is a crew that is late and says so."**

He looked at it for a while and did not take it.

**"That is yours,"** he said.

**"It is mine and I have had one in my own box for two years and it has never been in anybody else's, and I am not giving it to you and you are not having it off the table in a clerk's hand."**

Then she said the thing about the sheet on the middle table, and she said it to the wall rather than to him, because the wall is where she speaks when she is saying something she means.

**"I am not going to put a name at the foot of that list, and I am not going to ask anybody to. I have been out on that sill before the light came up this week and the week before, and there is not one hour of it in that list and not one name of the five of us on it, and that is not a complaint and I am not going to make it one to a man who is holding my bucket."**

She took the bucket off him and set it down.

**"I will be bound by it the day it says the fifth hour. Not before, and not because anybody in this yard is right about anything. My crew can be held to an hour and not to a feeling, and there is a difference between those two things and it is the only difference I have ever needed anybody to write down."**

Then Harlan Vetch came down off the north end with the hod and did four hours of the wall by himself while the crew was on the sill, and he did not stop to talk to anybody about the wall or about the shape on the middle table.

He said four things to the yard at the end of it, and the fourth one was about the barrow.

**"Where the wall begins there is eight hundred and thirty-eight cut in it, and where I am standing there is eight hundred and eighty-five, and the whole of the difference between the two is forty-seven of masonry. I have laid eleven courses this week at the top and taken up four at the bottom where the frost got under them. Near to me there is the mark inside the tool house: four inches, forking twice, and no second mark in four years, and the four accounts this holding has of that arm are four and nobody has added a fifth this morning by watching me work."**

He set the hod down.

**"And that barrow has eleven journeys marked against it and there is not a twelfth marked on it, and eleven is a floor and not a number anybody in this place is trying to pass, and I have seen two men this month look at a barrow as though eleven were a challenge."**

The fourth place's man came down at about the second hour of the afternoon and read the launder with the whole yard behind him, because the crew had come in and washed and were standing at the boards, and he read it the way he read it when there was a crowd, which is to say that he read the whole and the window and then the halves in the middle of it and did not care.

**"One thousand and two hundred and ninety-five hundredweight, the four hundred and fifty-fourth of the run, eight on because this morning is an even morning. Window four hundred and twenty-one, rising half two hundred and sixteen, falling half two hundred and five. The upper half moved in the night and the lower one has not moved since Saturday and I have stopped apologising for saying them in that order."**

He wiped under the line and then wiped the stone above it where nothing was written.

**"I have been asked three times this week by people who are not in this holding whether I add the two halves together. I have told all three of them no and I will tell this yard the same thing for nothing. They are two halves of a window and not two quantities of anything."**

Kellan Rusk had the book open on his knee under the eaves at about the third hour and read the three ages out in the order of the days they were made rather than the order of the shelf, because it was the third hour and he was bored of the shelf.

**"The register form is three hundred and forty-eight days old and it was made before the charter, and the charter is two hundred and fifty-four days old, and the ruled line in Silling's own book is three hundred and forty-two days and there is nothing on it. Three things, and if you want the truth about the three of them, the youngest of the three figures is the only one of them that has been written on."**

He shut the book on his thumb and did not put it back on the shelf until the fourth hour.

Hesta Lyle came out of the one room about the fourth hour with the long sheet against her chest and read the head of it and the two limbs under it out to Odile Vray, who had not asked.

**"Four hundred and fifty-two at the top, four hundred and fifty-first under it and four hundred and fifty-third over it. That is a sheet with three figures on it and it does not know about your sill and it does not know about your hours and it has never once been asked to."**

She looked at the seed sack on the boards with its back to the wall.

**"I have kept that sheet for four years because it is the only piece of paper in this holding that anybody has ever been frightened of getting wrong, and I have got it wrong twice, and both times it was on a day when the top figure did not move."**

Nia Vale did the wall at about the fifth hour when the light was off it.

**"Thirty-one paid on that line, and nothing has ever been let out of it in thirty-one or in thirty or in anything. Forty-seven of masonry is the whole of the difference between the two figures cut in that wall. The drawer behind this end is shut, the key is on its nail, and it has been shut every hour of every day this week and it will be shut at this hour tonight."**

She came to the long table and stood at the head of it.

**"Four sheets and not five and not one of the four entered and not one refused and no column cut under any of them. The nearest is a hundred and fifty-one days old. And on the middle table there is a fifth piece of paper which is not one of those four and is not going to be one of them, and it has five lines on it and a space at the foot of it, and that space is the same size as the space at the foot of the sheet that came down the lane, and I would like the people who ruled those two spaces to have a talk to each other one day."**

Soren Rill came in at about the sixth hour, and the crew were on the boards eating, and he went along the wall and then along the inside edge of the eleven acres and did not go near the ring and did not go near the ladder.

**"Nobody has been on the ladder. Nothing on the low board has a day on it or a name at the foot of it and nothing has been taken back. The ring took no boot and no tape and no price this morning, and there were five women standing within forty feet of it all morning working and not one of them put a foot in it and not one of them asked what it was for."**

He stood at the middle of the eleven acres.

**"And nobody in this yard has been asked to choose anything today. I want that said by somebody who has spent a morning watching a crew do work and not once been asked which of the five things they should do first."**

Then the boy came up the middle road from the second place at about the sixth hour and asked at the gate for the mason, and the message this time was that a household with two signatures at the foot of it wanted to know whether the sill would be on the list, and Odile Vray answered it herself from the boards with her mouth full, and she did not answer it politely and she did not soften it.

**"Tell them the sill is on the list and the hour is not, and tell them I will write the hour on my own sack and not on their paper, and tell them the second place is without water and has been since the reach gave it away and that there is no way round that which anybody in this yard has found."**

At dusk, Kellan Rusk said it from inside the one room.

**"Thirty-nine blanks and the second rule open and empty at the thirty-ninth time. A Sunday enters none of the seven sessions. The two other books are at fifty-three apiece and have not taken a seed sack off the boards. Eleven journeys and no twelfth, and a use log at fifteen lines with a clean space above the fifteenth, and the mark on that arm is four inches and forks twice and has not been measured by anybody today except by me, from the doorway, out loud, where he could hear me."**

He shut the rack.

**"There is one more standing and it is the one I say last because it is the one that costs me something to say every morning. Twenty-nine fetchings, not fetched, and nothing put to him at any hour of a Sunday. That is nine mornings. It will be ten tomorrow unless a man from an office comes down that lane again."**

The shape lay on the middle table at dusk with the space at the foot of it still empty, and the seed sack lay on the boards at the north end with five names and five hours on it and a gap for the truth, and a man of about fifty was where he has been every evening since the middle of the month, with his hand on a door frame and nobody going past him, and the yard went on working.
