# Chapter 687

## The Thirtieth Of The Eighteenth, And Five People Out On A Sill Before The Light, And A Sheet With A Moment On It

The sill crew was out before the light came up. Five of them from the second place, and Odile Vray went out with them at about the fifth hour in a hard frost with two lamps and a coil of rope, and they were standing on the sill at the north end before the gate was open and before there was anybody else in the yard at all.

Marek was in the yard at the sixth hour with a rolled sheet under his arm and he did not unroll it until after the crew had gone out to their work, and he did not say the word order to anybody before they went or after.

The fourth place's man read the launder at about the seventh hour because he has read it every morning since Tuesday and nobody has told him to stop.

**"Four hundred and fiftieth of the run,"** he said, **"and it came down at one thousand and two hundred and eighty-nine hundredweight, which is eight on, because a Wednesday is an even morning."**

He wrote it on his own copy sheet with the pencil he keeps behind his ear and did not look at the sheet while he was saying it.

**"Upper half two hundred and fourteen. Lower half two hundred and three, and that one has not moved since Monday. I am saying them in that order on purpose, because a man who has had a rising half in his mouth for two days starts writing it down as though it were the one that goes at night. The upper half took a point in the night, because this is an even morning and the upper half is the one that moves on an even morning. Window four hundred and seventeen."**

He put the pencil behind his ear again and left his hand flat on the stone.

**"That is the whole of it, and I have not added the two halves together and I am not going to, and anybody in this yard who wants them added can go and add them on their own paper."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the north end about half an hour later with the hod still on his shoulder and stood at the wall at the tool house end and ran his thumb along the stone without wiping it.

**"Eight hundred and eighty-one,"** he said, **"and it is cut the same depth it was cut the day the wall went up. Nearer the gate it says eight hundred and thirty-four. Forty-seven of masonry is the whole of the difference between the two, and it has been the whole of the difference since the wall went up and it will be the whole of the difference when I am under it."**

He set the hod down and looked along the wall away from the gate.

**"And I want to say a thing about that far end, and I am going to say it once and I am not going to say it again this year. The far end of a thing is held by two people and neither of them is me, and no instrument anywhere in four counties says whether it is on or off."**

Nobody answered him. Nobody asked him which far end he meant, and nobody in that yard said it was a hard thing to have said on a frost morning at the tool house end of a wall, and nobody thanked him for saying it.

**"I laid the last eleven courses at that end twice and took them up twice,"** he said, **"and the two people who hold the far end of it are not me and are not this holding, and I found that out by taking the stones up and not by being told it, and I would not have found it out at all if I had left them down."**

Then Marek unrolled the sheet on the middle table. It was the same print as the sheet that had come down the lane four mornings ago and it carried the same seal, and it was not the same paper, and it did not carry a space ruled at the foot of it for a name.

He did not read it out. He put it flat on the wood with his hand on the top edge and looked at the yard rather than at the paper.

**"That came in with the second load of seed and the sacks of lime at the sixth hour, and it is correct. I have gone down it line by line twice and there is nothing in it I can put a finger on, and I am not going to invent something. I am also not going to have anybody in this yard say that we did not like it. It is not a lie. It is a schedule."**

He turned it round so that the yard could read it upside down from where they stood.

**"It fixes one moment and that is all it fixes. The moment is the last working morning the office has in front of it, and I am not going to give that morning a name of my own because the moment I give it a name it becomes a thing I could be argued with. On that morning this holding says in writing what it will maintain and what it will not, and the office takes the writing and does whatever it does with it. That moment is not mine. It was fixed by somebody four days north of here who is not coming down this lane to argue it and who does not have to."**

**"Can it be moved?"** somebody said from the gate.

**"No,"** Marek said.

**"And is that a date?"** Harlan Vetch said.

**"It is a moment,"** Marek said, **"and a moment is not a thing anybody in this yard can argue with, and that is the whole reason I brought it down instead of leaving it up there. If it had been a date I would have fought it and I would have lost, and this yard would have spent a morning losing. It is not a date and I have nothing to fight."**

He went to the sill end and stood behind the crew's lamp and watched them work for forty minutes without saying anything to any of them, and then he came back and said the second thing.

**"There are four things I have lost, there were four yesterday morning, and there are four now,"** he said, **"and I am going to say one sentence about that and then I am going to go and look at a gauge."**

Nobody in that yard asked him which four.

**"The comfort is not standing, the father is not alive in the wood, and the day does not come back."**

Nobody said anything about it, nobody said it was a hard thing to say, and nobody in that yard thanked him for it, and two of the five people on the sill carried on laying without turning round.

Nia Vale came along the wall at about the eighth hour with her cloth and did the gate line first, because the frost had put a bloom on the stone that she wanted off before the sun came on to it.

**"Paid at thirty-one,"** she said, **"and it has not been discharged in thirty-one or in thirty, and it has not been discharged in anything, and I am going to keep saying it in this yard until it is written down somewhere that is not this yard."**

She wiped the two figures on the wall in turn.

**"Nearest the gate, where you are standing, eight hundred and thirty-four. Walk away from me to the tool house and it is eight hundred and eighty-one. The drawer behind this end is shut, it was shut every hour yesterday and it will be shut every hour today, and the key is on one nail in the one room doorway and it goes on that nail tonight."**

She came down to the long table and stood over it with the cloth doubled in both hands.

**"Four sheets on this table, and this holding's book has no column under any one of them. The nearest has been lying there a hundred and forty-seven days with nine inches of clear board at either end of it. The second is shut on its fold. The third carries a name and a seal and nobody here can find anything wrong with it. And the fourth has a ruled space at its foot, and that space has been empty since the day the paper came."**

She wiped the boards around all four of them and did not touch the papers.

**"And there is a fifth piece of paper in this holding that is not one of those four. It is mine, it is in my own hand, it is folded in my coat, nobody else signs it and nobody has ever been asked to. It is not on this table and it is not going on this table."**

Kellan Rusk came out of the one room with the second book under his arm and read the three ages off the rack where they stood, in the order of the shelf, because the shelf was behind him and he had not turned it round.

**"The charter is two hundred and fifty days. The register form is three hundred and forty-four. And on a ruled line in a dead man's own book there is a figure that has grown by one a day for longer than that book has been on the shelf, and not one word has ever been written on it: three hundred and thirty-eight."**

He shut the book on his thumb, carried it back into the one room and put it on the rack in the order the shelf wanted and not the order he had read it in.

Hesta Lyle had the long sheet out on the long table and had squared it with the edge of the wood and had not opened it at the second fold.

**"Four hundred and forty-eight at the head of it,"** she said. **"Beneath the head, the four hundred and forty-seventh. Above the head, the four hundred and forty-ninth. Three figures, and not one of the three is either of the others."**

She put her hand flat on the top of the sheet.

**"It will have a third figure under it before the days turn over again and a fourth after that, and I am not going to read you the two of them together, because a yard that keeps figures straight is the only thing standing between this holding and a very bad afternoon."**

Soren Rill came in at about the ninth hour, went to the ladder against the tool house wall and stood at the bottom of it for a while without touching it.

**"Nobody has been on that ladder and nobody is standing under it,"** he said. **"There is a thing on the low board with no day on it that has not been picked up and has not gone back to anybody, and the two people it belongs to are both standing in this yard, and neither of them has a figure against their name in this holding or in an office four days north."**

He laid his hand flat on the low board itself, well clear of what was lying on it.

**"The ring of bare ground inside the eleven acres took no boot this morning and no tape and nothing said about what it would cost to put a thing in it. And nobody in this yard has been asked to choose anything today. I have been in this holding four years and I still have no answer to what I would do on a morning when somebody asked me to, and I have stopped expecting one."**

The girl from the second place stood at the gate with her bundle under her arm, her back to the frost, and watched the sill crew work, and had not asked this holding for anything since the middle of the month and did not ask it for anything now.

A boy came up the middle road at about the tenth hour from the second place on foot and asked at the gate for the mason, and gave him a message from a household with two signatures at the foot of it, and did not go past the gate.

Harlan Vetch read the message standing at the gate post and handed it back.

**"There are four households in that lane with two names at the foot of every one of them, and this holding has never had the four in a book, and I am not going to start a book at a gate on a Wednesday morning."**

Then the boy came back up the road at the middle of the day with the second place's own word in his own mouth, and Nia Vale heard it at the long table and put her cloth down on the wood.

**"They are asking whether the water comes back up that road this season,"** she said.

Nobody in that yard answered it.

**"The second place is without water, and it has been without it since a reach gave it away, and the reach cost it water and said so out loud at a gate post. Nobody has found a way round that and nobody is going to find one this month. It is not a thing that gets put right later. It is a city that has none now, and it will have none when the days turn over."**

She picked the cloth back up and went on along the wall.

At the second hour of the afternoon Nia Vale went out along the middle road as far as the gate and back with the gate shut behind her, and a door nine hundred yards off the lane went past the whole of the way and was looked at by nobody and read by nobody and offered to nobody, and she did not stop at it.

In the one room, Kellan Rusk shut the rack on the last of the afternoon and said what he had to say from inside the room rather than from the step.

**"Thirty-nine blanks cut in that board, and the rule underneath them standing open and empty at the thirty-ninth time. Six ruled rows on that shelf against what this holding does not know, and the shelf above the sixth is bare. Nothing goes into this holding's book on a Wednesday. The other two books are fifty-three apiece and have never once been the same list."**

He shut the rack again and stood with his back to the door of the one room.

**"The barrow carries eleven journeys marked against it and there is no twelfth marking. The use log stops at fifteen lines and the space above the fifteenth is clean. And for the man at the end of the north row there are twenty-nine fetchings, unfetched and unasked, and I have said that in this yard five mornings running and I am not going to stop now for a sheet off an office."**

He put the key on its nail in the doorway, which is where it stands every other hour of the day, and stood with his shoulder against the frame until the light came off the top of the wall.

He had said nothing at all since the seventh hour. His hand was still on the frame of the one room doorway at dusk, and the six weeks he asked to be noticed with went on being noticed with by everybody in that yard except himself.

At dusk the frost came off the wall in a long grey line, the sheet with the moment on it was still flat on the middle table where Marek had left it, and five people from the second place came off the sill and put their lamps on the boards and did not look at the sheet, and the yard went on working.
