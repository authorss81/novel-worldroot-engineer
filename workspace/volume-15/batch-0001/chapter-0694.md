# Chapter 694

## The Seventh Of The Nineteenth, And Two Figures In The Same Hundred Range Written Two Different Ways On The Same Board, And A Paper Come Back Up The Road With Two Answers On It And No Choice Between Them

Kellan Rusk went into the one room at the seventh hour with the chalk and stood in front of the board for longer than the writing takes.

**"One hundred and ninety-nine of three hundred and eighty-one, of a month nine months back,"** he said to the board and not to the yard, and then he stopped with the chalk still against it.

He went out and came back with the second book and a scrap of paper and put them on the sill outside and wrote two things on the scrap side by side and held it up to nobody in particular.

**"Look at these two and tell me whether they are the same size number."**

Nobody could see it from where they were standing and he did not read either of them out.

**"There. First one, one hundred and ninety-nine. Second one, a hundred and fifty-four. They are both in the same hundred, they are both on this yard's books, they are both days or both counts or both of something, and I have written the first one with one in front of the hundred and the second one with a in front of the hundred, and I have been doing that every morning this month without once thinking about it."**

He put the scrap down on the sill.

**"The first of those two is a figure a person reads out loud at a trough and it is written the way it is spoken, one hundred and, because if you said a hundred and ninety-nine over that trough every morning for four years it would come out wrong in a man's mouth. And the second one is a figure on a page and it is written a hundred and, because a hundred and fifty-four days is how long a piece of paper has been lying on a table and there is nobody reading it out loud and there is not going to be."**

He picked the scrap back up.

**"They are two different figures with two different houses and I have been keeping both houses for four years without knowing that is what I was doing. A man in an office reads both of these in the same column of the same book and thinks he has got a rule for this holding, and he has not, and he has got a rule for two figures that happen to sit under the same hundred on the same morning."**

He put the pair on the board where it goes, under the heading, and the heading said what it has said every morning and did not move by so much as a letter.

Then he came back out and said the rest of it in the yard.

**"Two figures on that wall and no voice, and that is what it is now and it is not going to be anything else. The numerator on that board is in the last morning of its own hundred. Tomorrow morning, or the morning after, it will go out of that hundred and it will be written like every other figure in this yard, and I am telling the yard that now so that nobody stands here in a week and says the writing changed its mind."**

Nobody thanked him for it and nobody asked him to keep it up, and he went back inside and shut the door and it stayed shut for an hour.

The paper from the third place came up the middle road at about the eleventh hour with the same boy carrying the bundle, and it went to Soren Rill and not to the engineer of record.

Soren Rill read it at the chalked wall and then read it out loud in the middle of the yard, and it was one sheet and it had two columns on it and the columns had both answers in them.

**"The first column is what I said to that man on Saturday at this wall, word for word, and the second column is what Harlan Vetch said to him at the tool house end, word for word. And there is no third column and there is no line under the two of them and there is nothing at the foot of it."**

He held it up so that the yard could see the two columns and the empty foot.

**"I asked the boy who wrote it whether the third place had chosen one of them and he said no, and he said it the way a boy says a thing when the grown man who sent him has told him exactly what to say and nothing else. The third place has written down that it was told two things and that it has not chosen, and it has sent it to us like that, and it has not put a mark against either column."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the north end and read his own words in the second column and did not say anything about them for about a minute.

**"That is my sentence in a boy's hand and I have not got anything to add to it and I am not going to try to add anything to it. A man does not get to improve on what he said at a wall."**

Somebody at the boards asked whether the two columns were going to be ruled under on the long table, and the answer was no, and nobody in that yard said who had given that answer.

Harlan Vetch said one more thing about it before he went back up.

**"It is not a coward's paper. I want that said about it. I have met paper like that four years ago and I know what it costs a household to send a thing up a road with two columns and no mark, and this holding knows what it costs to say no in writing and so do I, and I am not going to stand here and be glad about it."**

Hesta Lyle was at the long sheet at about the middle of the day with her hand on the head of it when the launder was read, and she waited until the fourth place's man had finished before she said anything at all.

**"Four hundred and fifty-five is the head of it. The four hundred and fifty-fourth sits under that, and the four hundred and fifty-sixth sits over it, and the fourth figure is not due and will not be entered early."**

Then she looked up the middle road at the stone.

**"And while that stone is being read every morning I am going to say a thing about it once, and then I am going to leave it alone. There is a rule in an office four days north, in a book that they still keep, that says that channel will never carry what it is carrying now. It has been in that book longer than I have been in this holding and nobody has struck it out and nobody has told me it was a guess when it was written."**

She took her hand off the sheet.

**"I am not saying the rule is wrong. I am saying it is a rule, it is written down, and that channel has been coming down that road every morning of my life going up and coming down going down. Somebody four days north has a book with a thing in it that this yard can prove wrong, and that is worth more to me than another row of small figures on this sheet, and I have not got anywhere to write it."**

The fourth place's man read the launder at about the third hour of the afternoon with the whole yard around the stone and gave it in the long form, and he read the ordinal first because there was a man from the third place's brother-in-law standing at the boards.

**"The four hundred and fifty-seventh of the run came down at one thousand and two hundred and ninety-three hundredweight, five off, because a Wednesday morning is an odd morning. Window four hundred and twenty-four. Falling half two hundred and seven, which took its point in the night, and rising half two hundred and seventeen, which has not moved since Tuesday."**

He put the pencil behind his ear and left his hand on the stone for a while.

**"That channel's own weight has been read off this stone every morning for eight mornings and I have got them all on my copy sheet in a column, and the highest of the eight is the one I read on Tuesday, and the lowest of the eight is the one I read on Thursday, and Friday's is neither of them, and it has not done the same thing twice this week."**

He looked along the wall at the office end of the middle road.

**"There is a book four days north that says a number and a number that I cannot change and that they have not got anybody to look at this stone for a week. I am not going to say the book is wrong and I am not going to walk down that lane to say it is right either."**

Kellan Rusk came out of the one room about the fourth hour with the book and read the three ages standing against the wall so that the light was on the page.

**"Charter two hundred and fifty-seven days. Register form three hundred and fifty-one. And then there is a figure in a dead man's book that has gone up by one a day since before the wall went up and has never had a word written against it: three hundred and forty-five, and it is the youngest of the three, and it is the only one of the three nothing has ever been done with."**

He shut the book and stood with his thumb in the middle of the cover.

**"And the nearest of the four sheets on that long table is a hundred and fifty-four days old this morning, and I have had two people ask me this week whether that figure is a count of days or an age of a paper, and it is both, and it is the only one of the five figures in this yard that is both."**

Nia Vale came along the wall at about the fifth hour and did the two cut figures and the gate line and did not touch the chalk.

**"Eight hundred and forty-one at the gate end, eight hundred and eighty-eight at the tool house end, forty-seven of masonry. Paid at thirty-one, not discharged in thirty-one or in thirty or in anything. The drawer behind this end is shut and the key has been on its nail since the sixth hour."**

She came to the middle table and looked at the shape and then at the wall.

**"Two sheets in this yard with a space ruled at the foot of them and nothing in either space, and one of them has five lines on it in the engineer's own hand and the other one is the fourth of the four on this table, which is the sheet a woman four days north put on it eight mornings ago at the ninth hour and that was refused in one breath, and a third sheet has come up that road with two columns on it and no space at the foot of it at all, and I have stopped being able to count the paper in this holding and I have been doing this for four years."**

Soren Rill came along the inside edge of the eleven acres at dusk with the third place's sheet folded in his coat, and did not go near the ring and did not go near the ladder and did not go near the low board.

**"A paper came up that road today with two columns and no mark and I have carried it into this yard in my coat and it is not going on the long table and it is not going in the rack and it is not going in either of the two other books, and that is three places it could have gone and it has gone to none of them."**

He stood with the coat buttoned over it.

**"And nobody in this yard has been asked to choose anything today. The third place has not chosen. The woman who sent the sheet has not chosen. I have not chosen and I am not going to choose, and the whole of what I have done this week is refuse to be the place where somebody else's two columns get turned into one."**

Then Kellan Rusk turned the rack key and said it from inside the room.

**"The second rule under the blanks stands open and empty at the thirty-ninth time. It has opened nothing today and it is not going to open because a boy came up a road with a sheet. Nothing is entered in this holding's book on a Wednesday, and the two other books stand at fifty-three and fifty-three, have never been the same list, and did not take that sheet either."**

He shut the rack.

**"Eleven journeys and no twelfth, and fifteen lines in the use log with a clean space above the fifteenth. And for the old man at the end of the north row: twenty-nine fetchings, unfetched, unasked at any hour today. I have said it twelve mornings running. I am going to say it on every morning until somebody in this holding tells me to stop, and nobody has, and I am not going to take the telling kindly."**

The two columns were on the middle table at dusk with nothing at the foot of them, and the board in the one room carried two figures under a heading that had not moved, and one hundred and ninety-nine was in its last hundred of its own, and the yard went on working.
