# Chapter 716

## The Twenty-Ninth Of The Nineteenth, And Four Terms Written In Four Different Hands On The Back Of A Clean Sheet By People Who Will Do The Work And Who Will Not Sign It, And A Schedule That Is Not An Order And Five Short Lines At The Foot Of It With Nothing In Any Of Them

Three men went out of this yard at the seventh hour with shovels and a barrow and a rope and came back at about the fifth hour of the afternoon, and it has happened on enough mornings this month to be a habit and it will go on happening until there is no silt in a branch four miles off.

The work in this yard was on the crossings that exist, and about nine people were on them at the same time, and nobody in this yard was asked to be on one.

The man who lays for three councils was putting the third course of the north sill back in for the fourth time in as many days, and he stopped about the eighth hour and said the thing he had come to say.

**"We want it written down. That is all. Not in that room and not on that long table and not with a name at the top of it. We want it written down in our own words and we want it to be four things and we want anybody who has not written one of them to be able to walk past it."**

He put his barrow down.

**"And I am not asking the man from the office to write it and I am not asking the engineer of record to write it, and if either of those two had offered this morning I would have taken my barrow and gone and laid stone somewhere else."**

So they wrote it at the second hour of the afternoon on the back of a clean sheet, at the middle table, and four people wrote one each and no two of them wrote in the same hand.

They put it in the order of the four terms that came down this lane a month ago with a seal on it and a place for a name at the foot, because every one of those four was buildable and a mason in this yard said so at the time, and because nobody in that yard had been given a better four than that.

> What this crew will and will not hold, said in four hands, at the middle table, in the second hour of the afternoon, and this paper belongs to nobody and is not a record of anybody and is not to be entered anywhere:
>
> **First, in the hand of a woman of about forty.** We will take the harvest that is written down for one season, and we will not go on treating it after the season, and we will not go about checking it, because it was never ours to check and it is not ours now.
>
> **Second, in the hand of a man of about thirty-four.** The ledger stays open. We will say what is in it. We will not make a report of it, and we will not be asked to, and a report is a copy with an opinion at the front of it.
>
> **Third, in the hand of a man who lays for three councils.** Any council can put in writing whatever it wants to put in writing. No council will open a branch again. That one is ours and not only theirs and it is the only one of the four that came here without an argument.
>
> **Fourth, in the hand of the woman who keeps the common key at a place four miles off.** Nothing passes to a man because of a day. What we hold here holds for a season and not for a person, and if that is the one that is going to be argued about then I would rather argue about it on this paper than anywhere else.
>
> **And under those four, five short lines for five hands, and nothing written on any of them.**

Nobody wrote a name on any of the five lines.

The mason from the third place came and looked at it and put his own hand flat on the sheet, across the fourth of the four and well clear of the five short lines at the foot of it, and left it there for a while.

**"I am not one of the five. I am not of this holding and this holding has not asked me anything and this yard has never once let me past that sill. And I am not going to write my name on a set of terms that says a branch does not open again when I have laid stone over three branches in my life and know exactly what happens to the ground under one."**

He took his hand off.

**"So I am going to say one thing about the third of them, because I am the only person standing here who has an opinion and it is not a favour and it is not a complaint. That third one is true. And the day somebody comes up that road with a petition in writing and no work behind it, the third one is going to be tested, and I would like to be on the sheet when it is."**

Marek brought the schedule down at about the third hour and put it on the middle table beside the sheet, face up, and it had four hours ruled under it and a rule at the foot in his own hand.

**"That is mine. Four hours and what goes in them and the order the mornings go in. It is not an order. There is nobody in it who has to do anything in it, and I have written it so that there is nobody, and I have looked at it twice this morning to make sure."**

A man at the gate asked him to put his name at the top of the four terms, because the paper was in the same place as the schedule.

**"No."**

Somebody asked him why not, and he gave the reason once and did not give it a second time.

**"Because the moment a man's name is at the top of a sheet, every one of the five people whose hands are on the other half of that sheet has been handed his work, and not one of them would say so and all five of them would have to carry it. I have watched that happen to a person three miles that way in this year and I am not going to do it in my own holding with my own hand on the paper."**

He put two fingers on the schedule and not on the terms.

**"And the schedule is here for anybody to argue with who wants to. The four are here for anybody to refuse to sign. Those are two different objects on one table and I would like this yard to keep them two different objects, because in a month one of them is going to be used as an argument against the other and they will not fit."**

Nia Vale said the four sheets on the long table and the ruled column in one sentence and then made herself say a second one.

**"Four sheets, none entered, none refused, no column cut under any of them. And on the sheet of terms in the same room there is a third column ruled and empty under a heading nobody has improved, and there are four ruled lines under two words with nothing on them, and neither of those has been filled in this month and neither of them is filled in by that sheet of paper on the middle table, which has four things on it and is not the same paper as either."**

She looked at it for a moment longer.

**"And it is not a fifth and it is not a sixth. It is four, and they were four before anybody in this yard wrote a word of them, and the four on the middle table are the same four and none of them is new."**

Odile Vray gave the whole of the day in one paragraph at about the sixth hour and gave it in the arithmetic of five women and three men, which is the only arithmetic she has ever used.

**"Seven bodies on the sill at the fifth hour. Three on the wall at the sixth. Two on the sluice and one on the boards at the seventh, and that is me. One man on the tenth hour who cannot be anywhere else at the tenth hour. Three men four miles each way with a barrow and a rope at the fourth, and that hour is not on the schedule and never has been. One boy with two cans at the second hour, and that is not free, and it has not been free since the spring."**

She put her hand down on the boards.

**"That is eleven people a day in this holding maintaining what exists, and every one of those eleven has said a no to somebody about something this month, and there is no schedule anywhere in this yard that can make any one of them say yes to anything, and I would like that written on the same paper as the four and I am not going to write it myself because I am one of the eleven."**

The bookkeeper brought the ages out at about the seventh hour and read them and then read them again in the opposite order and made no explanation the second time.

**"The charter is the oldest of the three at two hundred and seventy-nine days, and it is the one nearest being a plan instead of a form."**

He put a finger on the shelf and left it there.

**"The register form is at three hundred and seventy-three days and I have never once known what anybody used it for. And the one I keep on the end of the rack, which is Silling's own line, stands at three hundred and sixty-seven days with nothing written on it and nothing above the word."**

He shut the rack and then opened it again.

**"I have said that in this yard five times this month and I am going to stop saying it, because a thing said five times is a thing people stop hearing, and the day they stop hearing it is the day somebody puts something in it. The letter is a hundred and seventy-six days and nothing has been done to it at all this month."**

He closed the rack and stood with his palm flat on it.

**"Thirty-nine blanks on the board in there and the rule under them standing open and empty at the thirty-ninth time, and a book came round yesterday and did not open it, and four terms went onto a sheet this afternoon and did not open it, and I have been standing at this rack for nine years and it is the most reliable thing in this yard."**

The boy who carries water wrote the two figures on the north board at about the seventh hour and wrote them better than he wrote them on Sunday, and nobody in that yard said that he had written them better.

**"Two hundred and ten of four hundred and three."**

Harlan Vetch read the stones and the launder from forty feet of wall and gave them in the order of a man who is nearly done with a wall.

He came down off the wall and did the two stones first, which is not the order any of them expect.

**"The far one is nine hundred and ten. The near one is eight hundred and sixty-three. Forty-seven of masonry between them and it has been forty-seven since the wall went up."**

Then he did the channel.

**"One thousand and three hundred and twenty-six hundredweight, five down on yesterday, and it is the four hundred and seventy-ninth of the run."**

He wiped his hands and did not do the window at all until he had been asked for it twice.

**"The halves. Four hundred and forty-six of it, two hundred and eighteen under and two hundred and twenty-eight over, and the one under is the one that went in the night."**

He looked at the girl from the second place while he said it.

**"That is the third odd morning running I have had that the right way round and I am going to be asked about it by somebody tomorrow and I am going to say that I was taught it by a woman with a can, and I would like it written down now while she is standing here."**

Soren Rill gave the aggregate from the gate end and gave it last, which is what he has been doing all week.

**"The head of that long sheet is four hundred and seventy-seven. The four hundred and seventy-sixth is under the line and the four hundred and seventy-eighth is over it, and there is a fourth place on that sheet that has not been filled since the spring, and I have stopped looking at it."**

Then he stopped and looked at the sheet on the middle table for a while without touching it.

**"That is four things in four hands and five empty lines and I have been in this yard twenty years and I have watched about nine sheets go through it, and eight of them had a name at the top by the end of the week. I am not going to predict anything about this one. I am going to walk past it every morning and see."**

At dusk there were four hands on a sheet on the middle table and nothing on five lines at the foot of it, and a schedule beside it with four hours ruled under it and no name at the top of either, and the third place's mason had said one true thing about a branch and had not signed, and the seven were off the sill, and the yard went on working.
