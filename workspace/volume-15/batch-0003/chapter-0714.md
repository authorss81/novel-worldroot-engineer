# Chapter 714

## The Twenty-Seventh Of The Nineteenth, And Six O'Clock At The Gate, And Five People Saying Out Loud That They Will Not Have The Bridge, And What It Costs To Say So Said First And Not Softened Afterwards By Anybody

About forty people were at the gate at the sixth hour because a paper on a middle table had named that hour and that place, and nobody had said a word about it since the Saturday.

The five came up the middle road together. Ismay Rooke was in front, and he did not stop at the boards and he did not put a tape down.

He said it in the plainest voice he had and it took him under a minute.

**"We will not have the bridge. That is the whole of it and I have walked down that road to say it out loud in the place where it was asked for, and I am not going to soften it and I am not going to be asked to say it again."**

Nobody in that yard said anything.

**"The span at ninety-four feet at the crossing below the second place's branch. We have weighed it and we are not carrying it. There is no date on which we will carry it, and I want that said in front of all of you as plainly as the no, because a no without a date is a thing people hear as a maybe, and it is not a maybe."**

A man at the gate said so there were then no votes on it, and Ismay Rooke answered him without turning round.

**"There have never been any votes. There is no book and there is no clerk and there have never been any votes and there are not going to be any this morning. Five people agreed. I am not going to tell you how, because how we agreed is our business and not a thing to be examined at a gate."**

Then he named the price, in the same voice and in the same order as three people had named it in that yard on Sunday, and he did not lower his voice for any part of it.

**"It costs a neighbour. Three things on a piece of paper in your doorway and a fourth one crossed off it, and I have read them at my own table and they go back up that road on me. It costs a season. Nine cans and nine cans and four houses and a mile and a quarter, and the woman who keeps the pumps there has a sheet of her own with four thousand one hundred and one on it, and it is her paper and it is in her hand."**

**"And it costs her the other thing as well and she said that one herself on Sunday and I am not going to say it better than she said it. After this morning she is a woman who went and asked."**

Then he said what the five were going to do instead, and he said it as a list and not as an answer to a question.

**"Two things at two places and neither of them is a bridge. A haulage arrangement out of the fourth place on the days the road will take a barrow, which is not every day and I will not pretend it is. And a set of hours at the head of the branch with two people on it, which is what is already happening and which will go on happening whatever we say here."**

A man in the second row said that was nothing, and Ismay Rooke said it back to him.

**"It is less than a bridge. I have said so. I have not come down that road to tell you that two things at two places are worth a span, because they are not, and the woman who is going to carry the cans is going to carry the same number of cans this morning as she was going to carry at the fifth hour, and that is the honest size of what we are able to do."**

Then he looked at Marek Vale, who had been standing by the boards since the sixth hour with nothing in his hands.

**"And there is one thing I owe you and it is not the no, it is that you asked me a question on Saturday and I am going to answer it in front of these people rather than in a room. You did not say whether it was needed. You said whether it was wanted. That is a real question and nobody has answered it properly all month, including me."**

He waited.

Marek said it in one breath, and he did not begin with a preamble.

**"It is wanted and it is wanted by me. I have said that in your tool house and I am saying it at this gate with about forty people standing here, and I am not going to take it back and I am not going to have anybody tell me that wanting a thing is the same as a thing doing good."**

He put his hands behind his back.

**"The far bank of that crossing is the bank the gauge is on. If you do not build it, the woman at the second place who reads that gauge keeps reading it from the near bank, and she reads it about a foot out in a high week, and she will go on reading it about a foot out, because the near bank is the only bank there is. That is not a thing I can fix from here and I have known it since Saturday and I said it in that room and I am saying it here."**

Then he said the other thing, and he said it flat and without any of his own opinion attached to it.

**"And it would not survive a flood off that branch. A loaded barrow at full is the whole of what will ever go across it and a flood takes it. That is not the council's finding. That is mine, it is in my own hand, and it is on a sheet in this yard that the five of you came and read. You have said no to a bridge that I told you would not last, and I am not going to stand at this gate and let anybody say to you afterwards that you did not know."**

Somebody in the yard said that so the engineer of record agreed with the council, and Marek said no.

**"I said that it would not survive a flood and I said that the gauge stays wrong. Those are two facts and neither of them is a vote and I have not been given one and I am not going to take one."**

Ismay Rooke said the rest of it before he went.

**"And then the last of it, and I am going to say it once. Nobody in this yard has to agree with us. Nobody is going to be asked. No one's name goes on a paper and no one's name goes off one, and there is no field anywhere in this holding that anybody has to take because of a morning, and I would like all of you to notice that and I would like nobody to thank us for it, because it was not a favour to you, it is how we have always done it and there are four other councils out there that do it the other way."**

Then a man at the gate said that he wanted to thank the five, and he got two words out, and a woman at the front of that crowd said the thing he was not going to be able to finish.

**"No. And I am going to say why and then I am going to go and open my shutters, because I have kept this house on eleven gallons a day since before any of the five of you were born and there is a bucket in my kitchen that came from off that road."**

She turned round to the yard rather than to the five.

**"A straight answer is worth having and I am not going to say it was not. A straight answer is not water. Four miles off there is a branch with nothing in it and it is going to have nothing in it the morning after that one as well, and none of these five can put anything in it and they have said so themselves twice in this yard."**

Nobody thanked anybody and the five went down the middle road at about the seventh hour.

Auret Sill went first and Hanne Brack went with her, and about two hundred yards up that road the woman who keeps the pumps at the second place stopped and said something to the woman whose house is at the end of it, and the yard on this side of the wall could not hear what it was, and did not try.

Then the yard did the only thing it knew how to do with a morning.

The sill crew were on the stone at about a quarter past seven. They had been on the stone before the sixth hour and nobody had told them to keep going and nobody told them to stop, and they kept going, and there was no vote about it and no decision about it and nobody put a question to a single one of the seven.

Odile Vray came down off the boards and looked at the north end and said one thing about the gate and then went back to the sill.

**"About forty people standing at that gate at the sixth hour and none of them on the stone, and I am not going to read anything into that either way, and there are seven of us here and we were here at the fifth hour and we will be here at the seventh."**

Harlan Vetch was at the far end of the wall with a hod and the girl from the second place, who had walked up there at about the sixth hour without saying anything to anybody.

**"One thousand and three hundred and twenty-three hundredweight, and five off yesterday, and the four hundred and seventy-seventh of the run. And the stones are eight hundred and sixty-one at the gate end and nine hundred and eight up at the top of it, forty-seven of masonry, and that figure has not moved all week and it is not going to move on a morning like this one."**

He set a stone and then he gave the window, which nobody had asked him for.

**"And the window on the same wall is four hundred and forty-four, split two hundred and seventeen underneath and two hundred and twenty-seven over the top of it, and the figure that went in the night was the one underneath, because this is an odd morning and I have got that right three times in four days and I am not going to give anybody a chance to correct me about it."**

The girl from the second place wrote the two figures on the far stone in chalk while about forty people were still standing at the gate, and nobody was standing near her, and she wrote them in the same badly turned hand as the boy had used on the north board because she had asked him for the piece of chalk.

**"Two hundred and nine of four hundred and one,"** she said to the stone.

Then she said it again to the stone because nobody else was within forty feet of it.

Soren Rill gave the aggregate at the boards at about the eighth hour in the voice of a man reading somebody else's paper, because there had been two in the yard that morning and there was only one he would take.

**"Four hundred and seventy-five at the head, the four hundred and seventy-fourth under the line and the four hundred and seventy-sixth over it. It is the fourth day I have stood at that board today and it has not moved, and neither has anything else in this holding, and I have been in this yard twenty years and I have never once watched a gate at six in the morning."**

He turned his collar up against the wind off the fen.

**"And I am going to say the one thing I have been not saying for three weeks, and I said on Saturday that I would say it on a morning when this holding turned a figure of its own, and nobody here turned a figure this morning. But a gate did. So: there are two things I am not going to say in this yard and I am not going to tell him first, and I have said that in here four times now and I am going to keep saying it until one of us stops believing it."**

Kellan Rusk came out of the one room at about the ninth hour with the ages in his mouth because it is what he does at that hour, and said them in an order he had decided on in the room with the door shut.

**"Charter two hundred and seventy-seven days. Register form three hundred and seventy-one. The ruled line in Silling's own book, three hundred and sixty-five, with nothing on it. And the letter is a hundred and seventy-four days and it is the only one of the four that has not been touched by anything that has happened in this yard for a fortnight."**

He stood in the doorway.

**"Thirty-nine blanks on the board in here and the rule under them standing open and empty at the thirty-ninth time. Five people said no at that gate and a woman from the second place carried nine figures down a road on her own and none of it came into this room, and I have been keeping this book for four years and I would like it recorded that this morning it was the correct room for it to be a wrong place."**

Nia Vale read the compost line off the gate board on her way out and said it exactly as it stands and nothing around it.

**"That board still reads paid at thirty-one and the other half of that board is empty and has been empty as long as I have been alive, and I am going to say those two things the way I say everything else in this yard, which is once, and then I am going to do my work."**

She went out of the gate and stopped and came back.

**"Four sheets on that table, none entered, none refused, no column cut under any of them. I have been waiting for a morning all this month on which somebody would come and put one of those four in front of the other three and pick one, and I have stopped looking forward to it."**

Marek Vale spent the rest of that morning at the north end of the sill and was told what the water was doing by a woman who had a barrow in both hands, and he looked at her while she said it, and he understood her, and he did not pretend to have read it off anything.

At the far end of the north row a man who has not been fetched in twenty-nine fetchings was sitting outside his door in the sun with a bowl, and nobody said anything to him about the gate, and the fourth house was exactly where it had been all month.

There is no bridge on that crossing and there is not half a bridge and there are nine canfuls at the second hour at a place four miles off and four thousand one hundred and one cans behind them and two hours each way of a road a loaded barrow cannot take in the wet, and the seven on the sill laid to the seventh hour, and nobody in this holding thanked the five, and the yard went on working.
