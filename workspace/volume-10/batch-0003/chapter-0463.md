# Chapter 463

## A Day And Not A Figure

The second line of the rota came round at the second hour on the sixteenth of this month and was worked, and it was the outlet end and the two stones at the head of it, and the near stone had not moved since Tuesday morning, and the far one had turned about a quarter of an inch and had been put back by hand.

The clay round the near stone has not moved with the stone, and the clay round the far one has, and the difference between those two clays is about four inches of ground and it took two people from the second hour until the fifth to find out which one it was.

The near stone is the one a cart puts its near wheel against and it is about the size of a fist and it is grey and it is bedded in clay under about four inches of the yard surface. The far stone is the one a barrow goes past and nobody has ever known what it is for, and it is about the same size and it is a different grey, and the difference between the two greys is a different quarry and nobody in this holding has written down which.

The man of about thirty-one of Silling put the butt of a spade under the far one and lifted it, and the clay came up with it in two pieces and about a foot of it had to be taken off the underside and put back by hand, and the frost had got into that clay and it came out in lumps and had to be broken by hand before it went back.

**A stone that turns in a frost is doing what a stone does.** It is also doing what the man who set it did not, and nobody in this holding has been asked to say which of the two matters more, and the man who set it has not been asked either and is not in this county.

There was about a quarter of an inch of white on the ground at the sixth hour and it was gone off the yard by the eighth and still in the shadow at the north side of the lean-to wall at the ninth, and the wind had gone round to the south in the night and had dropped the smoke out of the corner by the gate for the first time since Sunday.

The water at the outlet was about a foot and a half at the sixth hour and about a foot and three inches at the seventh and it is doing that twice a day while the north end of the middle ditch is running full, and nobody in this holding has put a figure on how many times a day a thing does that.

There is no figure for the run of mornings on this page and the reason is on the page. The board is read at the seventh hour on alternate mornings, and this is not one of them, and the reason is that the man who reads the near side of it was up at the outlet end with the other three until the fifth hour and then went back to the low field gate and did not come into the yard again until the ninth, and the man who reads the far side did not read his own figure out alone and nobody in this holding thought to ask a fourth man to do it on a Wednesday.

The pair stands on the boards at **six hundred and ten days** and **six hundred and fifty-seven**, forty-seven apart, and the drawer was not opened.

The sixteenth went in at about the ninth hour and came back out of the other book, and the two of them were the same.

**Nine hundred and fifty-three hundredweight.**

Wednesday put eight on it, and this is the two hundred and twenty-sixth figure of a run in which no number has been written down twice since the morning it started, and the rise went onto the rises. A hundred and two against ninety-one is a hundred and ninety-three, and the two ends of that have never been put together with the middle of it in this building in four years.

Two hundred and twenty-four days is the standing of the four bodies of households and the clause beneath it reads the two hundred and twenty-third of the mornings out of two hundred and twenty-fifth, one short and one over, and that is a count of mornings and neither the launder nor the window.

At about the fourth hour a rider came up the coast road with a fold of paper inside his coat and he stopped at the gate and did not get down off the horse.

The paper was four days old when it came out of his coat. The well on that shelf is four hundred miles off and it takes four days to say anything at all about itself, and it has been read on the sixteenth of every month for eleven years, and it was read this morning at that far end by the man who occupies the first house on that shelf, and what came up the road was a day.

The man of about fifty took the paper standing in the yard, read it, and gave it back without a word, and the rider put it inside his coat again and went on up the coast road with a bag of wire for a man at Havergate, and he was not fed and did not expect to be and nobody in this holding has thought about that since.

**"Ten,"** the man of about fifty said, at the door of the one room, when the clerk asked him how many the second book had of them. **"It is ten this morning and it will be eleven by the time the light goes, and that is the whole of what is going to happen to that well today."**

The entry was made at about the fifth hour in the second book, in his own hand, with no figure against the day and no line ruled under it, and it was read back once and not read back twice, and the book went onto the shelf at the place it came from and not a foot to the left of it.

**"I know what that is going to be about,"** the clerk said, to nobody, in the yard, about ten minutes later. **"It is going to be about a man four hundred miles off who has nothing to write beside a day, and it is going to be the best line in this book, and there is not going to be a second one for about a month."** Nobody in that yard gave him a reason for saying it and nobody has asked him for one, and the count of times this holding has heard him give a reason out loud is three and is three.

The road it came on is the coast road and it is not the fen road and it is not the four-mile road, and it joins the four about a mile short of the gate at the top, and a man who has been in this holding for nine days has still not been told which of the three is which, and nobody has thought to tell him and he has not asked. Marek was in the one room at the fourth hour when the rider was at the gate and did not go out, because a man who has a road to be on goes on it.

It went into the entry for this holding as a day and not as a figure. There is no ordinal against it and there is no figure against it and there has never been one, and no unit has been supplied for it in eleven years, and nobody at that far end has been asked a question about it in any of the eleven.

The second book now carries eleven entries that are a day and not a figure, and ten of them were entered before this one, and the count of them went from ten to eleven this morning at about the fourth hour and it is eleven this evening and it is not to be read as a figure of anything.

A person who has read a well for eleven years and written down the day and nothing else is not withholding a number. There is nothing at that end of four hundred miles of road to put a number beside, and the not writing of one is not an omission and is not a refusal and does not become a question because somebody in this county found it worth a morning.

Nobody in this building has sent a question to that shelf in eleven years, and the count of questions this holding has put to anybody about it is nil and is nil, and it has been nil in every one of the eleven and it is not going to be repaired this month.

The day sheet with no date at the head of it is in a stack of about a foot and a half behind the fourth book and it is in the order it was used, and the one on the top of that stack is the one from the fourteenth and there is no reason in this building why the top of a stack of day sheets should be anything in particular.

The clerk of this holding wrote the day into the second book in his own hand at about the fifth hour and put no figure against it and drew no line under it, and read it back once and did not read it back twice, and the second book was shut and went back on the shelf at the place it came from.

The entry is a single line and the line is a day, and it is the eleventh line of that kind in that book, and the fourth book in this building has never carried one and the third book has never carried one, and the two of those are correct books and neither of them is short of anything.

Eleven is a count of entries and not a count of readings and not a count of mornings. The well on that shelf has been read on the sixteenth of every month for eleven years, and the count of readings and the count of entries are two different numbers about the same well, and nobody in this holding has made them agree.

The paper went back down the coast road inside the rider's coat at about the fifth hour and it is the only copy, and it is a copy of a day and not of a figure, and there is nothing at that far end to have a second copy of.

The two sheets are still on the long table where they were put back on Monday night, the first of them entered under a clause and the second of them entered as a sheet, and they are still about nine inches apart, and the two of them have been on that table every morning since the first of this month and neither of them has been folded, endorsed, copied or put in a drawer.

That argument did not move this morning and it was not argued this morning. It is still the case that one of the two is about a pattern and the other is about a term, and that the charter gave a piece of paper for the first of those and did not give one for the second, and that the two of them cannot be carried on one sheet, and the man of about fifty said that on Monday evening with the door shut and he has not said it again and nobody has asked him to.

Somebody in that room said this morning that if the two could not be joined then the one that was under a clause ought to be put first on the table and the one that was not ought to wait, and somebody else said that a thing which cannot be joined does not have an order either, and then the room stopped.

Nobody in this holding has been asked which of those two columns it wants, and the count of times either question has been put to anybody in this room is nil and is nil.

The woman who keeps the seed house came in at about the seventh hour with a sheet under her arm and put it face down on the long table without opening it and went out again, and nobody asked her what it was and nobody asked her to turn it over, and the sheet was still face down at the ninth hour.

She has had four books open since the sixth of this month and one of them closed on Friday, and a woman who puts a sheet face down in a room and leaves it there has decided something about the room, and the man of about fifty looked at it twice and did not ask and has not been asked.

On the low board at the far end of the one room there is a sheet lying face up, nine feet off the floor, with nothing filled in, nothing withdrawn, no date and no line for a name, refused on the nineteenth of a month now three months back. A day put in a book four hundred miles from this yard is not a reason for taking it off that board, and this holding has never used a reason like that in four years.

A thing anybody in four counties can see from the door is a thing anybody in four counties can ask about, and nobody has, and a day with no figure against it is the least useful thing that has entered this building in a fortnight, and the board is still where it is and the count of days it has been there is not a figure anybody keeps.

The clerk of this holding shut the second book at about the ninth hour and put it back at the place on the shelf it came from and not a foot to the left or the right of it, and nobody in this building has ever watched a man do that twice to find out whether it is a decision or a habit.

The second line was four people from the second hour until the fifth and one of the four was a man of about thirty-one who is not on any list in this building and has not been on one since before the first book was opened, and the three of them would have done it in three hours and were four because one of the four wanted to be there and was not asked to refuse.

A man who is given a job he does not need for the sake of the person who gave it is a man who will still be given the next one, and nobody in this holding has decided to give him the next one, and nobody in this holding has decided not to either.

The water at the outlet was about a foot and four inches at the ninth hour and the far stone was holding and the clay round it had gone back to where it was before the spade went under it, and the near stone has a quarter of an inch of daylight under it where there was none on Tuesday, and that is a thing the outlet does about twice a year and it will be looked at again by whoever is on the second line.




The second of the four lines in the man of about thirty-one of Silling's own book is a hundred and fourteen days old and has nothing on it, and he was at the outlet end from the second hour until the fifth and was not asked about the paper and was not in the room when it came.

The frost was on the yard again at the sixth hour and thinner than yesterday, and the water in the middle road had come up about an inch overnight and gone down about two of that by the eighth, and the trough at the top of the four-mile road is still dry and the grey in the bottom of it is still about two inches deep and nobody has filled it.

The man who came up the fen road with the list of ten was in the yard at about the eighth hour and watched the rider go up the coast road and did not say anything about it, and he has a slate in the lean-to with eight words on it and a decision about a ford written under them, and nobody in this holding has asked him what he thinks the coast road is for and he has not been asked since Saturday.

The man in the bad coat was at the tap on the north side of the yard at about the fourth hour when the rider was at the gate, and the two of them were about forty feet apart, and neither of them spoke and neither of them was introduced and the count of times two men in this yard have been introduced to one another is nil and is nil.

Nobody thanked anybody. The rider had gone up the coast road before the light went and the second book was shut with one day in it, and the two sheets are about nine inches apart on the long table, and the paper on the low board is face up, and the well on that shelf will not be read again until the sixteenth of next month.
