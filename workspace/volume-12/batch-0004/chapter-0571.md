# Chapter 571

## A Load Going Up A Road, And A Rule Cut The Same Length As The One Above It

There was no wind at all in the night and a hard white frost on everything by five, and the second line of the rota came round at the second hour, which is the outlet end and the two stones at the head of it, and the pack under the near stone came out dry and went back wet before seven.

The clerk of this holding wrote the launder figure at the first hour and read it back once, out loud, at the ninth, to the long wall, and nobody wrote it down a second time.

**One thousand and one hundred and fifteen hundredweight.**

It came up eight on Saturday, and the three hundred and thirty-fourth figure of a run that has never had the same one in it twice is what stands against the launder this morning.

The two halves at the wall were a hundred and fifty-six mornings one way and a hundred and forty-five the other, and the man who said the first of them added the second of them in his head and said three hundred and one, and the other man nodded and went on to the outlet end.

There is nothing this morning in the second book under the heading where the run of mornings goes, and the reason is on this page and no figure is on the page with it. Half of that run is a half number on a Saturday, and the clerk has left the space empty and the heading standing over it of a month nine months back.

At the third hour something came up the fen road, stopped on the steelyard for a quarter of an hour, and went back down the fen road the way it had come, and the plates under it took the load twice over and gave it up once.

It came off at five hundred and forty-seven hundredweight, which is eleven above the last one, and the two halves were made up at the boards twice and were not added, and there are two names written side by side in one hand on the ticket and that is the forty-fourth pair of them.

The second rule cut in the board in the one room was cut the same length as the first and by the same hand and it has come back empty on every one of the fifty-four mornings this return has come. Thirty-eight is what it has now been counted, and it is thirty-eight and not thirty-nine, because the count goes on the return and not on the day.

The clerk of this holding said it standing up at about the sixth hour with his finger beside the empty rule and not touching it, and he said the thing he says on every return because he has said it on fifty-four of them and he is not going to stop now.

**"Blank on every one of those mornings, and now a date at the head of it from Sunday, and a date standing on its own has not turned that rule into anything."**

He moved his finger off the empty rule and laid it flat on the board beside it.

The rule stayed ruled and empty and the date at the head of it went on being a date.

**"A rule that came back empty opens nothing, and I have said that at six o'clock on fifty-four mornings and I am not going to stop saying it."**

The man of about fifty was in the doorway through the whole of it and did not go and ask what the rule is for, and he has not asked since the twelfth and nobody in this holding expects him to.

He went up the four-mile road at the seventh hour with three sacks in the barrow and came down it at the eleventh, and the going up took him most of the morning because the track was frozen hard at the top and soft at the second bend.

That was the last load he took up that road.

Soren Rill was at the head of that road at the same time with a bar over his shoulder and came past him going down, and neither of them stopped and neither of them said one word to the other one, and both of them had a reason for that which neither of them gave.

**"It is not a sulk,"** the man of about fifty said to the barrow afterwards, at the bench. **"He has said his piece about that wall and about that trough and I have said mine, and neither of us is a body and neither of us can be asked anything, and a greeting on a track with a barrow in front of you is a conversation."**

He put both hands flat on the handles and left them there.

**"I have got a morning of track left in me and after this I have got none, and I have known that since the thirtieth of the month before, and I have not told anybody in this yard that I have not told anybody."**

The trough at the head of the four-mile road was down about a foot of the ten it holds and had been since the middle of last week, and it was not full at seven this morning and it was not full at nine.

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden went up to the head of that road at about the tenth hour with a can and stood at the trough and looked down into it and came back down without filling the can, and nobody in this yard asked her why, and she told them anyway at the tap, and gave the reason first.

**"There is a foot of dead air in that trough and I have been carrying water past it for a fortnight from a standpipe that is full, and I have not said that out loud in a yard before because I did not want it to be a complaint about a road."**

She set the empty can down on the step.

**"It is not a complaint about a road. There is no column in this holding for a trough at the head of a road and I have looked for one in four books, and the man who reads the schedule off that wall in the outer office is not anybody's idea of a man to take a complaint to and neither is anybody else I can think of."**

She left the can where it was and went and did her own work and nobody in the yard picked it up for her.

Sera Quill came down the fen road at about the seventh hour and read the letter on the long table standing up, and then read it again a second time from the other end of the table, and did not touch it.

**"Thirty-one days,"** she said, to the paper and not to the clerk. **"I am a witness to records and not a witness to anything else, and I can tell you that piece of paper has a heading, a date, a hand that has had it read out to it, and a body behind it that keeps a book, and that is four things that can be checked in four years by anybody who was not standing in this room."**

She squared her bag on her hip.

**"I am not going to tell anybody in four counties whether it should be answered, and I am not going to tell them whether it has been dealt with, and I would like both of those not-answers written down as mine and as mine only."**

Nobody at that table told her she was right and nobody asked her a second question about it and the letter did not move.

The sheet in this yard that carries the four bodies of households has three hundred and thirty-two days against them and the clause under it takes the three hundred and thirty-first of those mornings out of three hundred and thirty-third.

The near board says seven hundred and eighteen and the far board says seven hundred and sixty-five. Forty-seven days sit in the middle of that pair with nothing on either side of them, and the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden read both numbers out loud on her way to the tap and did not ask about the middle, and the drawer behind the near board stood shut at every hour of this morning.

Marek was in the one room from the fifth hour with the door open and had the return and the rule and the road and the letter through the gap in it, and came out at about the eleventh hour.

He stood at the top of the four-mile road with his sleeve down over his forearm and watched a man put an empty barrow away against the wall of the one room.

**"A man who has been carrying things up a road for nine years put that barrow away this morning, and the road did not end and the sacks are still at the top of it."**

He gave the reason first and printed no figure of his own anywhere in it.

**"I am not going to call that a loss and I am not going to call it a handover either, and I am not going to be the man who writes down what happens to a barrow in this holding."**

He turned the sleeve back a half inch and then turned it back again.

**"The only two words anybody has for what a barrow is to a man are the wrong two words, and one of them is a word that people use for a dead man, and I have watched a district use both of them about a man with a bar who was still standing in front of them."**

He turned back up the road without finishing anything and went into the one room and shut the door behind him, which he does not often do.

The man of about fifty was not thanked by anybody in this yard on this morning, and nobody in this holding has been thanked for anything at any hour of this fortnight.

Two hundred and twenty-eight days is how long that form has lain on the middle table, and the column at the head of it is headed nothing and there is nothing in the column, and it has not been filled and it has not been refused and it is not going to be read out a fifth time.

A hundred and thirty-four days ago a charter went through a door nine miles off, and nothing has happened to either of those two documents since.

It went through a door nine miles off a hundred and thirty-four days ago, and the four bodies of households in that room are three that still draw on a machine for stored light and one that has twice in a fortnight sent a person out to a tap.

Two hundred and twenty-two days is how long the second of the four ruled lines in the man of about thirty-one of Silling's own book has been standing there with nothing on it, and the man who wrote the line was the one who got the cart back onto the plates at the third hour and did not look at the board on his way out.

The man of about thirty-one of Silling was the one who put the cart back onto the plates at the third hour, and he did not go into the one room afterwards, and he was at the long wall at about the ninth hour when the launder figure was read out and he said his own number under his breath to nobody and it was not written down by anybody.

**"Seven hundred and twenty-nine and a bit,"** he said, and then corrected himself out loud without being asked, which he does. **"No. That is the wrong one. Seven hundred and eighteen is on that board and it is the near one and I said the other one and I have said the wrong one out loud in a yard and I am leaving it there."**

Nia Vale came through at about the eighth hour on her way out to the boundary and stopped at the long wall and read both boards and said one thing about the pair and then went on without saying anything else about them.

**"Those two numbers have been forty-seven apart every morning of my time in this district and there is nothing in the middle of the gap and no one has ever asked me what is in it."** She put her hand flat on the near board for a second. **"I have stopped expecting the answer and I want that on the page, because a woman who has stopped expecting an answer is a different thing from a woman who has not started asking."**

She went out through the first gate before anyone could ask her a second question.

Paid goes in front of twenty-seven on the compost line and it is not discharged in twenty-seven, and the interval that carries that figure lands once more inside this month and not on anybody's instruction.

The use log has fifteen lines in it and there is no sixteenth, and nobody in this yard opened it today. The barrow's journeys stand at eleven and eleven is a floor and the twelfth is not going to happen on that road.

Nine hundred yards off the seventh column of a door is at sixty-six and is not read on any morning this week. The man of about seventy is at twenty-nine fetchings and was not fetched today, and the count of questions put to him in this holding is nil at every one of its sites.

Seven sessions are entered in this holding's book. A load going up a road and a man putting a barrow away are not sessions and there was no eighth this morning. The requests column and the section-nine notes are both at fifty-three and the letter went into neither of them, and six rows are ruled for what this holding does not know about and a rule cut the same length as the one above it is not a seventh.

Sack on a barrow at the top of a four-mile road, and a rule in a board in one room that has been empty fifty-four times, and a woman who wrote down two not-answers and asked for nothing, and nobody thanked anybody.
