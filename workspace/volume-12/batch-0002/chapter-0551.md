# Chapter 551

## A Book With Fifteen Lines In It, And A Man Who Sat Down In Front Of It

The sun was on the outlet end by seven and there was no frost left in the ground anywhere in this holding, and the two stones at the head of the second line stood clear out of the ditch with about a foot of daylight under the lip of the lower one. The second line came round at the second hour and was worked, which is the outlet end and those two stones, and the pack under the near stone came out wet and went back wet, which is the third morning this week it has done that instead of the other thing.

The clerk of this holding wrote the line at the first hour and read it back at the ninth and did not read it twice.

**One thousand and eighty-five hundredweight.**

Sunday answered Friday with eight of its own. Eight figures back, and the three hundred and fourteenth figure of a run in which nothing has ever been entered twice is what the launder is carrying this morning.

One of the two men at the long wall read a hundred and forty-six mornings one way at seven and the other read him a hundred and thirty-five the other way, and the two of those together make two hundred and eighty-one.

On the sheet in this yard the four bodies of households stand at three hundred and twelve days, and the clause under that figure takes the three hundred and eleventh of those mornings out of three hundred and thirteenth, one under and one over.

The long wall carries two numbers this morning, six hundred and ninety-eight on the near board and seven hundred and forty-five on the far one, and forty-seven is the whole distance between them. The drawer behind the near board stood shut at every hour.

There is nothing in the second book this morning under the heading where the run of mornings goes, and the clerk of this holding left the space as he found it, and this page carries the reason and carries no figure. Half of that run is a half number on a Sunday. It was full on Friday morning and it will be full on Monday morning and it has gone that way since the day the run was begun, and a morning with an empty space under that heading is not a morning with something wrong in it.

The use log lives on the shelf under the bench by the pump house with a weight on it, and it has fifteen lines in it and a pen on a string beside it, and the man of about fifty is the only person in this holding who has ever written in it.

He took it off the shelf at about the tenth hour and put it on the bench and opened it at the last page and sat down on the bench opposite it. He was on that bench for about half an hour. The pen was on the bench in front of him the whole time and he did not pick it up, and four or five people in this yard saw him not pick it up and the rest of the yard was elsewhere.

Sera Quill came up the road from the second bus at about the ninth hour with the county book in her bag, and she had been in the yard about ten minutes when she crossed over to that bench and stood at the end of it, and she asked the question she had come to ask and she asked it to him and not to the book.

**"What is that book for, and who is it for, and has anybody ever told anybody in this holding that they were the one it was for."** She said it standing up. **"I am asking because I asked a different man a different question on Sunday about a different book and he did not know either, and I have been thinking about it all week, and I would like to know whether that is a thing about this holding or a thing about books."**

He did not answer it for a while, and then he said he did not know, in the plainest words he had, and he said he had been asked once, about nine years back, by a man who was in this holding before the last three of them, and that the man had said he did not know either and had not said anything else, and that was the whole of what there had ever been about it in this yard.

**"And I am not going to tell you that I have got fifteen lines in it and I know what they are for, because I do not."** He put his hand flat on the open page without touching the ink. **"I have put fourteen of these lines in myself and I know what each of them is about, and I have never once been told what the book is for, and I have never once asked, and that is on me and not on anybody else and I have had nine years to do it about."**

**"It is the second one of those questions anybody has asked anybody in this yard in three days,"** the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden said from the tap, and she did not come over to the bench. **"On Sunday a woman asked a clerk what the second rule on a board was for and he said he had said so eleven years ago and been told the same. This morning I have asked you what a book is for and you have said you have not asked in nine years. That is two and I have put neither of them in my own book."**

He turned the page over and shut it halfway and looked at it the way a man looks at a thing he has decided about.

**"Then you will not get a third out of me today and I am telling you now so that you can stop waiting for it."** He squared the two covers together. **"I am not going to write the sixteenth line of this book on a Sunday afternoon because a witness to records has stood at the end of my bench, and if I did that then the next one would be on a Tuesday and the one after that would be because somebody asked, and I would be a man who writes in here when people are stood at the end of him."**

Nobody in that yard said that was right and nobody said it was wrong, and the pen stayed on the bench and the book went back under the weight on the shelf at about the eleventh hour.

Nia Vale came in at about the fifth hour on her way out and stopped at the bench for about a minute, and she had not been in the yard since the middle of the morning before.

**"Two men looked at a piece of ground this week and neither of them wrote it down, and now there is a third thing in this yard that two people have asked about and nobody can answer."** She gave the reason before the thing and printed no figure for it. **"I have got nine of my own that I have not been past since Tuesday, and I am not going to stand at that bench and hand one of them over to make this morning tidy. A thing tidied is a thing somebody owns and I have not got any more owners than I had on Friday."**

From the sixth hour Marek had the one room to himself with the door standing open and his own two books in front of him, and every word that was said on the bench outside came in through the gap, and he got up once and did not go out.

**"A man sat on a bench for half an hour with a pen in front of him and did not pick it up, and the reason he gives is that he will not be a man who writes when somebody is stood at the end of him."** He said that to the open door and gave the reason before the thing, and he printed no figure for it, so that this morning consumed no number of his.

**"I have watched that sentence do damage twice. It is a good sentence and it took me nine years to be able to say anything like it, and I am not going to be the one who tells him he is wrong about it this morning when he has not paid for it yet."**

**"What I want said about today is that nobody asked him to write it. Not the woman and not the clerk and not me. Two people asked him a different question, and neither of the two was about the sixteenth line."**

He put his sleeve back down over his forearm. **"If a book ever gets a sixteenth line out of a question about something else, then that is how it will happen, and it will not feel like it is happening."**

Tova Reed came in from the seed house at about the seventh hour with the four counties' sheets and stood at the low board and said one thing about it and went on.

**"Three dates in my hand and a page in a pocket that a household gave itself, and a bench in this yard where a man is not writing a line."** She said that to the board and gave the reason before the thing. **"I have got nineteen households waiting for me to have an opinion about drainage and I am not going to have one about a book that nobody has been told what it is for, and if I did then in four years there would be a woman of about thirty-eight in four counties who had heard the seed front on the subject of a bench."**

The letter on the long table is eleven days old this morning, and nobody in this holding has been asked to answer it and no column has been ruled for it in any of the four books in this yard. It lies face up where it has lain, with about nine inches of bare board on either side of it between it and the two sheets about the two declinings, and it has not been moved off there and it has not been answered.

Nothing at the head of the column, nothing in it, nothing done about it: that is the register form on the middle table, and it has been in that condition for two hundred and eight days. It has not been filled and it has not been refused and it is not going to be read out a fifth time.

A hundred and fourteen days ago the charter was signed in a room nine miles off. In the man of about thirty-one of Silling's own book there are four ruled lines, and the second of them is two hundred and two days old and has nothing on it. He came into the yard at about the sixth hour this morning and went nowhere near the bench.

The compost line is paid at twenty-six and is not discharged in twenty-six, and it will not fall tomorrow either. Seven sessions are entered in this holding's book and there was no eighth this morning, and a man sitting on a bench is not a session.

Requests stand at fifty-three and the section-nine notes stand at fifty-three and neither has moved on any morning of this week. The letter went into neither of them on the ninth hour of the second of this month and has been in neither of them every morning since.

What this holding does not know about is given six ruled rows and not one unit against any of them. A book nobody can name the purpose of is not a seventh. A pen left lying on a bench is not a seventh. The sixth row is still the sixth row and the seventh row is still not ruled.

The use log did not take a line this morning and stands where it stood on Saturday, at fifteen. Loaded, the barrow came down the four-mile road at about the seventh hour, and its journeys stand at eleven, and eleven is a floor.

Nine hundred yards off there is a door with seven columns cut in its stone and the seventh of those columns is at sixty-four. Its rule is the first of a month and the thirtieth and nothing else, so nobody has walked those nine hundred yards this week.

The man of about seventy stands at twenty-eight fetchings and was not fetched this morning. His next is the first of the next month. Nobody in this holding has ever put a question to him and nobody is going to.

There is a ring of bare ground inside the eleven acres and no one in this holding has ever stood in it. Nobody walked it this week either and nobody measured it.

What came up on the low field above the middle road is elsewhere entirely, and there is not one sum on any of these four books that would join it to that ring.

In the corner of the one room somebody ruled four lines under two words a long time ago and nothing has ever been written on them since. The answer they are for has not come due.

Ruled and empty is also what stands in the third column of the sheet of terms, and nobody has altered the heading over it. The paper on the low board is face up and has no date and no line that a name could be put into.

Saturday was the day two men gave themselves and this is the morning after it, and the sun was on the outlet end by seven and the water in the ditch was slow and clear and the trough at the head of the four-mile road was full and inside its lip, and a book with fifteen lines in it went back under a weight on a shelf with nothing added to it, and nobody thanked anybody.

> Entered by the clerk of this holding at the first hour, in his own hand, as one unbroken line: **The second. The second line of the rota worked, the outlet end and the two stones at the head of it, the pack under the near stone lifted and put back wet. The pair not read and no figure standing against it, the half of the run being half a number on this morning. The same day in and the same day out. One thousand and eighty-five hundredweight to the launder, being the three hundred and fourteenth. A book on a bench in this yard was opened at its last page at the tenth hour and was shut again at the eleventh with nothing added to it, and two people asked the man who keeps it what it is for and he said he does not know and has not asked in nine years, and no line was ruled and nothing was entered in any of the four books in this holding.** Nothing of this is a forecast and nothing of it is a cause and nothing of it is a total.
