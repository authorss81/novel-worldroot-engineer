# Chapter 629

## The Second Of The Seventeenth, And A Mason Who Told Them On A Monday

Harlan Vetch came through the gate before the wall was read, with a hod on his shoulder, and put it down on the frozen ground before he said anything, which is not how he usually come into that yard.

**"I dug the bed four inches deeper,"** he said. **"At the north end, two feet of the third course, and I did it the second time I took those stones out. I did it without a level. I am telling you at eight in the morning on a Monday because you would have found it by Thursday and because I have had a weekend to think about what Thursday would have looked like."**

Nobody spoke. The clerk had his slate half up and let it down again.

**"You have told us,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"You have not told us what you want us to do with it."**

**"That is correct and I do not have an answer for it,"** Harlan Vetch said, and picked the hod up again and went to the wall.

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden came to the tap step at seven with the count going in her head and stood with her palm flat on the iron until she had it, and the iron was cold and then it was not, and she lifted her hand off at a hundred, and nobody in that yard interrupted a woman counting to a hundred at a step.

The clerk read the wall at nine and read it twice.

**One thousand and two hundred and two hundredweight.**

**"That is over a thousand two hundred,"** he said, and stopped there, and then went on, and it was plain that he had gone past the place where a man stops. **"Saturday was one thousand one hundred and ninety-nine, and that is a rise of eight. It has been a rise or a fall every morning since this run of forty-nine began."**

**"In four years I have never once had to say anything about where it stands, because where it stands is not a thing anybody in this holding is watching. This morning it has gone over a thousand two hundred, and there is nobody in this yard who has been told that is a thing this yard can do."**

**"Then somebody had better tell somebody,"** Hesta Lyle said.

**"I have just told you,"** the clerk said.

The three hundred and ninety-second of the run went on the slate under it and not in the book, and the wall got the chalk and the slate got the rest, and that has been the arrangement for four years.

**"The pair on the board is not going up this morning,"** Kellan Rusk said, from the gate, before anybody had looked for it. **"It goes up at the seventh hour on the odd mornings and today is a Monday, and I stood in this yard two weeks ago and said I would read it either way, and what I have decided since is that I do not get to decide which way the week goes. Renn Ashby reads it. It is his figure. I have had my hands near it long enough."**

The two halves went on the slate under the wall reading, in the order the rising one moves first on an even morning. A hundred and eighty-five, and then a hundred and seventy-four, which does not move on a Monday. They came to three hundred and fifty-nine and were written with nothing above them and nothing below them and nothing added to the launder, and Kellan Rusk put his thumb on the sum to hold the chalk still while he wrote it.

**"Those two have never made a number with that figure on it,"** he said. **"There are three of them on this slate and no two of them are the same size. I have never worked out what they are for and I have stopped working out what things are for in this yard, and that is not a complaint, it is a job."**

He walked the length of the wall after that with the slate against his hip and read the two ends of it out loud the way you read the two ends of a roof beam, which is to say without any expression at all. Seven hundred and seventy-six at this end, where he was standing. Eight hundred and twenty-three at the far end, where he could not see the figures and read them off the slate because he had written them there himself at eight.

Forty-seven between them, and forty-seven between them since the two boards were first put up. That number is not written on either board and has never been written on either board, and nobody in this holding has ever added either of them to anything.

Sera Quill had the two reckonings on her slate by eight with the day on them and her name under the day, and under the two figures the third line stood in a different hand from the rest of it and said: carried forward, no rule shown.

**"The first is eighty-fifth,"** she said. **"The second is seventy-second. Thirteen apart, the same as it was when this slate started, and I am going to say the line underneath out loud in a yard rather than leave it in my hand for one reader. The second of those two is the pair I copied forward. It went up by one, and the thing that moved it up by one is that I copied the pair I already had. There is no rule in this building that produces that, and if a rule turns up in a book one day it will be because a person found one, and not because I did."**

The count in force came round by itself on the Monday, being the fourth time since this holding began keeping it, and stood at a hundred and thirty-seven with twenty-nine taken and a hundred and eight not, and no field came off the low ground, and no name went on anything.

Sera Quill read the long sheet out loud while she was writing the day on her slate, which put the figures in the middle of two other pieces of work. Four bodies of households, four lines, two signatures to a line and not a name to any of them. Three hundred and ninety on the line itself. Underneath it, in a hand half the size of the clerk's, the clause that never gets read with it: the three hundred and eighty-ninth out of three hundred and ninety-first. One under and one over, and the over has never once come down onto the line, and the clause is not added to the launder and has never been added to the wall.

**"Nobody took a field this morning,"** Hesta Lyle said, to the yard at large. **"And nobody is going to be told off this afternoon for not taking one either. That is not a kindness and it is not a rule, it is what a cycle is, and it has been what a cycle is since before any of us were asked to hold anything."**

In the one room the man of about fifty had the register form in his left hand and the charter behind it in his right, which is the wrong way round for anybody else in that building, and the clerk came round the table, put them the right way up, and did not touch them again.

**"I am not going to write down what you are about to ask me,"** the clerk said.

**"I am going to ask you how long a piece of paper has been lying on a table, and you are going to tell me by looking at it."**

**"Then look at it."**

**"That is two hundred and eighty-six days on the form,"** the man of about fifty said, **"and I have got it in my hand, and you have got the other one."**

**"That is the charter and it is a hundred and ninety-two days,"** the clerk said, **"and I have been saying three numbers out loud at that table for four years, and you have just said two of them in the wrong order and got both of them right."**

The two of them stood there with one figure between them and neither would put it down, and the third one was not spoken by either. It stood at two hundred and eighty on the second ruled line in Silling's own book with nothing on it at all, and it got established that morning by a finger resting on the edge of the shelf, which is not a way of saying a figure and is the only way left for that one.

Marek went out to the tap at nine to look at a thread that had nothing wrong with it and stood there long enough to be asked about it.

**"It has been in five days and it has not wept,"** he said. **"I have spent five days waiting for a reason to take that joint apart again and I have just gone out and looked for one and there is not one, and I am telling this yard that I went looking, because a man only goes out to a tap when he has already made up his mind is not looking."**

**"Then do not go out to it,"** the woman of about thirty-eight said. **"There are four of us who would rather you did not come out to it in front of us twice a week."**

**"Then I will come out to it when there is something to see,"** Marek said, and he did not come out to it again that day.

He stopped in the middle of the yard at ten with a barrow handle under his arm and said the thing he says most mornings.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning, and I am going to give the reason before somebody asks me for one, because if I wait to be asked then the not saying is a refusal and it is not. A woman was standing at that step counting to a hundred with her hand on the iron and she was not going to stop for me. There is one kind of not saying that is discipline and there is another kind that is manners, and this morning it was manners, and I am not going to stand here and dress manners up as anything else."**

The three sheets were still on the long table. The first of them has lain face up for eighty-nine days, and there are two hands of bare board either side of it, laid out by a man who took more trouble over the bare board than over the paper. The second is folded on its crease the other way round. The third carries a name, a date, a hand and a seal, and there is nothing in it that anybody in this holding has ever been able to fault. Not one of the three has been answered, refused, moved, or ruled under.

The man of about fifty walked the yard after dark with his hands in his pockets and did the thing he does on a walk, which is to look at each thing once and not twice.

The board in the one room: thirty-nine blanks, and the second rule open underneath them, and a rule that comes back empty opening nothing. The two ledgers on the shelf, fifty-three in the first and fifty-three in the second, both the same length and neither one short of a line to put a thing in. The book by the door with seven sessions entered in it. The six bare rows with clean paper under the last of them, and the four ruled lines in the corner under two words, and the third column on the terms sheet with a heading over it that has never been improved.

Harlan Vetch came back at dark to put the hod down and found that what he looked at instead was the low field.

The compost line out by the bins is paid at twenty-nine and has been let out in nothing at all, and a Monday is not an interval. The use log inside the tool house is fifteen lines and the sixteenth has never been opened. The barrow by the gate says eleven journeys and eleven is a floor, and nobody has ever put a twelfth mark on it.

At the bottom of the low field the ring of bare ground stands as bare as it did the day the thing came up. He looked at it from the near side and did not go over it, which he has never done, and which is the whole of the arrangement he has with it. The ladder is against the wall above the field with two people belonging to it, neither of them on it, and nothing written against either of their names. The offer lies on the board under the ladder in the dust it has been lying in for four years.

The man of about seventy was not fetched and nothing was put to him. The door nine hundred yards off keeps its sixty-six scratched into the seventh column by somebody who has not been in this yard for years, and there is one reading left in it and nobody claimed that reading this morning.

The drawer behind the near board stood shut at every hour of the day, and the key to it stood on its nail in the one room door at every hour of the day, and it was on the nail when the clerk turned the yard key at dusk.

Monday was the morning a figure nobody in this holding had ever had to say out loud went past a thousand two hundred, and nobody was told to do anything about it, and the tap ran all afternoon, and Harlan Vetch put his hod down before he spoke and picked it up again after.
