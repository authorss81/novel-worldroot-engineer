# Chapter 633

## The Sixth Of The Seventeenth, And A Hand That Stopped A Form Being Turned Over

The clerk was putting the register form back into the rack at ten with its face down, which is the way it goes in, and a hand came across the top of it and stopped it before it went under.

**"Leave that one up,"** the man of about fifty said. **"I want to see it."**

**"It goes in the other way up,"** the clerk said.

**"I know it goes in the other way up. I have watched it go in the other way up for four years. I am asking you to leave it up for a minute."**

The clerk left it up. That was the whole of it, and nobody in the room said anything else about it for eleven minutes.

He got his thumb on the date in the top corner and turned the sheet a quarter round so the light from the door fell across it, and he read the date and not the age, and then he read the age.

**"Two hundred and ninety days,"** he said, **"and I have got it out of the date and not out of a person, which is what I wanted, and it took me the better part of four minutes and I would not have got it at all out of memory."**

**"You could have asked me,"** the clerk said.

**"I could have asked you and you would have told me,"** the man of about fifty said, **"and I would have had two things this morning instead of one, and I am trying to learn what I do on my own with a rack. Six weeks is a fortnight of learning and I have had two of them and I am not very quick."**

He turned the charter round after that, and read its date with his thumb on it the same way, and got a hundred and ninety-six out of it. The second ruled line in Silling's own book he did not touch and did not ask about, and it stands at two hundred and eighty-four with nothing on it, and it got established for the room by the clerk putting his hand flat on the shelf to the left of it and saying nothing, which is the second time in four years that the man in this building has acknowledged that line without reading it.

Silling was in the doorway at half past nine for the pair on the board that is not there on a Friday, and he said why, and he said it in a way that stopped two people who had come down the yard ready to say it first.

**"It goes up on the odd mornings,"** he said. **"That is not a rule anybody made. It is a thing a man has done for four years on the mornings he was here and has not done on the mornings he was not, and it has turned out to be a rule because the arithmetic underneath it does not care whether he is standing in the doorway. I read it out on Mondays and Fridays for eleven months of one year and eleven days of the next, and there is no principle in it, and I would be ashamed to give it one."**

**"Then why do you keep your own count of it,"** Kellan Rusk said.

**"Because I keep my own count of everything and I am seventy-one and there is a use log in the tool house with fifteen lines in it and I have never once been the man who writes the sixteenth."**

The count in force came up on the Friday the way it always comes up on a Friday, and it is a hundred and thirty-eight now, twenty-nine of them taken and a hundred and nine not. The field that came round with it did not come off the low ground, and no name went onto any of the blanks, and the six bare rows did not get a seventh cut under them while everybody was looking at them.

**"I am going to say the thing I say on these mornings out loud once more this morning,"** Hesta Lyle said, to the yard, **"and then I am going to stop, because four of us have now said it this week and one of us saying it well does not make it more true. Nobody took a field. Nobody is going to be spoken to about not taking one. That is the fourth time this month and the arrangement is older than that."**

Sera Quill had the two reckonings on her slate and had put the date on them, and read them both out in the one room with four people listening, which is the first time she has read them to anybody.

**"Eighty-sixth,"** she said. **"And seventy-third. Thirteen apart, and the third line underneath is mine and it says what it has always said, and I am going to say the second one out loud in a room rather than only in my hand. The seventy-third is the pair I copied forward. It is not arrived at. There is no rule in this holding, in this district, or in any paper I have ever been shown that moves that number, and if one is found it will be found by somebody with a reason, and it will not be found because a woman copied a pair."**

Harlan Vetch took the two stones of the third course out at eleven, in the yard, with the whole of the holding able to see him do it.

He dug the bed back to where it was before Saturday, which is shallower than it is now by four inches and is the depth his grandfather would have dug. He put the stones back into it by hand and by eye and without a level, and he rammed the joint at the back the way he rammed it, and when he was finished he put both hands flat on the course and left them there while the yard came and looked at it.

**"That is where it was,"** he said. **"It is not level and it never was and there is no figure coming for it. A man dug a bed deeper so that a stone would sit where he wanted it, and a man has put it back where it was, and the only thing anybody has learned today is that the second one was harder."**

**"You have made it worse,"** the woman of about thirty-eight said, from the tap.

**"I have made it honest,"** Harlan Vetch said. **"They are not the same and I would like somebody to write that down, and nobody is going to."**

The clerk read the wall at nine and had the frost out of it before the sun.

**One thousand and two hundred and eight hundredweight.**

**"Eight on, three hundred and ninety-sixth of the run,"** he said, **"and Friday is an even morning, so there is no pair in the doorway this morning, and I have been told why and I am not going to ask again."**

The two halves came off the slate without a reading. The rising one had gone up by one and was a hundred and eighty-seven. The falling one had not moved and was a hundred and seventy-six. Three hundred and sixty-three between them and no sum said out loud for a second morning running, and the clerk has now stopped writing the sum down at all and only the two go on the slate, and Hesta Lyle asked him on Thursday whether that was a decision and he said it was a Wednesday.

On the long sheet the four bodies of households are at three hundred and ninety-four, and in the small hand under the line are one under and one over, being the three hundred and ninety-third and the three hundred and ninety-fifth. Two signatures to a line and not one name to any of them. Seven hundred and eighty at this end of the wall and eight hundred and twenty-seven at the other, forty-seven between them, and the drawer behind the near board has been shut every hour and its key has been on the nail every hour.

Sennik Vaul asked the man of about fifty at the gate in the afternoon whether the door nine hundred yards off was going to be read again.

**"It has been read once in four years,"** the man of about fifty said, **"and it was me that read it, and I gave the number to one person and wrote it nowhere."**

**"There is one reading left in this holding,"** Sennik Vaul said. **"I have not counted it. Somebody has, and it is not me, and it is written somewhere I have been told I am not to be told about. I am asking whether it is going to be read."**

**"No,"** the man of about fifty said, **"and I want you to hear the reason, because I have never given it to anybody. If I read it and the number comes back at sixty-six again then a person has stood at a door nine hundred yards off and read a figure and it has not moved, and that person will never be asked again for the rest of his life. I have been asked twice this month whether it was a floor and I could not answer either time. Sixty-six is a figure that has never been proved and it is not going to be proved by me."**

The three sheets were on the long table, where they have lain for longer than most of the men who laid them out have been in this yard. The first of them is ninety-three days old. Nobody has answered it, nobody has refused it, and the bare board either side of it is the same width it was on the day it was put down.

Paid stands in front of twenty-nine on the compost line and has been discharged in nothing. The use log holds fifteen lines. The barrow holds eleven journeys and eleven is a floor. Thirty-nine blanks. A second rule open and empty under them. Seven sessions and no eighth. Fifty-three of one thing and fifty-three of another and neither has taken a sheet off the long table or a page out of a coat. Twenty-nine fetchings, and the man of about seventy was not fetched and nothing was put to him. Sixty-six, unread, one reading left.

The six bare rows took nothing. The four ruled lines under two words took nothing. The third column on the terms sheet is ruled and empty under a heading nobody has improved. The ladder went up no rung and no figure stands against either of its two people. The offer on the low board is undated, unmoved, untaken, not taken back. Nothing has ever been measured in the ring of bare ground.

Marek spent Friday at the north end of a wall that had been put back wrong on purpose by a man who knew it, and did not go out to the tap, and told nobody he had not gone out to the tap.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning,"** he said, at three. **"And I am going to be plain that this is a small reason and I am not going to make it bigger than it is. There was a man in the one room at ten o'clock with his thumb on a date and eleven minutes of the room's whole talking time went past a piece of paper, and that was the loudest thing that happened in this yard today before two men started arguing about a bed."**

**"You would have made it louder,"** the woman of about thirty-eight said.

**"I would have made it louder and it would have been about me,"** Marek said.

The clerk turned the yard key at noon and at dusk and did not look at the nail this evening, because the man of about fifty was standing at the door when he did it, and the key was on the nail at both hours, and the drawer behind the near board stood shut through a Friday on which a mason undid his own work in front of a yard and then told them it had been the harder half.

Lime went hard on the face of the north end all Friday, because Harlan Vetch squeezed that joint instead of striking it, and at dusk he went back with a chisel and took his own lump off the wall in about twenty minutes. He did not tell anybody that he had done it. There is a clean place on that wall now where the lump was, and it is the only clean place on it.
