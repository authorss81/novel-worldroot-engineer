# Chapter 637

## The Tenth Of The Seventeenth, And The Morning Both Of Them Were Said Whole

The run had four hundred numbers in it on the last morning of it, one for every day since it began, and the clerk wrote the ordinal on the slate before he read anything else so that the number would be on the board before the figure was, and then he read the wall.

**One thousand and two hundred and fourteen hundredweight.**

**"That is the four hundredth,"** he said, **"and it is eight on, and it is the highest figure this run has ever been at, and I have written the number under it as well as the figure because a man who has been counting for four hundred mornings ought to be allowed to say so out loud once."**

**"You have been counting for four years,"** the man of about fifty said from the gate.

**"Four hundred mornings of this run and about four thousand before it,"** the clerk said, **"and I am not going to say what that is worth, because the only person in this yard who is allowed to price it has refused, twice, this week."**

The window is three hundred and sixty-seven, being a hundred and eighty-nine against a hundred and seventy-eight, and the rising half has moved on every second morning of this run and not on any of the others, and nobody has ever drawn that line through anything.

The long sheet carries the four bodies of households at three hundred and ninety-eight. Under it the small hand has the three hundred and ninety-seventh under the line and the three hundred and ninety-ninth over it, and the two have been one under and one over for as long as the sheet has been on the wall, and the line itself has never once been the same figure as either of them.

The near board reads seven hundred and eighty-four and the far one eight hundred and thirty-one. Forty-seven between them, which has been the distance between them for four years and which is not written anywhere and which nobody in this holding has ever added to a third number.

The drawer behind the near board stood shut at every hour of this morning and at every hour of every morning of this run. The one key to it never left a nail in the frame of the one room door, on any morning, at any hour, and it was still on that nail when the clerk turned the yard key at dusk.

The count in force came round on the last morning of this run. A hundred and thirty-nine, twenty-nine of them taken, a hundred and ten of them not.

What came round with it was a field, and the field did not come off the low ground, and no name went into any of the thirty-nine blanks on the board in the one room. The second rule under those blanks stood open and empty for the last time this run has, and a rule that comes back empty opens nothing, and it opened nothing.

**"No field,"** Hesta Lyle said. **"And that is the last of these and nobody is going to be told off about it, and I am not going to say it again for a month."**

The three ages were read in the one room at eleven by two people, one figure each, because the man of about fifty had asked whether it was still done the way it was done and the clerk had said he did not know of a better way.

**"Two hundred and ninety-four days on the register form,"** the man of about fifty said, **"and I have got it off the date and not out of a man, which is where I have got two hundred and ninety since Friday."**

**"A hundred and ninety-nine on the charter behind it,"** the clerk said, **"and I have been saying that one out loud since before you were in this yard and I have never once got it wrong."**

**"And two hundred and eighty-eight,"** the clerk said, **"on the second ruled line in Silling's own book, and that one I am not going to say to the room. I am going to put my hand on it."**

He put two fingers flat on the shelf to the left of the line and left them there for as long as it takes a man to be sure that a room has heard nothing, and the room heard nothing, and he took his hand away.

**"Three of them in one morning for the first time in four years and the third of them is a man touching a shelf,"** Sennik Vaul said.

**"The third of them has been a man touching a shelf on and off for two years and this is simply the morning it got said out loud,"** the clerk said.

Renn Ashby was in the doorway at the seventh hour with the book shut under his arm and did not open it, and gave the reason himself, in his own words, which he has now done on six mornings out of the seven this week, on no two of them in the same order.

**"It goes up at the seventh hour on the odd mornings and this is an even morning and the pair is not mine this morning and it will not be mine tomorrow either, and there is a woman standing in this yard who has been turning down every kind of job offered to her all week on the grounds that she has not got time, and I am not going to be the last man in this holding who was asked to take something and said yes for the wrong reason."**

The reading ran for four hundred mornings on the odd ones and stops today for good in this yard, and the heading over it is of a month nine months back and has not moved since the first of the fifteenth and did not move across either month turn.

Three sheets are on the long table. Ninety-seven days is the age of the first of them. The second has been folded back on the other crease since the day it came, and it has never been opened. The third is headed with a name, a date, a hand and a seal, it is correct in every particular, and nobody in this holding has ever been able to put a finger on the thing wrong with it, and no page of this holding has ever called it anything else. About nine inches of bare board stands either side of each one. Nobody was asked to choose anything this morning.

Paid is in front of thirty and has been discharged in nothing. Fifteen lines in the use log and the sixteenth never opened. Eleven journeys on the barrow and eleven a floor. Seven sessions in this holding's book and this morning did not add an eighth. Fifty-three and fifty-three and neither has taken a sheet off that table or a page out of a coat.

The man of about seventy was not fetched and nothing was put to him at any site. The figure in the seventh column of the board door nine hundred yards off is sixty-six and it was not read this morning and it is not going down, and the one reading this holding has left stays where it is.

Nobody climbed the ladder and nothing has ever been written against either of the two people who belong to it. The offer on the low board has no day on it, has not been touched, has not been taken up and has not been taken back. At the top of the low field the ring of bare ground has never been measured, paced or pegged, and nothing that came up on that field has ever been added to it.

Four ruled lines under two words are bare. A column on the terms sheet was ruled under a heading and the heading has never been improved and there is nothing under it. Inside the tool house a mark on the wall measures four inches, forks once into two forks, and has not acquired a third mark in four years. Six rows stand ruled against what this holding does not know, and no seventh was cut for the covenant, the machine, the schedule or the confession.

The tap ran all morning. The standpipe joint at the far end of the tap house has been in nine days and it is weeping and it is holding, and a Tuesday has now happened to it and proved nothing, and the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden said all of that out loud at ten and nobody argued with her about a word of it.

Harlan Vetch was on the wall at the north end with a green thread of yarrow in the second joint of the third course and a chisel in his hand, and he did not take it out.

Marek stood in the middle of the yard at noon, which he has done perhaps eleven times in four years and always badly, and this time he stayed there while the yard went on doing what it was doing behind him, and at some point the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden came round the butt with a can in each hand and said the thing that has been in her since the third morning of this run, and said all of it, unshortened, with nobody interrupting and nobody answering.

**"The far end of a thing is held by two people and neither of them is me, and no instrument anywhere in four counties says whether it is on or off."**

She went on with the cans. A man came past with a barrow and asked her about the second can.

Then Marek said the other one, because the yard has two things it says out loud and one of them had never been said in front of nine people at once, and because he had given a reason out loud every morning for nine mornings and the ninth reason was the last one he had.

**"The comfort is not standing, the father is not alive in the wood, and the day does not come back."**

Nobody answered that either. Tova Reed went on sorting. The clerk turned and looked at the window and then looked back at the wall, which is what the clerk does when there is a figure on it and nothing new. The tap ran. The man of about fifty stood at the gate with the loading stick in his hand and did not say a word about six weeks or about the nail.

They were said and nothing happened, which is what a thing does when it is said out loud in a yard at noon instead of at night, and the yard went on, and at dusk the clerk turned the yard key and the second key was on its nail in the one room door and the drawer behind the near board stood shut on the last morning of this run.

Thresh is the noise a floor makes when a barrow is tipped on it, and the loading went on at four o'clock and the yard was full of it and nobody heard it, and that was the sound of the last morning of this run and it was made by nine people and none of them was listening.
