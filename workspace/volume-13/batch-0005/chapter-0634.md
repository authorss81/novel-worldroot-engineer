# Chapter 634

## The Seventh Of The Seventeenth, And A Load That Came Up The Road At Six Hundred And Twenty-Four

The load came up the fen road at twenty past seven in the morning with the frost still in the hedges, and it was the sixty-first of them to come up that road in four years, and it came up at six hundred and twenty-four hundredweight, which is eleven on the last one and which nobody in that yard confused with the nine days between the two.

The man of about fifty stood at the gate with the loading stick and said both numbers out loud before he let the wheels through, and he said them out loud on purpose, having practised nothing.

**"Six hundred and twenty-four hundredweight,"** he said. **"That is eleven above six hundred and thirteen. There are nine days between this and the last one. Those are two different figures and I am not going to have anybody in this yard print them as one number, and I say that every time now because every time now somebody new asks me which of the two it stepped by."**

**"It stepped by eleven,"** Kellan Rusk said.

**"The load stepped by eleven and the interval is nine days. Yes."**

The fifty-first pair of names went onto the board in the one room at half past eight, in the place they have gone for four years, written by a man who did not read them out and had been asked to read them out and would not.

The two halves of the load were checked twice against each other and were not added to each other, and were not added to anything else, and the checking took nine minutes. The second rule in the one room stood open and empty under the thirty-nine blanks, and thirty-nine did not move, and nobody in that room looked at a blank the way a person looks at a blank they are thinking about.

The compost line went at ten past eight, while the yard was still on the road side of the load.

Nobody had set an hour for it. Nobody in this holding has ever set an hour for the compost line. It went on the interval it has always gone on, twenty-six mornings after the last time, and the figure in front of paid went from twenty-nine to thirty, and the line is paid at thirty, and being paid at thirty is not the same thing as being let out at thirty, and there is no discharge in it and nothing has been discharged in it.

**"It has stepped,"** the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden said, from about forty feet away, with a can in each hand. **"Twenty-six mornings, same as the last time, and not one person in this county knew what hour it would do it."**

**"I did not know,"** Harlan Vetch said, and then, before anyone else had said anything at all, **"I did not know and I am glad, and I have got no more to say about it than that, and I am going back to a wall."**

**"You are allowed to be glad about a compost heap,"** Kellan Rusk said.

**"I am allowed to be glad about anything that happens on an interval without a person in it,"** Harlan Vetch said.

Renn Ashby read the run out of the doorway before the yard came in from the road, with the page turned his way and the stone on the corner and four voices on the last sentence, which he mentioned to nobody and which the yard mentioned for him all morning.

**"One hundred and sixty-nine mornings of three hundred and twenty-one,"** he said. **"The heading over the pair is of a month nine months back and it has not moved since the first of the fifteenth and it is not going to move because a load came up a road. If I ever read that pair and my voice goes up on the word mornings, somebody take the page off me."**

The clerk read the wall at nine with a load still unloading behind him, which is the worst possible condition for the man who reads the wall and which he has not mentioned once in four years.

**One thousand and two hundred and three hundredweight.**

**"Five off,"** he said. **"Three hundred and ninety-seventh of the run, and this is an odd morning so there is a pair in the doorway, and I would like it noted that a man reading a wall with a wagon behind him gets the right answer nine times out of ten and that is not good enough and I do it anyway."**

The two halves came off the slate in the hand of the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden, who was standing at the gate with the loading stick in her teeth and had taken the slate off the wall because both her hands were full and she would not wait.

**"The one that moved this morning is a hundred and seventy-seven,"** she said, **"and the one that did not move is a hundred and eighty-seven, and they are three hundred and sixty-four."** She hung the slate back on its nail and wiped her hands down the front of her skirt and did not go over the numbers again. **"Do not ask me to say them twice, because I have just said them while holding a stick in my teeth and I could have got either of them wrong and I would not have known."**

She then said the three ages from memory at the gate, while a man unloaded a sack off the tailboard and nobody was holding a rack for her.

**"The register form in that rack is two hundred and ninety-one days,"** she said. **"A charter of a hundred and ninety-seven is behind it. Then there is the line in Silling's own book that nobody has ever written on, and that stands at two hundred and eighty-five, and I am telling you that last figure out of nine months and a week of memory and not out of a book, and if I have it wrong then I am wrong and you will know by Friday."**

**"It is right,"** the clerk said.

**"Then I will say so on Monday, and not before."**

The long sheet carries the four bodies of households at three hundred and ninety-five. Written under it, in a hand half the height of the clerk's, are the two figures of the clause, and the clause is a single one under the line and a single one over it: three hundred and ninety-fourth and three hundred and ninety-sixth.

Of the two boards, the near one reads seven hundred and eighty-one and the far one eight hundred and twenty-eight, and the forty-seven between them is the same it was this morning and the same it has been since the wall was built.

The drawer behind the near board has been shut at every hour of a Saturday, and its key has been on the nail in the one room door at every hour of it.

Harlan Vetch put the two stones out of the third course at the north end again at half past ten, and dug the bed at the depth it was at before he ever touched it, and put them back, and this time he had two men watching on purpose, and one of them had a level in his hand and did not offer it.

**"Do you want me to put this level on it,"** the man with the level said.

**"No,"** Harlan Vetch said. **"And that is the fourth time I have said no to a figure on that wall and the reason has not changed and I am not going to give you a new one. It is not level. It was not level when I started. If you put your level on it now you will find what I have always known, which is that a wall built by four generations of men with a line of sight and not with an instrument is a different object from a wall built with an instrument, and both of them stand up, and only one of them is worth arguing about."**

**"Then what is worth arguing about,"** the man with the level said.

**"The bed I dug,"** Harlan Vetch said.

The count in force did not rise on a Saturday and stands at a hundred and thirty-eight with twenty-nine taken and a hundred and nine not. Fifty-first pair of names on the board and thirty-nine blanks and the second rule open and empty underneath them, and no name in any of the blanks, and the rule that came back empty opened nothing.

Paid is in front of thirty now, and was in front of twenty-nine at seven o'clock this morning, and the line has not been discharged in anything. Fifteen lines in the use log and the sixteenth not written. Eleven journeys on the barrow and eleven a floor. Seven sessions in this holding's book and a Saturday is not an eighth. Fifty-three and fifty-three and neither has taken a sheet off the long table, where the first of them is ninety-four days old, or a page out of a coat.

The man of about seventy was not fetched and nothing was put to him. Sixty-six in the seventh column of a door nine hundred yards off was not read, and there is one reading left in the whole of this holding, and a Saturday morning with a load on the road is exactly the morning a man would spend it on, and nobody did.

Marek was not at the gate, which is the second load in four years he has not met at the gate, and he had been asked about it on the Friday and had said he would not, and he had said why, which was that a yard that greets its own loads at the gate has got one more job than it needs.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning,"** he said, when he came down to the road side at noon. **"And I am going to give the reason and it is a small one and it belongs to a different man. The man at that gate has got a number right out loud twice now in a week, in front of people, and it is the first time in four years he has done that, and he has done it by refusing three things. I am not going to walk up beside him and take the size out of the morning."**

**"You could stand further off,"** the woman of about thirty-eight said.

**"I could,"** Marek said, **"and I did not, and that is what I am not going to do again tomorrow."**

The clerk turned the yard key at noon and at dusk. The other key was on its nail in the one room door at both of those hours, and the drawer stayed shut through a Saturday on which a load came up a road at six hundred and twenty-four hundredweight and a heap stepped up a number at an hour nobody in this holding knew, and the one reading this holding has left went unread for the sixth morning running.

Salt was on the road by nine, where the frost had been at five, and the wheels had it coming up off the fen road in a white line, and the yard got it on the gate track and left it, because a yard that salts its own gate track is a yard that has decided the ground belongs to it, and nobody in this holding has ever decided that about the ground outside the gate.
