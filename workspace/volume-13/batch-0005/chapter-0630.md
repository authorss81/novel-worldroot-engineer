# Chapter 630

## The Third Of The Seventeenth, And A Notice Given To A Nail

The cart that takes the week's slate off to the district went at half past six, and Soren Rill stood at the gate and read the three ages into the driver's back rather than into the yard, because a Tuesday morning is a morning with a timetable on it and he would not have stopped that cart for four words.

**"Two hundred and eighty-seven days, a hundred and ninety-three, and two hundred and eighty-one,"** he said. **"The last one is bare and I am telling you it is bare at the same time as I am telling you it, because if I leave it to the end you will think I forgot it."**

Two of the three of them went out over the yard wall. The one that did not go was the bare one, which is how a yard learns nothing.

**"I am not going to say them again,"** Soren Rill said, when the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden asked. **"Not because they are secret. Because a man who says a thing twice in one morning is a man who has started enjoying it, and I have been doing this long enough to know what my own voice does when I let it."**

Renn Ashby had the second book open in the doorway at the seventh hour with the page turned his way and a stone on the corner of it, and the heading over the pair is of a month nine months back and has been of a month nine months back since the first of the fifteenth.

**"One hundred and sixty-seven mornings of three hundred and seventeen,"** he said. **"I am going to say the thing I have been not saying for a fortnight, which is that I do not know what it is for. I have said it every odd morning for six weeks as though I did, because a yard does not want to hear a man admit that he is reading."**

**"It is not a rate. One side of it goes on by a single and the other side goes on by a pair and the two have never once been made into a number by anybody. The only thing I have ever been certain of is that the heading must not move, because the day that heading moves is the day this stops being two figures and becomes one figure with a date on it."**

Two voices came in on the last sentence of it, and he said nothing at all about that, which is the second Tuesday running he has said nothing about it.

The clerk read the wall at nine.

**One thousand and one hundred and ninety-seven hundredweight.**

**"Five off,"** he said. **"And three hundred and ninety-third of the run. Tuesday is an odd morning, which you can see from the fact that Renn Ashby is standing in the doorway being worth listening to, and which I would not have said out loud four years ago."**

Harlan Vetch laughed at that and went back to the north end, where two feet of the third course has been out and back and out and back again since Saturday and where the bed under it is now four inches deeper than anybody's grandfather would have dug it.

The two halves on the slate had already been settled by the time he got back to it, in the order they settle themselves on an odd morning. The falling one does not move on a Tuesday and goes first in the hand, a hundred and seventy-five. The rising one follows and is the same figure it was on Saturday, a hundred and eighty-five, because the rising half only moves when the total moves and the total moved on Monday.

Between them, three hundred and sixty. Nobody wrote the sum out loud this morning, and that is now the third morning running of the yard not writing it, and the clerk has stopped putting a line under the two figures because there is nothing to put it under.

The count in force stood where Monday left it, at a hundred and thirty-seven with twenty-nine taken and a hundred and eight not, and it did not rise on a Tuesday, and the two reckonings on Sera Quill's slate are still thirteen apart with the third line under them in her own hand saying that the second of them is carried forward and that no printed rule in this building has ever been shown to anybody.

The six ruled rows against what this holding does not know stand empty, and this morning somebody said all four of them out loud, which they do not do every day.

**"Covenant, machine, schedule and confession,"** Sera Quill said, counting them on the flat of the slate. **"Those are the four that would go in a seventh row if anybody ever cut one, and nobody has cut one, and there is paper down there with a clean edge on it waiting for a person to be the first. I say them out loud on a Tuesday because I am tired of the yard guessing which of the four I mean when I say not that one."**

**"You have never once been asked to cut it,"** the clerk said.

**"No, and I have never once been asked to cut it, and I have said them out loud forty times with nobody asking, and if that is a fault then it is a fault I have committed for free and I will take it."**

The long sheet went up on the wall with three hundred and ninety-one against the four bodies of households, and the clause under it in the small hand took the three hundred and ninetieth out of three hundred and ninety-second, one under and one over, and it is never added to the launder and it is never added to the wall, and nobody in this holding has ever tried to make it one thing with either of them.

The two boards are at seven hundred and seventy-seven and eight hundred and twenty-four, forty-seven between them and forty-seven between them since they were put up, and the drawer let into the masonry behind the near one has been shut every hour of the morning and the key to it has been on its nail in the one room door every hour of the morning, and at eleven a man stood in front of that nail and made everybody in the one room listen to him.

**"I gave six weeks' notice in this room before I went out on the Monday before last,"** the man of about fifty said. **"I gave it out loud and I gave it to about eleven people and I gave it to the door, and I have thought about it every day since and I have come to the conclusion that I did it wrong."**

**"You cannot take it back,"** the clerk said.

**"I am not taking it back. I want it written down."**

Sennik Vaul had come into the one room behind him and stood by the rack and said the part that was going to be said next, whether anybody wanted it or not.

**"Put a date on it,"** he said. **"Put the day it was given on it and put the day it runs out on it, and write it into the book the way you write the other things, and then in six weeks there is a day and on that day somebody either hands the key over or does not."**

**"There is nothing to write it on,"** the clerk said. **"I have been in this holding four years. There is a register form and there is a charter behind it and there is a book with four ruled lines in it and there is a wall with chalk on it. There is no page in this building where a man who says out loud that he will stop doing something in six weeks can be recorded, because no man has ever needed one before and because nobody who drew these forms up thought anybody would."**

The man of about fifty did not answer for a while.

**"Then write it on a form,"** Nia Vale said, from the door. **"It does not have to be the right form. It only has to be a thing with a day on it."**

**"No,"** he said, and he said it flat and fast, before anybody could agree with him, which is how you can tell a man has thought about it. **"If it is paper then somebody can hold me to it. I have given a word in a room, and a word in a room is a thing I can carry or not carry as I like."**

**"The moment it is paper with a date on it then the date is doing the carrying and not me. In six weeks there will be a man in here asking me whether a piece of paper says what I said, and I will not be able to tell him it does, because I did not write it."**

**"That is a very good reason for refusing,"** the clerk said. **"It is a terrible reason and I am not going to pretend it is both."**

**"It is one reason,"** the man of about fifty said, **"and it is about me and about nothing in this yard, and that is the last of it. The key stays on the nail until I decide otherwise and if I never decide otherwise then the key stays on the nail and one day the nail falls out of the door."**

Marek took the middle of the day and spent it on the north end of the wall without being asked, which he has done four mornings out of the last five and which nobody has now commented on, which is the yard's way of saying that a man doing something with his hands all morning is a thing that has stopped being news.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning,"** he said, when he came in from the cold. **"And I will give you the reason the way I gave you Monday's, which is that I am not going to have it be a refusal this time. Sera Quill asked me in this yard yesterday afternoon whether a copied figure is worth writing down, and I said no, and she took the no, and she had a place to put it. A man who has refused a thing in a yard has used up what he was allowed to be loud about that day."**

The three sheets are on the long table. The first of them has lain there ninety days and is not ninety-one, because the second is a Tuesday and a Tuesday does not add a day to it. The middle one has gone over on its old fold so that the crease runs the other way from the crease it was sent with. The third carries a name and a date and a hand and a seal, and nobody in this yard has ever found the thing wrong with it.

There is still no column ruled under any of the three in any book in this building, and there is a hand's width of bare board on either side of each one that the man who laid them out took the trouble to leave.

Kellan Rusk walked the boundary at dusk with his hands behind him, which he does most evenings and which nobody in this yard reads as an inspection, and he reported four things in the order he came at them.

The man of about seventy was not fetched and nothing was put to him. Out past the gate road a board door nine hundred yards off has a figure scratched into its seventh column, and the figure is sixty-six, and it was read once three weeks back by a man who told one person and has walked past it every day since. There is one reading left in this holding and nobody spent it on a Tuesday.

He put his palm flat on the lid of the compost bin, which is paid at twenty-nine and has been discharged in nothing, and said that a Tuesday is not an interval the way a Monday was not. In the tool house the use log is fifteen lines and the sixteenth has never been started. The barrow carries eleven journeys in chalk on its side, and eleven is a floor.

Inside the one room the board still carries thirty-nine blanks with the second rule standing open underneath them, and a rule that comes back empty opens nothing. The two books on the shelf hold fifty-three in one and fifty-three in the other, and neither has taken a sheet off the long table this morning or a page out of a woman's coat. Seven sessions are entered in the book by the door and a Tuesday is not an eighth.

Nothing has been done about the ladder. Its two people were not on it and nothing stands written against either of them. The offer is still on the low board in the same words under the same dust, undated, unmoved, neither taken up nor taken back. The ring of bare ground at the top of the low field is as bare as it was and nobody has paced it or driven a corner in it.

The clerk shut the yard at noon and again at dusk and looked at the nail in the one room door on his way past both times, because he has looked at that nail four times a day since a man gave notice to it. The key was on it at noon and on it at dusk. The drawer behind the near board stayed shut through the whole of a Tuesday in which its owner refused, out loud, to have his own notice put in writing, and that refusal was not written down either.

Wren came into the yard at four on its own and went along the wall the way it has gone along that wall for years, and the yard did not count it, and the counting was the kind that does not go in a book.
