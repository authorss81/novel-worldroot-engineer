# Chapter 622

## The Twenty-Fifth Of The Sixteenth, And A Slate That Had Three Figures On It Before Anybody Asked

Monday was flat and warm and the yard got on with the morning, and the only new thing in it was a slate.

Sera Quill had the three ages on the slate at a quarter to nine and had them on it before the wall was read, and when the clerk came out of the one room with the rack under his arm and found her holding it up to the seed house door, she did not put it down.

**"It is on here and I put it there at eight because I was standing at the rack and the three of them were in front of me,"** she said. **"If I had waited for somebody to ask then it would have been on here at half past ten and I would have got one of them wrong and said so afterwards, and I have watched a person do that in these ten mornings."**

**"You have got ahead of the room,"** the clerk said.

**"I have got ahead of the room by an hour and a half and I have not got ahead of anything else, and if you would rather I waited to be asked then I will wait to be asked, and I will stand here and be wrong at half past ten instead of right at a quarter to nine."**

**"Read it out,"** he said. **"Read it out so that the room has it in its ears and not only on my slate."**

**"Two hundred and seventy-nine days is the age of the register form,"** she said. **"Behind it, the charter, a hundred and eighty-five. And the second ruled line in Silling's own book is two hundred and seventy-three, and there is nothing on that line and nothing due on it."**

At the seventh hour Renn Ashby stood in the doorway with the second book open and turned around so that the page faced him, and a stone held the corner of it, and this morning nobody in the yard took the last sentence up.

**"One hundred and sixty-three mornings of three hundred and nine. Read it as two figures and not as a rate, because the first of them goes on by a single and the second of them goes on by a pair, and a pair that moves at two speeds is not a pair a man can cut in half."**

He shut the book under his arm and stood at the door and waited and nobody took the last sentence up, and he came in from the doorway with the book shut under his arm and stood in the yard for a while and then went and found the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden at the tap.

**"It has been four voices every morning since Tuesday and this morning it was me,"** he said. **"I read it right. I read it right and I am telling you I read it right because I have got it into my head that the four voices are the point and I have just found out they are not, they are only what happens when a yard is loud."**

**"Then it is four voices when it is loud and it is one when it is quiet,"** she said, **"and that is not a fault in the yard and it is not a fault in you, and I am not going to have you stand at that door on a quiet morning deciding whether to perform."**

**"I will do it anyway,"** he said. **"I am not going to make a morning into a decision. I will read it and if nobody comes with me then I will have read it."**

The wall was read at nine by the man of about fifty, who had not read it in his life and had been asked to, and who read it out of the book because Hesta Lyle was at the tap and did not want it.

**One thousand and one hundred and eighty-five hundredweight.**

**"Five off yesterday, and Monday is an odd morning, and it is the three hundred and eighty-fifth figure of the run,"** he said. **"A hundred and eighty-one and a hundred and seventy-one, and that is three hundred and fifty-two, and I have written it on the back of a piece of paper in my own hand and I am not going to read the paper out because reading the paper out is not what I was asked to do."**

**"You were asked to read the wall,"** Hesta Lyle said.

Three hundred and eighty-three is on the line of the sheet of the four bodies of households and the clause printed beneath it takes the three hundred and eighty-second out of three hundred and eighty-fourth. Seven hundred and sixty-nine on the near board, eight hundred and sixteen on the far one, and the drawer behind the near one shut with the key on its nail as it has been since the eighth.

The man of about fifty went up the fen road at half past ten with a length of bar in his hand, because Marek wanted to know whether the well house was holding any pipe, and it is nine hundred yards and it is the longest walk anybody in this holding takes in an ordinary week.

He came back at eleven and found the clerk at the rack and told him and only him, standing side on so that nobody going past could hear the shape of it.

**"Sixty-six,"** he said. **"Seventh column, scratched, and the scratches have gone over it twice, so somebody was not happy with it the first time, and there is nothing else scratched on that door in seven columns and seven lines and I did not go over it twice and I did not go round the back of it either."**

**"What is it for?"** the clerk said.

**"I do not know,"** the man of about fifty said, **"and I have known four years that I do not know, and this morning is the first morning I have stood in front of it, and I am not going to go back up that road this week and I am not going to start guessing out loud, because the first man in this holding who guesses about that number is going to be believed."**

**"Then do not guess,"** the clerk said.

**"I am not going to,"** he said, and put the length of bar down against the wall, and went and got a barrow, and did not say one word about it to anybody else that day.

Marek had the two pieces of iron on the bench with a file and a vice and a piece of soap and a candle, and on Monday he cut the first thread of the taper by hand and put it in the vice and cut the second one and took it out and looked at it against the light and said it was wrong and cut it again.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning,"** he said, and Nia Vale had come out with the trays at eleven and was standing there, **"The reason is that the man with the key has gone off up the fen road on my account and has come back and told one person one number, and I am not going to stand in a yard this week and put two large things into a morning in which a man has done one careful small thing and gone quiet."**

**"He told the clerk,"** she said.

Eighty-two days is how long the first of the three sheets has lain face up on the long table, and the second and the third have not moved in that time. About nine inches of bare board is left either side of each one, which is the most careful arrangement anybody in this holding has ever made, and nothing has been ruled under any of them in a single one of the four books. One column carries fifty-three requests and the section-nine column carries fifty-three notes, and this holding's book has seven entered sessions, and Monday is not one of them.

Thirty-nine blanks are ruled on the board in the one room, with the second rule open and empty under them, and a rule that comes back empty opens nothing. A second sheet carries six ruled rows against what this holding does not know, all of them bare, with paper below the sixth and nothing cut on it. Two words in the corner have four bare lines under them that have never taken a word, and the terms sheet has a third column ruled and empty under a heading nobody has improved.

The compost line carries the word paid in front of twenty-nine and there has been no discharge in it, and it will not move for twenty-six mornings. The use log holds fifteen lines with a clean margin under them, and the barrow against the wall carries eleven journeys in chalk down its side, and eleven is a floor.

The ladder was not climbed and the two people at the foot of it have no figure written against either. The offer on the low board is undated, untaken, and not taken back. Nothing has ever stood in the ring of bare ground and no dimension has ever been given it, and the low field is land of its own and does not join it.

Handle was still cold from the tap when she turned it at six and shut the water off, and the one room door was shut behind the clerk and the key to the drawer was on its nail in it, and the gate stood with its bar up two inches in the dark, and neither the man who held it on Friday nor the woman who lifted it was in the yard to see which of them had left it that way.
