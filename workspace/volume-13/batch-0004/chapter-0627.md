# Chapter 627

## The Thirtieth Of The Sixteenth, And A Joint Put In On Purpose Wrong So That a Man Would Know What He Was Looking At

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden turned the water off at the main at ten past six and Marek had the standpipe joint apart on the step of the tap house by twenty past seven, in the open, in the cold, with the vice on a plank and a plank on two upturned cans.

**"You are going to do it out here,"** she said.

**"I am going to do it out here,"** he said, **"and I said I would and I have not stopped saying it, and I am going to say the rest of it now so that nobody has to work it out from what leaks later. I am going to cut the first thread at about ten, and I am going to put it in wrong, and it is going to be the worst joint anybody in this county has seen this year."**

**"You have told me that three times this week."**

**"Then it is a thing that is easy to forget and I would rather be a bore about it than have eleven people stand in a road at eleven o'clock looking at a piece of iron and thinking a man has cut it badly."**

He came out of the tool house at nine with the length of bar the man of about fifty had brought back from the well house on Monday, and used it as a straight edge on the top of the iron before he cut anything, and did it where nine people could see him do it.

**"That is straight and it is level and it is straight and level with a piece of bar and not with a spirit,"** he said, **"and I am not going to put a figure on it, because the moment I put a figure on it somebody will come along in a year and say the figure is wrong, and it will be a figure I wrote on a Saturday."**

He cut the first thread at ten and it came off the file, thin at the top and thick at the bottom, and he held it up and looked at it against the grey and put it down and picked it up again.

**"That is a thread,"** he said. **"That is what a thread looks like. Now watch which way I put it in."**

He put it in with the taper the wrong way round, because that is what is in the pipe, and he put it in with a quarter of the sacking she wanted and not three quarters, and he tightened it with the bar and a wrench and let go, and the water came on at eleven and went out of the joint in three places at once and ran across the step into the channel.

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden put her thumb in the first one and her forefinger in the third and looked at the middle one for a while.

**"That is the top,"** she said. **"It is coming out at the top. It is coming out at the top because you have got nothing behind the thread and everything in front of it, and I have watched that joint do that for four months and I have packed it four times."**

**"Now I know what I am looking at,"** Marek said, and shut the water off again, and took it apart in ten minutes, and laid the two ends on the plank with the thread on the plank beside them.

A man came down the road from the well house at half past eleven with a barrow and looked at the three wet places on the step and said that was a disgrace, and the woman of about thirty-eight told him it was on purpose and that he had been told at ten o'clock, and he said he had not heard it and she said that it had been said in the middle of a yard, which is where things are said, and he took his barrow to the butt and filled it.

The yard drew from the well house for six hours. Five people carried, and the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden kept the black pail for herself because a black pail was the standpipe and the standpipe was off, and she carried to the door and the door is nine hundred yards off the gate and nine hundred yards from the well house, and she carried that walk eleven times.

Tova Reed carried from the side of the yard where she could see the pails coming, and she had been told which pail was which on Friday and she had not had to ask once, and at one o'clock she said so to nobody in particular.

**"I can see a red pail from the seed house door and I cannot hear a black one being filled at all,"** she said, **"and this morning I have done eleven walks of this yard and I have not once had to ask anybody what a bucket was doing, and that is because somebody told me on Friday instead of letting me find out today."**

Nia Vale went out to the well house at two with the coat buttoned to the throat and carried four times and did not take the folded page out of the inside pocket once, and the page was in the coat for the whole of a Saturday in which the holding had no water in it but the well house.

Kellan Rusk read the three ages at four, out of the rack, one at a time, with his own folded sheet in the other hand the whole time, and he read them slowly and correctly and did not put his own paper down on the bench to do it.

**"The register form is two hundred and eighty-four days old,"** he said, and read them one at a time out of the rack with his own folded sheet in the other hand. **"The charter behind it is a hundred and ninety. And the second ruled line in Silling's own book is two hundred and seventy-eight, and that one has been bare since before I came to this yard and I have never once seen a thing go on it."**

**"You have been holding two pieces of paper and reading three figures off a third,"** the woman of about thirty-eight said.

**"I have been holding two pieces of paper and reading three figures off a third,"** Kellan Rusk said, **"and the reason I have not put mine down is that the day I put it down on that table it becomes one of the three, and it is not one of the three, and I have watched what this holding does to a thing that is one of three."**

Harlan Vetch said the reason for the empty space in the morning out loud at the north end of the wall, on his own, to two men who were carrying a pail between them and did not stop.

**"There is no pair on the wall today because it is not read on an even morning, and I am saying it because these two men are going to walk past this wall eleven more times today and one of them is going to think a figure is missing and start hunting for who took it."**

**"Nobody is going to hunt for anything,"** the man of about fifty said.

**"That is what I am paid for,"** Harlan Vetch said, and neither of them laughed, and both of them carried the pail on.

The clerk read the wall at nine before the joint started and had read it correctly.

**One thousand and one hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight.**

**"Eight on, and Saturday is an even morning, and it is the three hundred and ninetieth figure of the run,"** he said. **"A hundred and eighty-four and a hundred and seventy-three, and that is three hundred and fifty-seven, and I have been saying a hundred and eighty-four out loud since Wednesday and it is not a number I have said before and I checked it twice."**

The four bodies of households stand at three hundred and eighty-eight on the long sheet, and the clause beneath it in the smaller hand takes the three hundred and eighty-seventh out of three hundred and eighty-ninth. The near board says seven hundred and seventy-four and the far one says eight hundred and twenty-one. The drawer behind the near one stood shut at every hour of the day the yard had no water in, and the key to it was on the nail in the one room door at every hour of it.

Marek did not say the two things at any hour of Saturday, and Nia Vale did not ask him at any hour of Saturday, and at half past five he came and stood at the step where the three wet places were drying and said the rest of it out loud to the yard.

**"I am not saying the two things and I am not going to tell this yard why today, and I have got no reason and I have not looked for one, which is the first time in ten mornings of mornings that I have said that. The iron is wrong in a way I did not know about at ten o'clock this morning and I know about it now, and the second morning is tomorrow, and the tap stays off tomorrow until it does not need to be off, and I would like the yard to hear the shape of the day before I start it rather than after."**

**"Then tomorrow,"** the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden said, **"and I would rather have the water off for two mornings than have a piece of iron with sacking in it for another fortnight, and I have been carrying water up nine hundred yards eleven times today and I will do it again tomorrow and the day after if that is what it takes."**

The three sheets on the long table have nine inches of bare board either side of each, and the first of them is eighty-seven days old and lying face up, and no column has been ruled under any of them in a single book in this building. Fifty-three requests and fifty-three section-nine notes are in their two columns and have taken none of them, and this holding's book has seven entered sessions, and Saturday was not one of them.

The board in the one room carries thirty-nine blanks and its second rule stands open and empty underneath them, and a rule that comes back empty opens nothing.

Six rows are ruled on the paper headed with what this holding does not know, and every one of them is bare, and there is nothing cut for a seventh below them, and the four things anybody has ever asked a seventh row for are not going to be cut on a morning when a man has put a piece of iron into a woman's water on purpose. Four lines stand under two words in that corner and have never taken a word, and the terms sheet carries a third column, ruled and empty, under a title nobody has improved.

The compost line has paid standing in front of twenty-nine and has been discharged in nothing and will not be for twenty-six mornings, and a Saturday with no water in the holding has no more to do with that line than any other Saturday.

The use log holds fifteen lines and the sixteenth has never been written, and the barrow carries eleven journeys in chalk down its side, and eleven is a floor, and the barrow went out eleven times today with water in it and came back with water in it, and its eleven is a number about journeys and not about cans.

The man of about seventy has not been fetched by anybody and nobody has put a question to him, and the twenty-nine fetchings of him stand where four years ago left them. Sixty-six sits in the seventh column of a door nine hundred yards off, and it was not read today and it does not come down.

The ladder went no rungs and there is no figure against either of the two people on it. Nothing has been done about the offer on the low board, which is neither dated nor moved nor taken up, and certainly not taken back. The ring of bare ground has taken no boot and no measurement, and what is standing on the low field is land of its own and does not join that ring.

The clerk turned the yard key at noon and at dusk, and the second key was still on its nail in the one room door at noon and again at dusk, and the man of about fifty carried a pail at dusk and went past the nail and looked at the key on it and did not touch it, and the latch was still up two inches at the gate and nobody had pushed it down.

Taper lay on the plank on two upturned cans at the end of the day with the thread cut off it and the two ends of iron beside it with nothing fitted, and the wet on the step had gone in the wind and left three pale rings, and nobody in that yard wiped them out and nobody wrote any of it down, and at six in the morning the two ends of the iron were still on the plank and Marek was already at the vice.
