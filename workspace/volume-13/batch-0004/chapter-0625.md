# Chapter 625

## The Twenty-Eighth Of The Sixteenth, And Six Hundred And Thirteen Hundredweight Up The Fen Road Without Two Men In The Yard

The cycle came round in the night and Sennik Vaul had the board out on the bench at half past seven with the frost still on the ground and the load not due until two.

**"A hundred and thirty-six, twenty-nine taken and a hundred and seven not,"** he said. **"It came up by itself. I have said the same eleven words four times in this run of mornings and I am not going to find new ones, and I am not going to blame anybody for a field that has not been taken, and that is the whole of what a fourth morning is."**

**"Take a field,"** said the man of about fifty, from the gate, without turning round.

The yard stopped.

**"Not you,"** Marek said. **"You have been in this yard six weeks and you have held a gate and read a wall and carried a bucket. If you take a field on the strength of a number that came up in the night, then from this morning a man with a number is a man with a field, and that is a rule nobody in this holding voted for."**

**"I know that,"** the man of about fifty said. **"I said it out loud so that if anybody in this yard was going to say it, it would have been said before the load got here and not after."**

Sera Quill had the two reckonings on the slate and this morning she had written the third line under them in a different hand from the rest, because the clerk had offered to write it and she had said she would.

It said: the second of these two is the same figure as Tuesday with the date on it changed, and nobody has ever shown anybody a rule that moves it.

**"I am not putting a word under that saying it is carried forward,"** she said, **"because a rule is what moves a figure and there is not one here, and I am not going to write down that a figure moves because I wrote it down twice. Thirteen apart, and it has been thirteen apart every time, and I am not going to be the one who says the gap closed and it did not."**

**"Then it is copied,"** the clerk said, **"and a copied figure is not nothing, it is a figure with no rule behind it, and I would rather this holding had nine hundred copied figures than one figure that somebody has quietly given a reason to."**

Renn Ashby stood in the doorway at seven with the book shut under his arm and said one sentence and no figure at all, and she heard it from the tap and answered him across forty feet of yard.

**"The run is not read on an even morning and there is no number on this wall for it today,"** the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden said. **"You have said that out loud on four mornings this week and I heard it on all four, and today you said it in a different voice, and I would like you to know that I have noticed and I am not going to ask you why."**

The wall was read at nine by Harlan Vetch because the clerk was hoarse again, and Harlan Vetch read it off the chalk and got the ordinal wrong and did not know he had got it wrong until the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden told him at the tap.

**One thousand and one hundred and ninety-six hundredweight.**

**"Eight on, and Thursday is an even morning, and the ordinal is the three hundred and eighty-eighth of the run,"** the clerk said from the rack. **"A hundred and eighty-three and a hundred and seventy-two, which is three hundred and fifty-five. Nobody has to add the two boards to that and nobody has to add the aggregate to it either."**

**"Harlan said three hundred and eighty-seventh,"** the woman of about thirty-eight said.

**"I did,"** Harlan Vetch said. **"And I would like it noted that I said it from the chalk with the light coming off it at an angle, and I am not going to be told about a number twice in one morning by two people."**

**"Then it is in your ears and the chalk is in your hand and the book is on the bench and that is three ways of holding one figure in this holding,"** the clerk said, **"and two of them were right."**

The four bodies of households stood at three hundred and eighty-six on the long sheet at nine, and the clause in the smaller hand under the line took the three hundred and eighty-fifth out of three hundred and eighty-seventh. Both men at the gate could see that number from the road and neither of them said it out loud. The two boards at the ends of the wall stood at seven hundred and seventy-two and eight hundred and nineteen, with the drawer shut behind the near one.

At two the load came up the fen road. It came past four houses before it got to the gate, and the man of about fifty and Renn Ashby had been on the gate bench since nine, and this time they had gone out to meet it at the last house on the corner so that they were standing in the road when it turned.

The clerk put his hand flat on the rack and told the yard the three figures behind it were for noon in the one room and not in a gateway, and the reason he gave was the same reason he gave last time, which is that a man shouting three numbers in a gateway gets the same three numbers wrong twice in a week.

**"Read them yourself,"** the man of about fifty said, when the load was down and the barrow was off. **"I have never read them. I read a number off a door nine hundred yards off on Monday and I have not been able to put it down since, and I would rather have three figures in my own mouth that I cannot check than go on hearing other men read them."**

So he read them at noon in the one room, out of the rack, with a finger, and slowly.

**"The one that is bare is the second ruled line in Silling's own book, and it says two hundred and seventy-six days. The register form is two hundred and eighty-two. The charter behind it is a hundred and eighty-eight, and I have been coming in here four years and that line has not had a thing on it for any of them."**

**"You are the first person in this holding who has read them and then said how long they have been empty,"** the clerk said.

**"I have been counting a line of my own for a year and Silling came and asked me in the middle of it and I have not got over it,"** the man of about fifty said, and put the finger back on the charter, and left it there a moment, and then took it off.

**"Six hundred and thirteen hundredweight,"** he said, reading the board the load had come in on. **"That is up eleven on the last one, and the last one was six hundred and two, and the interval between the two of them is nine days and the step in the load is eleven, and those are two different numbers and I am not going to have anybody in this yard print them as one interval."**

The two halves of it were checked twice, once in the gateway and once in the one room, and both checks came out the same, and they were not added to each other, and they were not added to anything. The fiftieth pair of names went on the board in the one room in the place it has gone for four years.

Then the clerk went and looked at the second rule, and it stood open and empty, the way it has stood every time a load has come up that road since anybody in this holding can remember, and a rule that comes back empty opens nothing.

**"Thirty-nine blanks on that board this morning,"** he said. **"Thirty-nine this afternoon. It is the same thirty-nine it has been since I came here and it has not moved on any of the sixty loads that have come up that road while it stood there, and the next one is on Saturday week, and I am not going to be a man who fills one because a number went up in the night."**

Marek was in the seed house with Tova Reed from nine until two and he did not go out to the road at any hour, and he came out at two with the last tray and stood at the step.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning,"** he said, **"and the reason is that a load came up this road and I was not in the yard for it, and that is the third time in ten mornings, and a man who greets a load with two large things is a man who is pleased with himself about a load."**

**"You carried eleven trays,"** Tova Reed said.

**"I carried eleven trays,"** he said, **"and I would rather somebody in this yard counted that than counted me."**

At four the load had gone and the man of about fifty was still at the gate with the hook in his hand, and the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden was emptying the butt into two cans because Saturday is coming and Saturday wants more water than a butt holds.

He came across the yard to where she was doing it and asked her a question about the standpipe, which is at the far end of the tap house and is the one piece of water in this holding that nobody in this holding holds.

**"Who looks after that one,"** he said. **"Because Marek is going to have the tap shut off for most of the day on Saturday and if that standpipe is going as well then the yard is drawing from the well house for ten hours, and I have been at the gate all week saying the far end of this holding is somebody else's problem, and I would like to know whose."**

**"The far end of a thing is held by two people and neither of them is me, and no instrument anywhere in four counties says whether it is on or off,"** she said, and tipped the butt, and the can filled.

She did not stop what she was doing when she said it and she did not look at him afterwards, and a man came past with a barrow and asked her whether she wanted the second can carried for her, and she said yes, and he carried it, and nobody in that yard said one word about anything else, and the man at the gate stood where he was for a while and then put the hook on the wall and did not repeat it.

There are three sheets on the long table. The first of them is eighty-five days old and lying face up, the second is folded, and the third is headed with a name and dated and signed and sealed. About nine inches of bare board is left either side of each one, and there has not been a column ruled underneath any of them in any of the four books in this building. Fifty-three requests and fifty-three section-nine notes are in their two columns, and seven sessions are entered in this holding's book, and Thursday was not one of them.

Thirty-nine blanks are on the board in the one room and its second rule is open and empty. The sheet of what this holding does not know has six ruled rows with nothing cut below the sixth, and the four things a seventh row has been asked about are not spoken on a morning when a load has come up the road. Four bare lines stand under two words in that corner, and the third column of the sheet of terms is ruled and empty beneath a heading nobody has improved.

Paid is written in front of twenty-nine on the compost line and nothing has gone out in it, and it will not go out for twenty-six mornings, and a load coming up a road has no more to do with that line than a man standing in a gateway. The log on its shelf holds fifteen lines and has never had a sixteenth, and the barrow says eleven journeys, and eleven is a floor.

Nobody went for the man of about seventy and nothing was put to him, and the twenty-nine fetchings of him are where four years ago left them. The seventh column of a door nine hundred yards off carries sixty-six, and it was not read on this morning, and it does not come down.

Not one rung of that ladder was climbed and no figure is written against either of the two people belonging to it. The offer on the low board is undated, and it has not been moved, taken up or taken back. The ring of bare ground took no foot and carries no figure, and what is standing on the low field is a different piece of ground and the two are never added together.

The clerk turned the yard key at noon and at dusk, and on his way past the doorframe at dusk the key to the drawer was on its nail, and it had been on that nail at every hour of the morning the load came and at every hour of the afternoon it went, and the drawer behind the near board stood shut through a Thursday that moved three figures in the one room and one in a gateway and nothing at all on the board in the corner.

Pins had gone through the gate with the load and come back without him, and the man of about fifty took the hook off the wall and hung it on the nail beside the key without touching the key, and went home, and the latch was still up two inches, and the second can of water stood under the tap with the top of it level and not one drop over.
