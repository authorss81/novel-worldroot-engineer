# Chapter 626

## The Twenty-Nine Of The Sixteenth, And A Man Shown A Rack And Asked A Question With No Rule Behind It

Friday was the day before the joint and the yard knew it by the water.

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden had the butt filled to the top and two cans standing beside it and a third can on the seed house step that nobody claimed, and the man of about fifty came at the well house at seven with a barrow to start moving what was in it, which nobody had asked him to do.

**"One hundred and sixty-five mornings of three hundred and thirteen,"** Renn Ashby said, from the doorway, with the page turned to him and the stone on the corner, and the man of about fifty at the well house heard all of it and the rest of the yard was already at the water and did not.

Six voices went with him on the last sentence, and the woman of about thirty-eight shouted hers from the top of the butt a hundred yards off and got it right.

The clerk read the wall at nine and had to stop in the middle of it.

**One thousand and one hundred and ninety-one hundredweight.**

**"Five off, and Friday is an odd morning, and it is the three hundred and eighty-ninth of the run,"** he said, and then stopped, and started again from the halves.

**"A hundred and eighty-three and a hundred and seventy-three,"** he said. **"Between them three hundred and fifty-six. I am sorry. I have been at that wall for four years and I have lost my place in the middle of a sentence and I would rather say so than come out of the other end of it pretending."**

**"You lost your place because the two figures you were going to say are not the two figures that are on the wall,"** Harlan Vetch said from the wall, without any particular weight in it.

**"Then that is why I lost my place,"** the clerk said, **"and I am not going to be a man who reads the book out of habit on a morning when the book is right and he is not."**

He read the book and the two figures were a hundred and eighty-three and a hundred and seventy-three, and the figure on the wall was a hundred and eighty-two and a hundred and seventy-three, and nobody could say which of them had been on the wall since dark, and the clerk wrote both on the slate and neither on the wall, and left it.

Three hundred and eighty-seven is chalked on the long sheet that belongs to the four bodies of households, and the small hand under the line makes the three hundred and eighty-sixth out of three hundred and eighty-eighth. The board at this end of the wall says seven hundred and seventy-three and the one at the far end says eight hundred and twenty, and behind the near board there is a drawer that is shut, and its key is on a nail in the frame of the one room door.

Nia Vale read the three ages at eleven with her coat buttoned to the throat and her right elbow held hard down against her side, and she read them one at a time off the rack with the arm that was free, and she would not take the folded page out of the inside pocket to hold it flat, and Harlan Vetch made a joke about it that was not unkind.

**"You are reading three numbers off a shelf with one arm,"** he said. **"There is a table six feet away and a woman in this holding who is going to have both her hands full of water on Saturday, and if you put that paper on that table I will fetch you a chair."**

**"It is not on the table,"** she said, **"and it is not going to be on the table on Saturday either, and you may fetch me the chair."**

**"The register form is two hundred and eighty-three days old,"** she said, one at a time off the rack with the arm that was free. **"The charter behind it is a hundred and eighty-nine. The second ruled line in Silling's own book is two hundred and seventy-seven, and that one is bare, and I have been counting things off that rack one-handed since a Tuesday and I have never once got one wrong and I am not going to start."**

**"You have never once had anybody watching you do it,"** Harlan Vetch said.

**"Then this is a new morning for both of us,"** she said.

Marek had the taper finished on Friday morning and lying on the bench in the open air, and he had two pieces of iron trued and a piece of soap and a length of cord, and he told the yard in the middle of the day that he was going to cut it in the cold.

**"You have a fire in the tool house and a vice and forty years of doing this and you are going to do it in the open on a Friday,"** the woman of about thirty-eight said.

**"I am going to do it in the open on Saturday with you standing at the tap,"** Marek said, **"because a Saturday morning is the only morning of the week that this yard is all in one place, and I am not going to do the first half of a job in a warm room on Friday night and have everybody in this holding think they saw the whole of it."**

**"Cold iron is a bad thing to tap."**

**"Cold iron is a worse thing to tap and I am going to do it anyway, and I have not cut one of these since I was twenty-two, and if it is wrong then it is wrong in a way that I will know how to fix and not a way I will have to guess about."**

Tova Reed came out with the last of the trays at two and asked him the one question about Saturday that nobody else had asked, which was whether the seed work still went out while the tap was off.

**"It goes out at the hour it goes out,"** Marek said. **"You are not losing a tray because of a piece of iron. What you lose is the standpipe at the far end of the tap house, and so does everybody else, and the yard is drawing from the well house from six."**

**"Then I will work on the side of it where I can see the water coming,"** she said, **"and I would like to know now, before Saturday, not on Saturday, which pail is the well house and which pail is the butt, because a woman should not have to learn a yard's water on the morning it is all moved."**

**"Red pail is the well house,"** the woman of about thirty-eight said. **"Blue is the butt. Black is the standpipe and there will not be a black pail worth anything after ten o'clock."**

Marek did not go near the wall on Friday. He spent the afternoon in the tool house with the two pieces of iron and did not cut anything, and at four o'clock he came out and stood at the end of the long table with his hands empty.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning,"** he said, **"and the reason belongs to somebody else in this yard and not to me. A man stood in the one room yesterday and read three numbers out of that rack and told the room how long the empty line has been empty, and a man who does that is doing something careful and small, and I am not going to stand up in the same yard and be loud at a man who has been careful."**

**"You have been loud at nobody for a fortnight,"** Nia Vale said.

**"And the fortnight is doing me good and I am not going to spoil it on the eve of a Saturday,"** he said.

The man of about fifty was in the one room at half past four with the clerk, and he had not asked to be and he stood in front of the rack for a long time without touching it, and then he said the thing that had been in him since Thursday.

**"Is thirty-nine a floor,"** he said. **"Because if it is a floor then it is the only figure in this building I understand and I will leave it alone for the rest of my life. And if it is not a floor then it is a number somebody once wrote down and stopped at, and I would like to know which of those two things I am standing in front of."**

**"I have been the clerk here four years and I cannot tell you,"** the clerk said. **"It has not moved while I have been here and I have never seen anybody put a name in one of them and I have never seen one come out. That is all I have got and I am not going to give you a rule that does not exist to make this morning easier."**

**"Then it is a floor for me,"** the man of about fifty said, **"and I am not going to be the man who puts the fortieth name in that board in the six weeks I have just given you notice about, and if I am still here when somebody does put one, then it will not have been me."**

He said it to the rack and not to the clerk, and then he went out and got a barrow and did the last of the water.

There are three sheets on the long table, the first of them eighty-six days old and face up, and about nine inches of board either side of each one, and no column has been ruled under any of them in any book in this building. Fifty-three requests stand in one column and fifty-three section-nine notes in the other. This holding's book has seven entered sessions, and Friday was not one of them.

The board in the one room carries thirty-nine blanks and its second rule stands open and empty underneath them. The six rows ruled against what this holding does not know are bare, and there is nothing cut for a seventh, and four lines stand ruled under two words in that corner and have never taken a word, and beside it a third column stands ruled and empty under a title that nobody has improved.

Paid is written in front of twenty-nine on the compost line and has been discharged in nothing, and it will not be for twenty-six mornings, and a Friday before a joint is not an interval. Fifteen lines are in the use log and nothing has been written on the sixteenth, and the barrow has eleven journeys in chalk on its side, and eleven is a floor.

The man of about seventy has not been fetched and has not been asked anything. On a door nine hundred yards off, in the seventh column, sixty-six is scratched, and it was read on Monday by one man and has not been read since, and it does not come down.

The ladder was not climbed and nothing stands written against either of the two people on it. The offer on the low board lies there undated, untaken, and not taken back. The ring of bare ground has never had a boot in it and carries no figure at all, and the low field is another piece of ground and the two are never added together.

The clerk turned the yard key at noon and again at dusk. The second key has not left the nail in the one room door at any hour of the day, and the drawer behind the near board stood shut at every hour of the last ordinary morning before the joint.

Trowel was still in the frost on the north end of the wall on Friday morning where Harlan Vetch had left it, and it was still there at dusk, and two stones of the third course were still up off the bed, and he had said he would not put them back until the bed was deep enough and he had not measured how deep that is and never would.
