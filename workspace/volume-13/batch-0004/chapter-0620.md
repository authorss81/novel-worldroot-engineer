# Chapter 620

## The Twenty-Third Of The Sixteenth, And A Number Read Into A Yard Before The Light Was Up

Saturday was the first of the two mild days and the frost that had been standing in the ruts by the gate was gone by seven, and the yard came into the middle road out of the blue with the lamps still on in two houses.

Renn Ashby read the run at the seventh hour out of the doorway with the page turned to him and the stone on the corner of the board, and the only person in the yard awake to hear it was the man of about fifty, who had been at the gate since half past five with a lamp and a hook and nothing to hook.

**"One hundred and sixty-two mornings of three hundred and seven,"** Renn Ashby said. **"Read it as two figures and not as a rate. The first of them steps by one and the second of them steps by two, and a yard that takes a half of that away with it has been told something true and used it wrong."**

The man of about fifty had the hook down by then and came and stood in the doorway and said the last sentence of it with him, which is the first time in four years that a man who does not read the book has said the last sentence of it.

**"I have been at that gate since half past five and I have said the end of a pair of numbers I cannot check,"** he said. **"I would like somebody to tell me that is not a thing people do round here."**

**"It is a thing four people do round here now,"** Renn Ashby said, **"and you are the fifth, and you got it right, and I am not going to make a ceremony of it at seven in the morning with one lamp lit."**

The clerk read the wall at nine, and this morning he did not read it from the chalk.

He had the book open on the bench and read the figure out of the book, and after the figure he put his finger on the line in the book and then on the line on the wall, and the two were the same figure, and he said so.

**One thousand and one hundred and eighty-two hundredweight.**

**"Five off yesterday, and Saturday is an odd morning, and this is the three hundred and eighty-third of the run,"** he said. **"A hundred and eighty and a hundred and seventy, and they make three hundred and fifty, and I have added them out loud before I said them this morning and I did it once."**

The two halves went up on the wall in the order they are written, which is not the order that makes them, and Harlan Vetch came past at ten and said the sum before either of them got to it.

**"Three hundred and fifty,"** he said, and went on down the wall.

**"That is the fourth morning running somebody has got there first and I have stopped minding,"** the clerk said.

Three hundred and eighty-one is chalked on the wall sheet that belongs to the four bodies of households, and the sentence in small letters below it makes the three hundred and eightieth out of three hundred and eighty-second, one on each side of the chalk. Along the wall the two boards stand at seven hundred and sixty-seven and eight hundred and fourteen, and there is a drawer in the masonry behind the nearer one, and its key is on a nail inside the one room door, and it did not move all morning.

The three ages went out on a slate and went as far as the gate.

The clerk wrote them at half past eight because he could say them better than he could be heard saying them, and Sera Quill carried the slate out to the gate on account of the woman of about thirty-eight being at the tap, and the man of about fifty read it standing with his hand on the bar.

**"Two hundred and seventy-seven days, and that is the register form. The charter behind it is a hundred and eighty-three. Silling's own second ruled line is two hundred and seventy-one days, and nothing has ever been put on it, and nothing is owed on it either."**

He read it twice, the second time because he said the middle one was not the one he had expected in the middle, and the slate went back to the one room and was read again at the wall at nine by the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden, and the two readings were the same three figures and the yard checked them against each other for a while and then left it alone.

The latch was lifted for the fourth time at ten, and the fourth time was the one she had said would not need asking for, and she did not ask.

Marek spent the morning in the tool house with the frame saw and two files and did not come out to the yard except to fetch the water, and when Nia Vale brought the trays out at eleven he was sitting on the bench with a piece of iron in his hand and a candle stub beside him.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning and the reason is a plain one that will not interest anybody,"** he said. **"Saturday is the loudest morning in this yard. There is a saw going and a barrow going and nine people who all want something said to them, and I have found out in these ten mornings that the two things I do not say will not land in that. I am not going to spend them on a morning that has to shout."**

**"You have said that on other quiet mornings,"** she said.

**"Then they will wait for another quiet morning,"** he said, **"and there will be one, because a Monday in this holding is quieter than any Saturday and quieter than any Sunday, and I am not going to throw them at a Monday either."**

He went back in and the saw started again inside the tool house, and the yard got used to it the way a yard gets used to anything that is on purpose.

Soren Rill found out at noon what the latch had done to his day. He had been taking the barrow out through that gate four times a year and always straight, and with the bar up two inches and a pail-sized gap in it he could not get the barrow through square at all.

He did it the long way four times and did it grumbling, and the barrow went back against the wall at the end of it with the same eleven journeys in chalk down its side, and eleven is a floor and no twelfth mark went on it. The use log on its shelf in the one room has fifteen lines in it, and the margin under the fifteenth is clean, and nobody found a Saturday reason to write a sixteenth.

The corner of the one room is where the ruled paper lives, and on Saturday two people looked at it for no reason anybody could name. Four lines sit under a heading of two words and have never held a word. Beside them a column has been ruled on the terms sheet with a title over it that nobody has improved, and it is still blank. Further along, the sheet of what this holding does not know carries six ruled rows, all of them bare, and below the sixth there is paper with nothing cut on it.

Eighty days is how long the first of the three sheets on the long table has been lying face up, and the other two have not shifted in that time either. Each of them has about the width of two hands of bare board on either side, and not one has had a column ruled underneath it in any of the four books. Fifty-three requests went into one column and fifty-three section-nine notes into the other, and neither of those columns has ever asked for a sheet that was lying out in the open in front of them. Seven sessions are entered in this holding's book and Saturday was not one of them.

Thirty-nine blanks are ruled on the board in the one room and they were thirty-nine at seven in the morning and thirty-nine at six in the evening, and the rule that sits below them came back with nothing on it, which is a thing that opens nothing.

Paid stands in front of twenty-nine on the compost line and nothing has come out of it. Twenty-six mornings from the last time it moved is a Saturday a fortnight off, and a Saturday has never once moved that line in four years.

Soren Rill went past the ladder twice on Saturday because of the gate, and did not stop at it on either occasion, and the two people who belong to it went home at dusk with nothing written against either of them. The offer on the low board has lain undated and unclaimed since before his barrow had eleven journeys on its side. The ring of bare ground took no foot at any hour and carries no figure of any kind, and the ground that came up on the low field belongs to a different piece of ground and the two are never added.

At dusk the man of about fifty came off the gate and stopped at the nail in the one room door for as long as he stopped at it last week, and did not touch the key, and went home.

Butt had been emptied once and filled once that day and had three days of water in it, and the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden stood over it at dusk with the handle cold under her hand and said that a Saturday was the only morning of the week she would take a butt down for a man who had said he would be at a gate on Monday.
