# Chapter 610

## The Thirteenth Of The Sixteenth, And A Figure Read Off The Wrong Line By A New Hand

The rain had gone by seven and left the yard full of hard grey light and standing water, and the clerk was at the wall at nine with a slate under his arm instead of a voice.

He wrote two lines on it, turned it round, and held it up.

**"I can talk. I cannot be heard. If I say this at the wall then the man at the back has to be told twice, and he will be told once and it will be wrong."**

Hesta Lyle read the wall first, because on Tuesday the woman of about thirty-eight had read it and the yard had decided on its own that this week the reading was not going to be one man's job.

**One thousand and one hundred and sixty-seven hundredweight.**

**"Five off,"** she said. **"Wednesday is an odd morning. It is the three hundred and seventy-third of the run and it has not used that number before, and I have counted the words out loud twice this morning so I know I have not dropped one."**

Underneath it the halves stood a hundred and seventy-five and a hundred and sixty-five, and she added them out loud the first time wrongly, and Renn Ashby told her which way round to do it, and she did it the other way and got three hundred and forty.

She put her finger on the two figures and then took it off them.

**"A man does not need to be a clerk to add two numbers and he does not need to be anything else to get it wrong first,"** she said.

At the seventh hour the run was read out of the second book in the doorway, and the clerk opened it and put his finger on the line and could not make a sound, and Renn Ashby, who had been at the gate since six, stepped in and read it.

**"One hundred and fifty-seven mornings of"**

There was a silence in the doorway of about the length it takes to find out something.

**"No,"** the clerk said, in the voice he had left, and Renn Ashby put his finger higher up the page and swore at himself in a very ordinary way.

**"I have been reading the line above the run all week. The page is open at two and the light is behind me and I have been saying the line above it every morning and nobody has noticed because I have been stopping before the end of it and hoping nobody minded."**

**"Then say the end of it,"** Nia Vale said, and turned the page round to face him and put a stone on the corner of it so that it would stay.

Renn Ashby read the pair again with the page facing him and got it right.

**"One hundred and fifty-seven and two hundred and ninety-seven. That is not a percentage and neither of them is one against the other in any arithmetic, and the first of them steps by one on an odd morning and the second one steps by two, and a man who writes it down as a rate will be wrong in a way that takes him four counties to walk."**

Somebody in the yard laughed, and Renn Ashby said the thing he had come in to say before he could stop himself.

**"I have held that gate since the eighth and I have read that book for two mornings, and I am telling you now that I want to read it tomorrow, because a figure that one man says is a thing this holding has one pair of ears on and it has had them for four years."**

The clerk looked at him for a while and then wrote on the slate, which he held up at the same angle he had held it at before.

**"Seven o'clock. Page turned to you. Every morning you are here."**

The woman of about thirty-eight came off the tap and asked for the wall sheet of the four bodies of households and read it standing with the wet coming off her sleeves onto the long table.

**"Three hundred and seventy-one on the line. And under it the three hundred and seventieth out of the three hundred and seventy-second, which is yesterday and the day after, and not this morning, and there is a man in this yard who has been adding the two together for a month."**

**"I have not,"** Harlan Vetch said, from the wall, without turning round, and went on with the course he was laying.

Seven hundred and fifty-seven at this end of the wall and eight hundred and four at the other, and the gap between them is the gap between them and always has been, and the drawer behind the near board stayed shut through a morning of standing water and gone frost, with a key on a nail in the one room door and the man of about fifty walking past it twice.

Marek came out of the one room at the ninth hour and stood at the doorway while Renn Ashby read the second book out again for Nia Vale, who wanted it once more because she had been the one who turned the page.

The clerk put the three ages on his slate rather than say them, and Nia Vale took the slate and counted them off on her fingers against the rack, one document at a time, matching each number to the paper it belonged to.

**"Two hundred and sixty-seven on that form. A hundred and seventy-three on the charter. Two hundred and sixty-one days on the second ruled line of Silling's own book, with nothing on it and nothing due on it."**

She looked up from her fingers with her mouth slightly open and held the slate out in front of her so that the whole yard could read it.

**"They agree with what he wrote on the slate before I counted them. He wrote them down first and I got them off the paper after, and they are the same three numbers."**

**"They have been the same three numbers every morning for four years,"** the clerk said, in the voice he had, which at two feet was a whisper with the shape of a sentence in it.

Marek did not go near the wall after that. He took himself down to the soft end of the middle road with a bottle and a length of cord and sat on the step with his back to the yard, and Nia Vale came out with two trays and stopped.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning and I will not tell you a reason that is worth anything,"** he said. **"A man read a figure wrong out of a doorway in front of eleven people two hours ago and eleven people let him read it again. I do not want to be the one thing in this holding that gets handled more gently than a number."**

She set the trays down on the step beside him and took one of them back and went in.

Nia Vale was the one who went through the standing of the one room that afternoon while she waited for the light, and she did it out loud to herself because it is quicker than saying it is unchanged. Seven sessions are entered in this holding's book and none of them was entered on Wednesday.

**"Fifty-three requests in one column. Fifty-three section-nine notes in the other. Neither of those two columns has ever had a sheet put into it, and the three on the long table are the only three things in this building nobody has had the nerve to write down. The first of them has lain face up for seventy days and nobody has looked at it as a piece of paper rather than as a problem."**

She went the length of the one room with her hand trailing the edge of the bench and stopped at the board in the corner. Thirty-nine blanks on it, and the rule underneath them standing open with nothing on it.

**"Thirty-nine and the same as last week and the week before, and a rule that comes back with nothing on it is not a thing anybody has to answer for."**

Four lines stand ruled under two words in that same corner and they are as bare as the third column of the sheet of terms, whose heading has not been improved. Six rows are ruled on what this holding does not know and the sixth is the last one, and there is space cut below it for a seventh and nothing cut for the seventh.

Soren Rill took the barrow out at four to move seed and brought it back with the same eleven journeys in chalk on its side, and eleven is a floor and there was no twelfth. The use log in the one room has fifteen lines in it with a clean margin beneath, and sixty-six is scratched in the seventh column of a door nine hundred yards off and has not been read this morning and does not go back, and the man of about seventy stands at twenty-nine fetchings with nothing asked of him and nothing done to him.

The compost line went on carrying paid against twenty-nine through the whole of Wednesday, and no part of it went out, and it will not go out before the interval arrives.

Renn Ashby looked at the ladder when he came off the gate and said what is true of it without looking at it for longer than that. The offer on the low board has not been read out or dated or moved or taken back. Nothing stood in the ring of bare ground and nothing stands against it in figures, and the ground that came back on the low field belongs to a different question altogether.

The clerk locked up at noon with his left hand and turned the yard key, and Nia Vale found a stone at the corner of the second book where Renn Ashby had left it, and moved it to the shelf where stones are kept, and left the book open at the run.

Frost came back over the yard between three and four, white on the water and white on the rims of the cans and white on the edge of the steps, and it was gone again by the time the light went, and it left nothing behind it that anybody in this holding was going to be asked about.
