# Chapter 14

## Four Seasons In Four Layers

They went in at first light on Sunday, because there was no other light to go in at, and because Ilsa Kern had said on Saturday night that a tar pit in the middle of the season is a job you do early or not at all.

The wind was off the north-east and it was four below freezing by the time the water came off, and the pump shed had a brazier in it that Sera Quill had made them light at half past five, and the mill boy had brought two more from the mill.

The pad came off in four layers, and that was not a thing Marek had known before he was standing over it.

He had thought of it as a cake. Four coats, four seasons, one lump, and you get a bar under it and lift. But it was not one lump, and the reason it was not one lump was that it had never bonded to anything at all, which was the whole of his argument from the first morning, and each of the four seasons had run down over the one below and stopped a finger short of its edge, and so each one had set a lip, and four lips meant four sheets, and each sheet came off whole if you got under it right.

"You cannot lift," Sera Quill said. "Look at the low side. The third coat has run down and gone back under the first. Lift the first and you take the second with it, and you take an inch of wood with the second. Four inches of edge all round this wound and two hands of face. You would strip it in a morning and there would be nothing left to seal."

"Then how."

"You go in at the boundary." She crouched on the rim with the lamp off and her hand flat over the shape of it, which she did not let Marek copy. "Between the first coat and the second, where it has run down, there is a line of nothing. No tar bonds to the edge of the tar above it; it sits on it. Put the bar in there, along the line, not under the lip, and lift the sheet half an inch and stop, and take the sheet off in pieces from the top down. Take the top one off first. Work down. Never work up."

They worked down.

It took six hours for the top two, and it was the top two that were hard, and the top two came off in pieces about a foot across, and each piece was a hard black shell with a curve in it like a dried leaf and the underside of it was the color of the inside of a walnut and had a bloom on it that was four seasons old, and when a piece came off it went on the boards and Marek could stand it up against the well house wall in a row and see the whole of the cooperative's history of that wound in four standing pieces.

The third coat came off in water. The tar in it had gone to soup in the flood and had got under the wound and lifted itself off the wood, and it had made a black soup of the bottom half of the pit, and the water in the pit was the color and the consistence of cold gravy, and the only way to get the third coat off was to get water onto it and heat, and let it go.

That was the whole of the second half of the day: three of them on the brazier and the hot water and the flat knife, working a wet black paste off a wound in the dark in a pit four feet deep, and the fourth of them hauling the buckets up and tipping them on the embankment, and it took four hundred buckets, and the tar in the tip came back on the embankment as a black crust in the cold and the mill boy scraped it off with a spade and it went in a barrow and it was not on anybody's holding.

Ilsa Kern did the flat knife, because she was the best at it, and she had done eleven seasons of that same flat work in the last eleven years and she held it the way you hold a thing you have done ten thousand times. Marek worked beside her with a hook and a bar and did what he was told.

And at the fourth hour, with the third coat off and the fourth — the base, the one from four seasons back, the one that was actually holding — still on and half in the water, Jonas Byre came down the ladder to take the bar off him, and got to the third board, and stopped.

He did not say anything. He put one hand on the rail and stood there, and Marek watched his shoulders go up and stay up, and Ilsa Kern said "Jonas," in a completely different voice from any she had used all day, and came up the ladder past him, and the four of them had him off the ladder and onto the boards in about a second and a half, and he went down onto his side on the boards with his hand on his chest and made a noise that nobody in the pit enjoyed.

Anneke Roos got the lamp. Gael Senn got him flat. Ilsa Kern got his collar open with two fingers, fast, without being told, because she had done it before.

"He is going," Anneke Roos said.

"He is not," Ilsa said. "Jonas. Jonas. Look at me. You are going to swear at me and then you are going to breathe out for longer than you breathe in, and if you do it the other way round I will be a great deal busier."

"I cannot get my leg," Jonas Byre said, to the boards. "I cannot get my leg where it is."

"Your leg is where it is because you are forty-eight years old and you went down four feet on a ladder in the cold on an empty stomach," said Ilsa Kern, "and it is not going anywhere, and I am going to get you up this ladder, and Mr Vale is going to hold the bottom and you are going to go up on your hands and knees like a dog and nobody is going to say a word about the dog."

They got him up on his hands and knees. Sera Quill had not left the rim for two hours, and when it came to hauling a man up a ladder she came down it herself and took the back of his coat, and did not say anything about it afterward. It took a long time. He stopped on the fourth board and swore at Ilsa Kern steadily and without heat, in the voice of a man reciting something he had learned, and then he came up, and then he sat on the rim of the pit with his legs hanging over it for about a minute, and Marek stood beside him and did not offer him a hand, because it was not the kind of thing you offered a man on a rim.

"I am not going down there again," Jonas Byre said, to the yard. "That is the end of that. I will carry, I will hold, I will count, I will read the dial, I will write down the hour, and I will not go down four feet ever again while there is a God in Tarrow Fen."

"All right," Marek said.

"I have forty acres in the low ground and a drain that is not finished," Jonas Byre said, "and I have just found out the price of a Sunday morning. That is all. Do not write it up as a thing about my chest, write it up as a thing about a man in a pit who should not have been in a pit."

Anneke Roos wrote the hour of it into the working notes before anybody had stopped shaking, and the licensed person present against that hour was Ms Quill, who had been standing on the rim of that pit from first light to dark and had not once been asked to be thanked for it and was not going to be.

So the crew was four.

The last coat came off at half past two.

It came off in a way none of them expected, and Ilsa Kern, who had been stripping ditches and revetments for forty years, stopped and stood back and said "well" out loud, which from her was a considerable event.

The fourth coat had not bonded to the wood either. It never had. It was lying on it. But it had been lying on it for four seasons, and the wood underneath had been pushing up a very small amount every time the wound breathed, and the two had met somewhere in the middle, and the fourth coat came off in one piece with a shallow bowl in the middle of it and the shape of the bowl was the shape of the lip that had grown over the top of the cut.

Marek got down off the ladder and looked at it for a long time.

The scarwood under it was pale. Not white — pale, and dry-looking, and clean, and there was a ridge of new wood across the top of the cut about a third of the way down the face, and the ridge was a quarter of an inch thick and it had the tight grain that new callus has and it had a wet line along its lower edge where it was still working.

"That is what she said," Marek said. "On Thursday. In front of you all. A third of the face, clean and pale, off the top. That is a third of the face, clean and pale, off the top."

"You believed her," said Anneke Roos.

"I believed her because she had been in the pit twenty minutes and I had been out of the pit ten days." He put his hand on the rail. "It is a third. It is a third of the face and it has got a wet line on it, which means it is not a scar, it is a job in progress. And if the whole face does that in a fortnight at the rate this one is doing it, then the wound in Monday's work is not the two hands it was on Thursday. It is a hand and a half. And everything we put in there is going in against wood that is still moving."

That was the discovery, and it was the last good thing in the day, and it was arrived at by accident, and it did not make anybody happy, because what it meant was that there was no holding still in the pit at all.

They had, at that moment, no compound, no tar, no resin, no gasket, nothing that any of them had ever seen used for anything except a wound that was not closing. What they had was four baskets of the stripped pad.

The fiber came out of the lower coats. That was the thing they had not known and that Ilsa Kern had known in about four seconds on Sunday morning and had said nothing about, because she wanted to see whether anybody else was paying attention. It was chopped fiber, the length of a finger, bound into the tar like straw in a daub, and it had been in there for four seasons under four coats, and the tar had gone round it and protected it and it had come out of the flood dry in the middle of a black soup, and it was not rotten, and it was the coarsest and best pack in the county.

"Test it," Sera Quill said.

Marek took a double handful of it and wrung it and squeezed a handful of water out of it and broke a length between his fingers and then broke another one with his thumbnail, and held the ends up, and they broke with a snap instead of a squash.

"That is pack," he said. "That is what goes in the hollow so the wet has somewhere to go and so the face does not sit in its own water. That is the one thing on this holding that is exactly what it wants to be. It has been in a tar pit for four years and it has never once been wet through."

They got four baskets of it out of the pit by six o'clock, spread on the boards of the pump shed in the cold to stiffen, and it stank, and it was the whole of the material.

Sera Quill stood in the shed doorway with her case under her arm and looked at four baskets of tar-smelling fiber and a wound with no coat on it and a date on the calendar.

"You have no tar," she said.

"No."

"You are not going to get any. There is no tar within eleven miles that is not somebody else's and you have five pounds and a half and a quarter, and if you buy a pot of it in Fennmere you will have bought four gallons and a debt, and if you send for it you will be out of your minds by Thursday." She was not being kind and she was not being cruel; she was reading out a sum. "So there is no tar. And without tar you cannot make a Crown seal, and if you try you will make something that looks like a Crown seal and will not hold, and it will not hold for the reason that everything else on this holding does not hold, which is that it will be put on by people who do not know what they are doing and then it will be logged as if they did."

"Tell me the way you would do it with nothing."

Sera Quill took her hands out of her coat.

"You restrain it," she said. "That is the whole answer and it has been the answer for two hundred years. A seal is not a filling. A seal is a fill *and* a restraint, and the restraint is the part that does the work, and the Crown does it with tar and a dead weight over the top and so they do not have to think about it. You do it with stakes. You cut four green withies and you drive them in at the corners of the frame into the sockets in the cradle — there are sockets, look at the cradle, they have been in that frame since before I was born and they have never once been used for anything. And you weave the withies and you tie them and you put sacking over the whole of it and you leave it tied. A thing that is tied cannot move, and a wound that cannot move closes, and a wound that can move weeps. It is not a seal in the district's sense. It will not go in a book as a seal. But it is one, and it is better than a pot of tar, and in a year you can cut the withies out and look at it, which you can never do with a pot of tar, and that is the difference between a repair and a coat."

"And the return."

"You have no return," Sera Quill said. "I will tell you what a return is and then you will tell me what you have, and it will be the same thing and it will not be enough, and you will do it anyway. A return is something that goes back in, on a day, in an amount, from a name. You have a wound that has been taking the cooperative's water for a hundred years and it has not had anything back in it since the flock went. So the return is labor and it is compost and neither of them exists. What you have is five people and four of them are one-time. What you have is a monthly obligation, and if you write a name against the first of every month and put a thing in that pit, then the return is the checking of it, and the checking is the return, and I have never in four hundred students seen a cooperative do that and I have seen every one of them fail for want of it."

Then she took out her page and put it on the bench, and Marek saw that it was the second sheet, and the second sheet had a heading at the top.

"Fourteen pounds," she said. "For the twentieth, for a finding, for a load test, and for the certificate, and the money before I get here and not after, and I am asking for it on the fifteenth as I said I would, and I am asking in this shed with four of you in it and not in a house. I have no employer and the certificate will be in my name and my name will be on top of his, and I told him that on Thursday and I am telling you because you are the four who will be holding the thing he hands me."

There were four people in the shed and none of them said anything for a moment, and the brazier ticked.

"You have got five pounds eleven in the cooperative box," Sera Quill said, "and I have read the open book and I know that, and I am not asking the box. Sixteen households, at a round number each, and a woman on a round number is worth more than a man on a round number, and I will take it from anybody. And I will say now, while there are four of you here, that if it is not there on Friday morning I will not do it, and Mr Vale will not have a certificate, and the wound will be open to the air with nothing over it, and that is what the eighteenth will find." She put the pen away. "And I will say the second thing now so that nobody has to be brave later. If you do it and it is good, the certificate is worth a great deal to you and it is worth nothing to me except that I will have been right. And if it is not good, the certificate is a document with a cooperative's name on it that a district officer will read in February when the seed house is under water. There is no version of this that I am comfortable in. I have not been comfortable in it since Thursday and I have not slept properly in either."

Then she went out into the yard to look at the wound in the dark with a hooded lamp, and Marek stood in the shed with four baskets of tar-smelling fiber and a calendar.

Tomorrow was Monday the sixteenth, and there were four of them, and there was a licensed woman who could be in the pit for the whole of one working day, and the wound was open to the weather for the first time in four seasons, and it was going to be open to the weather all night.
