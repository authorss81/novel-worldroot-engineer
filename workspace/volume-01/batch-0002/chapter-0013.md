# Chapter 13

## The Line She Would Not Sign

Saturday was a working day. Saturdays had been working days on this holding for as long as anybody could remember, and the only difference about this one was that five people were doing the work for nothing, in the cold, with a book.

The pit had a foot of water in it and it had to come out before ten o'clock, because at ten o'clock four people were going to stand in it with a book.

Marek set them a rule before he handed out the buckets. *Out of the pit, not into anything else.* The pit water was iron and it had been standing over a wound since the flood night, and the return main under the north annex was the seed house's bed, and the only veto anybody had ever held over that bed belonged to Tova Reed, it was in writing, and it had not been withdrawn, and nobody was going to be the person who went into that pit and found out.

That took nine buckets a round with a hand bailer, and there were three hundred and something buckets out of a four-foot pit, and it took four of them from eight in the morning until a quarter past ten with two breaks for the cold, and it is the least interesting work in the world and they did it well.

Then the log.

Sera Quill sat on the well house step with the day book open on her knee and made Marek read every entry aloud to her before it went in, and made him read it to the other three after that, and only then would she initial the sheet.

She had a system and it was cruelly simple. Anything a stranger could not check without asking somebody was struck out and rewritten. Anything anybody was guessing at was struck out. Anything with a person's opinion in it was moved to a second column with the person's name against it, and she read that column at the end of the day and either agreed with it in writing or crossed it out herself and initialled the crossing.

The first two hours went like that. Ten, ten-fifteen, ten-thirty, and then eleven. The dial numbers and the water and the four names and the witness.

At the third hour, Anneke Roos wrote the entry and read it back, and Marek went over it and approved it, and then she did not put the pen down.

"Well?" Marek said.

"I will sign it after the third hour," she said. "I will not sign the second."

"Read it to me again."

She read it.

"Second hour, eleven of the clock. Head opened for examination of the wound. Collar off. Wound inspected. No adverse condition observed."

Sera Quill put her pen cap back on and said, "Well done," to nobody in particular, and Marek found that he had been about three seconds from an argument and had been saved by a word from a woman on a step.

"Which part," he said.

"All of it, and the last part worst." Anneke Roos put the pen on the page instead of off it. "Mr Vale, there was no examination. The pad is on. The pad has been on since the flood and it is one piece with four coats in it and it is bonded to nothing, and it came up under a foot of water, and it let go at the low side again this morning while we were bailing it and all four of us watched it do it. Nobody has put a hand on that wound. Nobody could. It is under a coat of tar and a wire cradle and eighty feet of nothing, and if you write *wound inspected* then in ten weeks a man in a cold room is going to read that page and ask you what you saw, and what you saw was a pad."

"Then write what we saw."

"I am going to. That is not the whole of it." She turned round on the step rail so that she was facing the three of them. "There is a second thing and it is the reason I am not signing, and I would like the licensed person in this yard to say whether I am right, because I am not going to have it that I said it."

Sera Quill took the cap off her pen again. "Go on."

"Four of us put our hands inside a hole that has a living thing in it." Anneke Roos said it evenly. "I want that written. Not the crew. Me and Miss Senn and Mr Byre and you. I went to the elbow. I put my left hand on the cradle at the point where the cradle comes over the frame and I could feel it move, and I did not like it, and I took my hand off, and I did not write down that I did not like it and I did not write down anything else about it. But I was there, and the entry does not say so, and the entry says *head opened for examination*, which is a thing a man does on his own with a lamp, and what happened in the third hour of the fourteenth day of this season was four unlicensed people were within reach of a living conduit for the better part of an hour."

"And is that bad?" Jonas Byre asked. "Because it is what you are going to be doing on Monday."

"It is what you are going to be doing on Monday, with a licensed person standing over the whole of it, which is a different thing, and the difference is the sixth column." She nodded at the word *none* on the head of the page. "If the entry for Monday says four unlicensed hands on a living wound and the column says *Ms Quill*, then anybody reading it in ten years knows exactly what was done and who watched it and what the law was. If the entry says *head opened for examination*, then it does not. And we will have had a day of work and a page of nothing."

Marek found that he had both hands on the rail of the well house and did not remember putting them there.

"Write it the way you have said it," he said. "All of it. And put my name on the third hour as one of the four, and put what you did with your hand, and put that you did not like it, because that is the truest thing anybody has written in this book since the third day."

"It will make the page ugly," said Anneke Roos.

"It is not a page for looking at," Marek said. "It is a page for being checked. I have had three days of finding out what a document is worth, and it is worth exactly what the worst entry in it is worth. If the worst entry in this book is a day when four frightened people did a small thing badly and wrote down that they did it, then that is what this book is worth, and I would rather have it than a book of *no adverse condition observed*."

She wrote it, and it took her a while, and it came out at nineteen lines where the draft had been four, and it said this:

*Second hour, eleventh of the clock, the fourteenth day. Collar off, cradle exposed. Wound not examined: compound pad in place, unbroken at the high side, let go at the low side this morning and re-set by hand, not dressed, not opened. Four unlicensed hands within reach of the conduit at the collar for the whole hour. Names on the head: Mr Vale, Miss Senn, Mr Byre, Mrs Roos. Mrs Roos left hand to the elbow on the cradle at the six o'clock wire; reports the cradle moved under it; hand withdrawn. Witness: Miss Senn. Licensed person present: Ms Quill, sitting on the well house step, taking the times from the operator. Condition of the wound not established. No instrument used. Nothing taken off the holding.*

Nobody signed that sentence for about a minute.

Then Sera Quill said, "That is the first entry in this cooperative's book in six years that I would put in front of a hearing officer without a word changed, and I have put a hundred of those in front of hearing officers, and I have never once had a cooperative write one for me. Sign it. All four of you. Sign it and put the hour at the top of the page and not at the bottom, so that if a page is ever torn out it takes its own time with it."

They signed it. Gael Senn signed it second, and Jonas Byre third, and Anneke Roos last, and Marek signed underneath them because he had said his name first in the list and he was not going to be a man who puts himself at the bottom of a thing he put at the top.

And then he did the thing he had not planned to do, which was that he wrote the rule on the inside cover of the standing log in his own hand, and the date, and the source of it, which was a tenant.

*An entry is made at the hour it describes. Not at the hour there is time to make it. If it is made late, the lateness is part of the entry: give the hour of the work and the hour of the writing, both, and say why. A record made after the fact is a memory. A memory is not a record, and a memory is the first thing a good lawyer takes off a cooperative and the second thing a bad man takes off a witness.*

*Recorded by Marek Vale, engineer, on the fourteenth day, on the statement of Anneke Roos, tenant, who declined to sign a shorter entry and was right.*

He read it back to her and she made him alter *lawyer* to *hearing officer*, on the grounds that a hearing officer was the thing they would actually meet, and then she signed it, and then Gael Senn signed it, and then there was no more room on the inside cover and Jonas Byre wrote his name round the edge of the last page in pencil because he had run out of cover and did not want to be left out.

That was the first half of the day and it was the best thing that happened in that yard all week.

Tova Reed came down at four with the bailer in one hand and a slate under her arm, and had, as usual, been somewhere nobody had been.

"I am not here about the log," she said. "One of you asked me this morning whether the pit water could be used on the trays. It cannot, and I am not going to let it be done on Monday, and I am not refusing you the use of the bed because of my condition. I am refusing you because the water has stood four days over a living wound and it will kill a root in a fortnight and I could smell it from the top of the ladder."

"Then the bed gets what it gets," Ilsa said, "and what it gets is what it has been getting since Tuesday, which is nothing, and it is four degrees today, and it was four degrees on Tuesday, and it will be five by Sunday, and we are moving trays to the mill floor at dusk and back at dawn and I am not asking anybody's permission to carry a tray up a road."

"Then somebody carries a tray," Marek said, "four times a day, in the cold, for as long as this lasts, and I am writing it in the return column as labor with a name against it, because it is labor and it has a name."

"Write *Roos* against it," said Anneke Roos, "because I am the one who will be doing it, and I would like it in a book before I have done it twice, so that when it is me that is too old for it there is a page somebody can point at."

Sera Quill, at the top of the ladder, said, "Write it twice a day, not four. A tray is not a sack. Twice a day and the rest of the night under sacking on the mill floor is what it actually is, and if you write four you will do four for a week and then you will do none, and the writing will be the lie instead of the work."

So it went in as twice a day, with two names on it, and the date, and the licensed person present: none, and she initialled that herself.

They went home at dusk. Marek stayed and closed the day.

He had taught four people that the last entry of a working day has to be closed by somebody, and signed by them, and that the day does not get to end at dusk and pick itself up in the morning. So he sat on the step with the book on his knee and went down the two columns of times and compared them against the sheet of the licensed woman's own page, which she had let him look at and not take, and the two agreed, and he entered the last entry, which was *work ended at the half hour after four. Book closed at the seventh hour. Six hours and a half, two breaks* — and then he read the whole page back, and got to the second hour, and stopped.

The third hour's entry was wrong. Not a matter of fact. It said he was one of four hands within reach of a living conduit at the collar, and it did not say that he had been the one who said *come and feel this*, twice, and had put his own hand on the cradle before he asked either of them to.

He read it again. Then he took the pencil out and held it over the line, and understood that he was about to make an entry that would be better and would not be true, and that the whole of the afternoon's teaching had been designed to make that impulse visible to a woman standing on a step, and that she had gone inside at four and had not seen him do it.

He put the pencil down.

He closed the book, and turned it round, and squared it on the bench, and read the last line of the day back twice to be sure he had it right, and went up to the seed house to tell his mother that four people had signed their own names to a page that said they were not qualified, which was the first honest thing that had gone into a Lantern Reach book in six years and which he had got by being told no by a tenant.

Four days.
