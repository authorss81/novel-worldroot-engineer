# Chapter 12

## The Column With Nothing In It

Not one of the four said no on the first morning, and every one of them had a reason that in anybody else's cooperative would have been a no, and Marek had been ready for three of the reasons, and the fourth was the one that taught him something.

He caught Jonas Byre at the north gate at seven, because Jonas Byre was out at seven whatever the weather, and had been out at seven for thirty-one years, and the cough had been with him since before the first White Harvest and had got worse in the wet and was no worse in the dry.

"Listen before you answer," Marek said. "I am not asking for a day. I am asking for three, and they are the fifteenth, the sixteenth and the twentieth, and the twentieth is a load test, and on the fifteenth and the sixteenth you will be standing in a pit four feet deep with three inches of iron water in the bottom of it in a coat that does not get dry, and the work is stripping four seasons of tar off a wound with a bar and a hook and a knife."

"Then it is the north row's gate that is going to be shut in February," Jonas Byre said, and coughed, and waited for it, and went on. "I have forty acres on the low ground and my landlord's man has been at me since the spring about a drain. You are asking me to be in a hole. Why are you asking me."

"Because you are the only man in this cooperative who ever said out loud that somebody should have checked a number."

Jonas Byre looked at him with the gate open behind him and a hand on the bar.

"I said that in a seed house with forty people in it," he said, "and I said it because I am sick of being a man who is surprised. Nine for and twenty-two against, and I abstained, and you want to know why I abstained." He pulled the gate to. "Yes. I am in. And I am telling you the reason so that you can put it somewhere and find out later whether I was right."

He was in by seven twenty. That was the easy one.

Anneke Roos was not the easy one.

She was fifty, and fifty-one in the spring, and she farmed the low acre, the one that goes under first, and she had no children and a brother in Fennmere who did not write, and she was standing at the end of her own drain with a bar in her hand at half past seven on a Friday morning because there was still a foot of water in the ditch four days after the storm and somebody had to be the somebody who went down it.

"I have heard what you are doing," she said, before he had got ten yards. "I heard it at the seed house last night and I have been thinking about it since the middle of the night, which I would rather not have done, and I am going to say the thing that is wrong with it and then you are going to tell me why I have got it wrong, and if you have got an answer I will go and get my coat."

Marek stopped.

"There are five of us," she said, "and one of you is an engineer and the other four are a foreman, a lamp-keeper, a man with a cough and me. And on Monday, four unlicensed people are going to open a thing made of living wood that a licensed woman has looked at once, and put their hands on it. And if it goes wrong the district does not send a letter. The district seals the head and takes the seed, and the seal does not care whose fault it was, and the seed does not come back, and it will not matter that we wrote it all down." She put the bar down against the ditch wall. "That is the thing that is wrong with it. It is not that it is dangerous. Everybody who lives on this ground is dangerous at some point in a week. It is that the four of us are going to be the four names in a document about a thing that went wrong, and the two people who are not going to be in that document are going to be the two people who arranged it."

There was nothing in the yard at that hour but the water going over the weir boards and one crow.

"Yes," Marek said.

"That is it? You are not going to argue with a thing that is true?"

"It is true, and you are going to be on the document, and I have not got an argument." He put his hands in his coat. "I can give you three answers and none of them is a reason. One: it is a holding seal, not rootwork, and it is not a graft, and there is nothing in it a licensed reader would have had to do differently except be licensed. Two: I have been in that pit twice with my own arm and got four seconds of nothing, and I have a licensed engineer standing over it for every hour of it on Monday, and if the rule is that four unlicensed people may not put their hands on a living thing then no wheelwright may ever dress an axle, and the rule would be about a different thing. Three, and this is the one that matters, and it is the one I did not expect to be saying to you at half past seven in the morning with you holding a bar."

"Go on."

"The rule is not about Monday. The rule is about what happens in five days, in a cold room, to four people whose names are on the last page of a book." He said it carefully. "If we do this and it holds, the four names on that page are the reason it holds, and they can be asked about it and they can answer. If we do this and it fails, the four names on that page are the reason anybody knows anything about it, and they can be asked about it and they can answer. There is no version of the next five days where those four names are not in a document. The only question is whether they are in it as the people who did the work or as the people who were standing around a contractor when the contractor was paid for it."

Anneke Roos stood in her own ditch water and thought about it for about as long as it took her to walk to the gate and back.

"You are asking me," she said, "to be one of the four, and not one of the four standing round."

"Yes."

"Then I want to say something at the table tonight and I want it written down, and if it is not written down I am not in the pit." She got her coat off the fence. "Say it with me. *I know less about this than the man who is asking me and I am going in anyway, and if I am wrong I want to be able to point at the hour.*"

Marek said it with her, and it did not sound right when he said it, and she said, "Better. There. Now I have said it and I have said it twice, and I want the first one in the book and not the second one."

Gael Senn had already said yes, at six, before anybody asked her, which is a thing she later admitted to and which cost her something in the argument about the eighteenth. Ilsa Kern did not have to be asked, and said so, and then said the thing that made the crew a crew instead of a queue: "I am in it because the ground is mine. If the pad fails, the yard goes under, and the yard is not the cooperative's, it is four families' houses. You are all in it because a woman with a lamp asked you nicely. I am in it because I would be standing in my own water in January." She looked around at the three of them. "Do not make a speech about that on Monday. Make the speech about the work."

Five days.

The afternoon was at the well head, and it was the first real teaching Marek had ever given anybody, and he discovered in about forty minutes that he did not know how to do it.

He had thought the subject was measuring. It was not. The gauge was easy; he had a brass one in a case and two hands and a boy to hold the lamp. What nobody in this yard could do was write down a thing so that a stranger in Fennmere could check it without asking them anything, and Marek found that the harder half of engineering was the half that made no improvement to anything at all.

"Four things," he said, to four people standing round a pump pit in the cold. "Every entry. Four things, every time, and if one of them is not there the entry is not a record, it is a thing somebody remembered.

"What was done, in the order it was done, and nothing in it that was not done. That last half is the half that will be argued about and I want it written the plain way.

"Who did it. A name against every hand. Not *the crew*. The crew is how you write a thing when you do not want to be asked which of you it was.

"When. The hour and the day and the day is the day it happened, not the day you had time to write it down. A reading with no hour on it is not a reading. It is a feeling.

"And what somebody else could check without asking you. If you cannot date it, you write *cannot be dated* in those words and you do not guess and you do not put a range on it. I will not have a guess in this book with a date on it."

"Who checks it?" Jonas Byre said. "That is the fourth one. If I write down what somebody else could check, who is the somebody else."

"Sometimes it's a number on a dial, and the dial is still there tomorrow, and anybody can put an eye on it. Sometimes it's the other three of us, because there are three other people who were standing there and they can say *yes, that is what happened, and I remember it because of the thing that was happening at the time.*" Marek tapped the rail. "And sometimes the answer is that there is nothing. And then you write that there is nothing, and you write what you would have needed to check it, and you write that you did not have it. And that is not a failure. That is the most honest thing a cooperative can put in a book, and I have been in this yard for two weeks waiting for somebody else to do it."

He got the standing pressure log out then, because it was a motion of the eleventh and it was lying on the shelf over the well-house bench where Nia Vale had put it, waiting to be started, and it was the only book in the building that nobody had a private feeling about.

It was a foolscap book bought out of the cooperative's own money in the spring for two shillings, and Lissa Vale had ruled the first page herself with a pen she had ground down for the purpose, and there were columns across it.

*Hour.* — *At the head.* — *At the sump.* — *Water, in.* — *Water, back.* — *Who was on the head.* — *Witness.* — *Licensed person present.*

Eight columns. Marek had looked at that last one for about a minute and had not known what to do with it, and Sera Quill had known what to do with it, and on the seventh day she had made him and Gael say *anchor, path, return* out loud twice in a pit, and this was the same instruction in a different coat.

"What is that for?" Anneke Roos said.

"It is for the truth," Marek said. "It is so that any fool in this yard can look at a page from four months ago and see at a glance whether there was a licensed person standing in the room when it was taken. And so that the answer is written down by the person who is reporting it, and not by the person who is being reported on."

The others looked at each other in the way people do when they have understood something and are deciding whether to be impressed.

"It will be *none*," Gael Senn said. "Most days it will be none."

"Yes," Marek said.

"Then the column is the whole book," Anneke Roos said. "Everything else in it is a number that anybody can argue about, and that column is not a number at all, and it is the only part of the page that cannot be argued with, because either a person was standing there or a person was not." She crouched down and looked at the ruling. "And whoever is the licensed person is the only person on this holding who is paid to tell us we have got it wrong, and the day there is nobody in that column is the day we are on our own, and the district's paper is going to say *maintained* or *not maintained* and it is going to decide it off that column and not off any of the numbers."

"Yes," Marek said. "Write that down."

"Write it down where?"

"On the inside cover," he said. "In my hand, and the date, and the fact that a tenant of this cooperative said it and not the engineer."

They started at two. Marek put the brass gauge on the head tap and a second dial on the sump tap, and Sera Quill came out of the pump shed with a third dial she had brought from Fennmere in her own case and would not let out of her sight, and stood over the whole of it without touching anything.

"Quarter hours," she said. "Write what the dial says and the hour and nothing else, and do not write what you think it means, and if you think something write it in a second column with your name against it and I will read it and either throw it out or tell you why."

Gael Senn read the hours out loud so that nobody would have to look away from a dial to write, which she had worked out on the eighth day of the season from a man with a nosebleed and two hands on a needle, and which nobody had asked her to learn.

The first page of the standing pressure log of the Lantern Reach Work Cooperative was written on the thirteenth day of the season, and it took from two in the afternoon until four, and it had six columns filled and one of them empty of everything but a word, and the word was *none*, and Marek wrote it himself, in his own hand, on the first line and underlined it, which was a habit he was trying to break and did not break.

The head held at three-eighths under the mark. The sump was colder than the head by a degree and a half and would not be argued about. The water coming back at the mill race came back the same, which was a thing Marek had worked out on the first afternoon from the mill-keeper's board across the water and had said out loud to nobody, and which four people in this cooperative had never once thought to be remarkable.

And at three-forty, in the fourth quarter after two o'clock, the frame of the well house went tight and quiet, and a beat came up through the boards and through the soles of everybody's boots, one about every four seconds, five or six of them, and then stopped.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Jonas Byre said, "Well, that is the pump going funny."

"It is not the pump," Anneke Roos said. "It is off the pump. I have been standing next to the pump for an hour and a half and the pump has not done that once."

Sera Quill took out her own page and read the times back and wrote nothing on it and closed it.

"Write the times," she said. "Two-forty, two-forty-five, two-fifty, two-fifty-five, three. Five beats. Then stop. And under it write: *cause not established*."

"Word," Marek said.

"Word," said Sera Quill, "and do not look for one between now and the eighteenth, or you will find one and it will be the wrong one, and I have watched men find one and spend a year on it."

Anneke Roos wrote it, and put the pen down, and read the line back, and then she did a thing that Marek did not know she was going to do: she turned the page round, and got the last line, and took the whole entry for the first hour in her own hand, and read it out loud from the top of the column to the bottom, word for word, the way you read out a will.

"Two o'clock. Head three-eighths under the mark. Sump a degree and a half colder. Water in and water back the same. Mr Vale, Miss Senn, Mr Byre and myself on the head. Miss Senn witness. Licensed person present: Ms Quill, standing on the boards, reading her own book." She looked up. "That is what is in it. Is that what happened."

"That is what happened," Marek said.

"Then it is a record, and I have never had one of those before in my life," said Anneke Roos, "and I am going to go home and sleep, and somebody is to bring this book back to this board with a stone on it and not take it into a house."

Marek stayed with the book after the others had gone, and turned the first page round, and read the eight column heads, and the six that were full, and the two that were not.

Five days to the eighteenth. Nobody who had ever worked on this holding had laid a seal, and the four people who were going to lay this one had been introduced to each other that morning, and the district would send a man on Wednesday to decide whether a well was maintained, and there was a book on a bench in the well house with five names in it and a licensed engineer's initials down the margin and an empty word in the middle of every page.

He squared it on the bench, and put the stone on it, and noticed that the stone was not straight, and made it straight, because a thing that is easy to find is a thing somebody will come and read.
