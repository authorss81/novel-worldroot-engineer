# Chapter 18

## Eight Trays And A Letter

Nobody in the cooperative slept much on the night of the eighteenth, and the thing that had actually kept Marek awake was not the charter.

It was that the pump had run all day and all night on a node that at midnight belonged to nobody, and it was still running, and there was no longer any document anywhere in Tarrow Fen that said it was allowed to.

He had got that straight by about two in the morning and had gone out to the well head in the dark to check it, and the pump was turning over on the idle, the counter going round, the yard trough full, the water tasting of iron, and there was no seal on the head and no charter on the holding and no clause in force that made any of it lawful, and it was the night of the eighteenth in a bad year, and the seed house needed eleven hundred turns a day and was getting them.

That was the siege. Not a threat. A pump that works and a book that says it should not.

"So somebody sits here," Ilsa Kern said, when he told her at the milking. "That is what it is, is it not. It is not a siege, it is a watch, and a watch wants a person on it."

"It is a watch on a well head that anyone can report," Marek said. "And the report does not have to be a lie. If a man in Fennmere tastes the water and says iron, that is a report, and Bray is right that it reopens the clause, and the three things are sealing the head, taking the stock, or relocation, and we have no charter to defend any of them with."

"Then it is a watch and it is a job," said Ilsa Kern, "and jobs on this ground have been done by whoever was standing there, and the standing has never once been in writing, and it is in writing now, and I would like the hours in it."

---

The money was counted on the table in the seed house at seven in the morning on the nineteenth, with the door open, because Dunmar Fitch had said that a count done behind a closed door in a cooperative that has just lost a charter would be the first thing anybody ever doubted.

Twelve pounds and six shillings.

Sixteen households. Nine of them signed the paper and put money in, which was nine out of nine, and nobody made an argument about that. Two of the other five who signed it put nothing in, which was worse than not signing, and both of them were people Marek had taught to read a dial. The third was Hesk's daughter, who put in a shilling and wrote *and I am sorry* on the line. Two of the sixteen never signed it at all, and their names are not recorded anywhere, because the paper said the name of anybody who did not sign was not to be recorded, and the paper was pinned to the well-house door for three days and somebody could have torn it down and nobody did.

Fourteen names. Twelve pounds and six shillings, and a shilling, and the shilling was counted separately and entered separately and not added in, because a shilling with a sentence attached to it is not the same money as a shilling.

"She is owed a sentence back," said Nia Vale, from the end of the table, writing. "Not from the fund. From one of us. That is not the cooperative's business and it is my business, and I will do it, and I am writing it in the book that I will do it, with a date, because in about a year she is going to remember that she put in a shilling and said sorry and nobody ever said anything about it again."

Sera Quill came in at a quarter past seven with a case and a lamp and no coat, having crossed the yard from the pump shed at ten to seven with frost still on the sleeve of a coat she had slept in, because the district clerk at Fennmere had offered her a bed and she had declined it and had not said why.

She took the money in a folded paper and counted it on the table and wrote the receipt herself and gave it to Dunmar Fitch, who entered it, and then she said, "Before I sit down, the other half of that. My scope was two days at the head and a written finding and it cost fourteen pounds and I have been in this yard for six days past it and I have not charged you a shilling and I am not going to, and Mr Fitch is going to want to know why and you may all tell him the same three reasons. One: I have not signed anything worth anything yet and I am not going to leave. Two: on the tenth a man in a district office emptied a book I had read into a file, and on the seventeenth a clerk carried a table across a yard and read it out, and if I go up the road now it is closed without me. Three, and I am telling you because one of you will go looking for it, and it is not noble: there is nothing else in this country for me to be doing this month, and I would rather be in a wet pit with four people who write down what they saw than in a room in Fennmere writing down what I think of them." She put the receipt down. "Now sit down, all of you, because I have to say one more thing and then I have to do a thing and I would rather say it before I do the thing."

She read the charter's lapse out of her own book in one sentence, without a note in her voice.

"You have no charter. You have not lost a *right* to anything, because your right to anything was in the charter, and the charter ended. So this morning you have no right to draw from that head, no right to work on it, and no right to put a name in a district book about it, and the head is still open and the pump is still running, and the reason your seed house has water today is that nobody has sealed it. I am not going to be able to fix that. Nobody can fix that. What can be done is that everything we do on that head from today is recorded as *unlicensed and unchartered*, with the date and the hour, and nobody is to be asked to touch it who is not going to be named, and if anybody is asked to touch it by a district man in a good coat, the answer is that the cooperative holds nothing and will sign nothing and will hand him the keys to its own well and let him do it on his own authority, which is the only answer that costs nobody else anything."

She put her hand on the case.

"And the second thing, and it is the second thing and I have thought about the order of them all morning. On Friday I load-test that seal to a full draw, in front of four witnesses, and I give you a certificate that says the node is maintained on that day. And I want all of you to understand exactly what that certificate is worth, so that nobody builds a plan on it in the winter."

"It is worth a great deal," said Marek.

"It is worth one day's evidence about one wound," said Sera Quill. "It has no charter behind it, because there is no charter, so it cannot be entered as a charter condition, so the district will accept it as an observation and not as a maintenance record, and in March a man will come with a file and read it and see a cooperative that was doing unlicensed work on an unchartered node for nine days in the last month of the year. It is worth that. It is not worth a house."

"Then it is still worth doing," said Anneke Roos, "because the alternative is a wound with the weather on it, and I did not spend two days in that pit so that a man in Fennmere could read a file and be pleased with himself."

"Yes," Sera Quill said. "That is the sentence, and I want it in the book, and I did not write it and I could not have written it, and I have been in this trade for thirty years and I have never had it put to me that plainly."

---

The seed annex was made real on the nineteenth, in the afternoon, on the loading bench by the seed house door, and it was not a meeting and it was not a document and nobody signed anything until four o'clock.

It was eight trays.

Eight trays of the Longneck, the last dry-land rye, eight alive out of eleven, and Tova Reed put them on the bench one at a time with the wet sand still on them from the north annex, and she did not put them on the bench so that people could look at them. She put them on the bench because that is where a thing gets put when it is going somewhere.

Nia Vale had the book and the columns, because Nia Vale had been the only person in this building for nine days who had insisted that the list of what Nia Vale was taking out of the seed house should have columns, and had then watched Nia write six different kinds of list and had written the list for the trays herself while Tova Reed explained what a comparison set is.

*Item. Crate. Quantity. Condition. Where it is going. Who is standing there when it goes. Witness.*

Eight lines, and then the ninth, which was the trust's own material — a sealed tin of the March's last two hundredweight of the same line, which was a fifth of what the March had, and which Tova was taking back, and which Lissa Vale said was not a fifth of anything and put her hand flat on the tin.

"That tin came in on the first day of this season on a wagon with a broken axle," Lissa said. "It has been in my north annex since the second of this month, under three dead trays, and if it goes down the road to Cray on a hired cart on Friday it goes in a basket with a strap over it, and I will not put it on a hired cart, and I do not care what the trust's paper says about carriage."

"The trust's paper says nothing about carriage," said Tova Reed, "because I wrote it."

They argued about it for twenty minutes and the argument was not a fight, because both of them were seed people, and the outcome was that the tin went into the seed house's own press under the ledger and the ledger's own weight, and the March's copy of the annex said *retained at Lantern Reach by the seed steward's decision, at risk, and the trust knows where it is*, and Tova Reed initialled that and Lissa Vale signed it, in that order, which is not an accident and which nobody remarked on.

And then Tova Reed put her hand on the bench and named the condition, out loud, to nine people, in a yard, which is a thing she does and which Marek has learned to stop arguing with.

"You are the last place in the fen with a living comparison," she said. "The March's northern relay went in the wet season and four lines died with it. Everything that is left of that line on this side of the water is in eight trays and one tin, and I am putting the March's line into the last place in the fen. If the fen goes, the March has nothing left to compare against, and it will not be able to plant a dry-land crop for four years, and there are nine thousand people on the March road who eat what that crop is. So that is the condition, and I am saying it here instead of putting it in a book and letting you find it in a bad month. I am asking the Lantern Reach Work Cooperative to look after the only seed we have got, at its own cost, for as long as it can, and I am telling you that I have asked a cooperative with no charter, five pounds and a half, and a wounded well to look after it. If somebody with a district badge comes in September and asks for this seed, you say no. Not to me. To them. And you say it without a charter behind you and that is exactly how easy it will be for them to take it."

"We will say no," said Ilsa Kern.

"Put it in the book," said Tova Reed, "and put the tin in the press, and put the March's copy in Cray on Friday in the same cart as my steward's trunk, and do not tell me the March can manage the carriage, because it cannot, and I have done the arithmetic twice."

Marek signed, because the person who accepts a condition for a cooperative is the engineer of record and it had been his signature that broke the vault's veto on the third of the month, and Lissa signed as seed steward, and Tova signed for the trust, and Nia entered eight lines and a ninth with a witness against every one, and the ninth line's witness was Nia herself and her own name had to be in it, which she said was a rule she had invented and was not giving up.

---

At four o'clock Marek wrote a letter to Kellan Rusk, and he wrote the whole thing himself, and the only person who read it before it was sealed was his mother, standing at the table, who was not asked.

*Mr Rusk. I am told by Mr Bray that your charter is the only licensed draw on this node as of the eighteenth and that mine has lapsed, and I am writing to say that I know it, and to say what I am not going to do about it.*

*I am not going to ask you to reduce it. I am not going to ask you to put anything in a return. I am not going to send anybody to Fennmere to look at your gauge, and I am not going to speak in a hearing about a log I have read but never measured, and I am not going to say in public that your acceptance column was a lie, because you did not lie about the pad, you only called it the wrong word, and I have had eleven days to work out the difference and I have got it.*

*What I am asking is this. On the eighteenth you were in a yard for an hour and a half and you said a thing about your pad to a yard and not to a man, and the clerk wrote it down, and the licensed engineer put a Crown specification into a district file as a thing to be compared against, and both of those are now in a file that is going to sit on a shelf in Fennmere until somebody who is neither of us reads it. I do not know who that is. I think it might be you, in a year, in a bad month, and I would rather you read it having had a letter than having had nothing.*

*If you are that man, I will send you everything I have, unedited, and it will include the errors, and there are a great many. If you are not that man, I would still rather you had the letter, because a charter holder of record who has never been told what a cooperative thinks of him by name is a charter holder who has been described to himself by other people, and I do not think that is fair to you and I do not think it is any use.*

*Your book I asked for and you refused and you were right. I have not argued with a woman about it and I am not going to.*

*This letter is not an offer and it contains nothing anybody can sue you on. I have had it read by our charter clerk and he has entered the fact that it exists.*

*Vale.*

He read it twice, and his mother's hands were on the seed house table and she read it over his shoulder, which she had not been asked to do, and said nothing, which was new.

"That is the second time this month," she said, "that you have written something for somebody to use and not for you to feel better about."

"Yes."

"Post it. Do not read it to me again. And go and count your eight trays, because your sister has been going up and down the ladder with a lamp for an hour and she is not your servant and she does not stop for you."

At seven that evening Nia Vale came into the seed house, put the letter on the table with a strap round it, and told him that the carter would be at the gate at seven the next morning and that she was not going up the ladder to the loft because it was raining, and that the crate list was finished and had been finished since four o'clock and that she had a spare column and wanted to know what he wanted in it.

"Whose," Marek said.

"Whose is not a column," said Nia Vale, "and you know perfectly well it is not a column, and if you put it in the book as a column then in five years somebody will be able to look up this week and see that a man wrote down a thing he was supposed to have asked for and did not ask for, and that is not going to be in this book." She capped the ink. "Tell me what goes in it."

"Say who refused to sign," Marek said, after a moment. "And say why they were asked and not asked. Not their reason. Why they were asked."

Nia Vale wrote it, and did not look up, and said, "That is a good column and I would never have thought of it, and I am going to be extremely unpleasant about having thought of it first, and you are going to have to live through that."

At ten, the levy board came down off the wall and went into the cooperative book with the amount and the hour and the fourteen names and the three of them with nothing in the line, and the shilling with the sentence on it, entered separately, and a line at the foot that read: *Short of the sum by one pound fourteen shillings. Made up by M. Vale from his own money. Entered, both amounts, on the nineteenth day.*

The pump turned over in the yard with nobody watching it and the charter gone, and the water came up the trough tasting of iron, and the sacking on the seal in the pit was dry, and somewhere up the fen a crew of two were loading a die into a cart for the twenty-seventh.
