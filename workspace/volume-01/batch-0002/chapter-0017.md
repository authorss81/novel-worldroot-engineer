# Chapter 17

## The Half-Of-An-Hour

The seal was two days old on the morning of the eighteenth, and it was standing, and it had not been touched.

It was standing the way a thing stands when it is not being asked anything: four green withies driven into the four sockets in the wire cradle, woven twice, tied off with withy and not with anything else, a frame about nine inches across lying over the face of the wound, and a piece of seed-house sacking spread and laced over the whole of it and cut short so that the sacking itself would show a wet spot if there was one. A flat willow stake stood at the four o'clock corner with the date cut into it in five strokes, and beside the date, in a smaller hand, five names, and the fifth of them was cut in a different hand than the other four, because Sera Quill had stood over the whole of the working day and had wanted to be on the piece of wood that was going to still be there in February, and Marek had not argued with her about it, because she was right to be. And there was nothing on the stake about what came next, because on the sixteenth nobody had written down yet what came next.

The withies had gone brown and hard in the cold, and iron had come out of them where the ties were, and green wood does that, and Ilsa Kern had said it would and it had. The whole of it weighed about four pounds. It was, by any measure in the district, a piece of rubbish.

It had not leaked. They had looked at it every day since Monday with a lamp and a cloth and had touched nothing, and on Tuesday the cloth had been dry, and on Wednesday morning it was dry, and Marek had got as far as writing the word *dry* in the working notes with the hour against it, and then had stopped, because *dry* on a Wednesday in the middle of the season is not a finding, it is a fact about Wednesday.

At seven they were all in the yard, because there was nowhere else to be.

The mill boy had the fire going in the well house. The seed house had the annex lamps lit along the top shelf. The open book was on the seed house table under a weight of stone with a slate on it, and the standing pressure log was on its board under its own stone, and the working notes were in Marek's coat and the fieldbook was in the press in his mother's keeping and was due to be produced before the charter expired and had not been asked for yet.

At half past seven, Inspector Ansel Bray came through the gate with a satchel and a folded umbrella he did not open, and Clerk Perrin Osse behind him with a bundle, and Kellan Rusk behind her, who had not been at the gate on a working morning in six years and who came in with his hat in his hand.

"Mr Rusk is the charter holder of record for the ground on the far side of the split," Bray said, before he had got to the well house. "He has to be here. He has told me he would rather not be and he is here, and I am going to record that he came."

"I have a charter to keep and a bailiff's hour," said Kellan Rusk. "I am here for the two hours and I am going to stand at the back and I am not going to say anything anybody wants me to say."

"Then stand at the back," Bray said, and went to the well head.

He had a satchel and an umbrella and a habit, and for twenty minutes he did exactly what he had done on the tenth, which was stand in the wet yard and not go near anything.

Then he said, "I am going to state the three things at the front again, because I have said I would say them every time, and Mr Vale, I know you have heard them, and I am not going to insult either of us by pretending that today is different. If I find unregistered maintenance on a non-scheduled branch, I may seal the head, take the seed stock into district custody, or refer the charter for relocation. Those are the three. I do not intend to use any of them and I have no immediate-risk finding, and I put that in the file on the tenth and it stands, and I would like everyone in this yard to understand that it is the only reason this morning is a morning and not an afternoon."

That was a third of the way through the first hour.

Then he went down.

He was in the pit twenty-six minutes, and he came up with lamp in hand, and he did not touch the seal, and he went round it twice before he said anything at all.

"This is not a Crown seal," he said.

"No, sir."

"It is not a compound seal. There is no tar in it. There is willow, withy, and seed-house sacking, and there is a stake with a date on it, and it is the first thing of its kind I have seen on a head in this district, and I have been in this district twenty-two years." He turned round to the yard. "Whoever laid it: was it you?"

Marek said, "Four of us, under supervision. It is in the working notes, with hours, and the licensed engineer of record was present for the whole of the working day."

"Where are the working notes?"

Marek gave them to him. They were six sheets, folded, in order, with the times, and the fourth page had five names on it and the first page had four internal cooperative entries in four different hands, none of which were an engineer's.

Bray read them at the well house rail in the rain, all six sheets, both sides, and he read the first page three times, and Marek watched him read it three times and understood what it was.

It was the schedule of the compound pad. Four seasons of it. Laid on, dressed, laid on, dressed, never cut back, never replaced, and the last top-up on the third day of the season with the hour against it, and a note under it that said *water coming up through the coat at six o'clock, warm, iron, noted on the first day and unchanged*. Four dates and four hands and four coats, and then a fifth line in a different hand entirely, which was not about the pad at all, and said *cooperative cannot demonstrate a return on this node; the return is short and the cause of the shortfall is not established.*

"That first page is a list of everything the cooperative has failed to do for four seasons," Bray said, to the yard, "and it is in five different hands, and the fifth line of it is not about the pad at all, and I did not ask for it, and it was not required of anybody."

"It was a working note," Marek said.

"It is a confession with a time on it," said Bray, "and it is the first document of its kind I have had out of a cooperative in this district, and I am going to say so in my report, and I would like all of you to understand that I am saying it on a morning when it does me no good whatever."

Then he asked for the log.

He asked for it himself. He went and got it off the board, with the stone still on it, and squared it, and read the whole of the standing pressure log from the thirteenth day to that morning, which took him about twenty minutes, because he read the way he did everything else and did it once.

He read the inside cover last.

Marek saw him get to it. He saw him stop, and go back, and read the nineteen lines of the rule about an entry made at the hour it describes, and the four lines under it about a record made after the fact being a memory, and then the attribution — *recorded by Marek Vale, engineer, on the fourteenth day, on the statement of Anneke Roos, tenant* — and then the initials, and the two more names written round the edge of the last page in pencil.

He read it twice.

"That is my own practice," he said. "I do not know who taught it to you and I do not care, and I want it understood that I am not being kind. That rule is going to cost you this morning."

"Sir—"

"Be quiet for one minute, please." He closed the log. He held it in both hands. "I have asked one question today and it is the only one that matters. When was the repair finished?"

"The fourth hour of the afternoon of the sixteenth," Marek said.

"And when was the entry recording that it was finished made?"

Marek found that his mouth had gone dry, and he had known it would, and he had known it on Saturday night in the well house with a pencil in his hand over a line he could have made better and did not make.

"Nine o'clock on the morning of the seventeenth," he said. "In the seed house. Alone. I wrote it at my own table and nobody witnessed it and I signed it myself."

"Why?"

The yard was very quiet, and somewhere behind him the mill wheel turned over once and stopped.

"Because I wanted to know whether it held before I wrote down that it was done," Marek said. "I had never done a holding seal. I had a licensed engineer standing in the pit for one working day and I had two days of a charter left and no tar, and I finished the work on Monday afternoon and I came back on Tuesday morning and looked at it with a cloth before I wrote it down, and it had not leaked, and then I wrote it down, and the hour on the entry is the hour I wrote it and not the hour I finished it, and the entry does not say that."

"Why does the entry not say that?"

"Because a note in a working book that says *I wrote this down on Tuesday because I was frightened* is a thing that stays in a working book," Marek said. "And I did not want it to stay in a working book. I wanted it to be one line and a date."

Bray opened the charter schedule on the rail of the well house, and it was the same schedule the cooperative's own book had printed in it, and everybody in the yard had read that section a hundred times.

"Fourth part," he said. "Second clause. *A charter holder without a licensed rootwright of its own shall evidence maintenance by dated record, and the record of any work shall be made at the hour at which the work was done, in the book of the holder, by the person responsible or by a witness of the work. A record made after the hour of the work is a recollection of the work and is not a record of it.* Read it again, Mr Vale. The words are *at the hour at which the work was done*."

"It is the cooperative's own rule on the inside cover of that book," Anneke Roos said, from the pit rim.

"It is exactly the cooperative's own rule," said Bray, "which is why I cannot do anything else. Mr Vale, if I waived this I would be waiving the only thing in this district that has ever kept a cooperative's account of itself straight. I would be saying that a record counts if the man who did the work will stand up in a room in six weeks and say that it was finished, and that is not a record. That is a man with a good memory and an interest in the outcome. I have used that standard to convict men. I am not going to use it to convict you and I am not going to pretend to convict you with something else."

He filled in the form, ruling the lines with a straight edge, and then read the finding out, and it was worse than the sentence Marek had been braced for.

"Finding: a repair attempt was made at this head on the sixteenth day, by four unlicensed persons under the supervision of a named licensed engineer, over a period of one working day, and the work is not disputed by the district and the district does not contest the workmanship. Finding: the record of the completion of that repair was made at the hour of the ninth of the following morning, and not at the hour at which the work was done, by the person responsible, alone, and the record does not disclose that. Finding: under the fourth part, second clause, the completion of the repair is not evidenced as maintenance. Finding: the node is therefore not shown to be maintained within the meaning of the charter. Finding: I am not the officer who determines your application, which stands referred, and I am not going to pretend to you this morning that my finding settles it, and I am not going to pretend to you either that it makes no difference, because your charter expires on its own terms at midnight tonight whatever a referred application does next, and after midnight there is nothing here to refer."

"That is the finding," he said. "That is my finding and I have read it over twice and I am not going to soften it in the last hour, and I would like the four people who are in that pit to understand that it is not a finding about their hands. It is a finding about a line of ink. Your hands are in my report and my report says the district does not contest them."

Kellan Rusk said, from the back of the yard, without moving, "Ask him about the tar."

"Kellan."

"Ask him what the seal is made of, Mr Bray, and then ask him what the pad was made of, and then ask him what the district's own specification for a seal on a wounded conduit is." He had his hat against his chest. "That is all. I will go back to the back."

Bray did not ask. Marek saw him decide not to, and saw him understand at the same moment that he was not going to get the last four minutes of the day back, and put the form in the satchel.

Then Sera Quill spoke, from the well house step, where she had been standing for a quarter of an hour with her case under her arm.

"Inspector. I am the licensed engineer of record and I have a statement, and I am going to ask you to read it into the record in my words, and it is adverse to a Crown practice, and I am not going to soften it, and you should have it from me and not from a page somebody else wrote."

"Ms Quill."

"The district specification for a seal over a wounded conduit is fortyweight of compound laid in a cold pit and renewed annually." She did not have paper and did not appear to need any. "A compound seal is a surface hold. It is renewed by adding to it, never cut back, never replaced, and in four seasons it is a pad and not a seal, and it is entered in a log as a seal, and a cooperative signs an acceptance of that log for six years. The wiring here is bound, not tarred, and the cloth is seed-house sacking, and the withies will have to be cut out and renewed in a year because they will rot, and a Crown pad cannot be cut out and looked at at all without destroying it. In my opinion the thing in that pit is better maintenance than the thing it replaces, and I have signed my name to that, and if it fails in February this is the document the district will use against me first, and I have asked for it to go in the record now rather than after."

Bray wrote it. It took him a while.

"That," he said, "is the first time in twenty-two years that a licensed engineer has put a Crown specification into a district file as a thing to be compared against, and I am going to carry it. It will not help you today. It will help whoever reads this file in three years, and I do not know who that is, and neither do you, and that is the correct position for both of us to be in."

He closed the satchel at about eleven.

"Now the rest of it, quickly, because I have a bailiff's hour and Mr Rusk has to get back to Fennmere." He faced the yard. "I am not sealing the head. I am not taking the seed stock. I am not referring the charter for relocation. Those three are for a node in immediate risk to public supply, and my finding of the tenth is that this node is not in immediate risk to public supply, and that finding has not been disturbed by anything that has happened in the last three weeks, including today.

"What I am doing is this. Your charter expires at midnight. That is a fact in a book and not an opinion and there is nothing anybody can do about it, and at midnight this cooperative holds no charter, and every piece of work any of you does on that node from tomorrow is done on a node you do not hold a charter over, and there is a clause about that, and the clause is not mine.

"Second. The emergency clause is not spent and not extinguished. I put that in the file on the tenth in those words and I am not taking it back. Any report from anybody, on anything, at any time, reopens it. A report from a neighbour that the water tastes of iron. A report from a district man about a wound. A report from me. I am not going to be the man who reads the next one — I am the charter officer for this district through the end of the year and the file does not travel with me, and if I am replaced in March by a man who has not been here, the next man will read a file with a maintenance record completed at the wrong hour, and he will do what the file allows, and the file allows three things, and I have said them out loud twice this morning so that they are in the yard and not just in his head."

"And third," he said, "and then I am going. There is a Crown-funded monitored repair in this cooperative's own file, offered on the sixteenth and refused on the sixteenth, in my clerk's hand, with a marginal note in the applicant's. That offer was made to a cooperative with a charter. Tonight you will be a cooperative without one, and I am not going to tell you the offer is dead, because I have never in my life seen a district let one die, and I would rather warn you than let you find out."

He got on the horse. Perrin Osse put the bundle in her satchel. And at the gate, with one boot in the stirrup, Bray turned round and did one more thing, and it was the only thing he did all morning that was not procedure.

"Mr Vale. The rule on the inside cover of your pressure log is the rule I have worked under for twenty-two years and I have never once seen a cooperative write it down before it needed it. Somebody in this yard taught you that on the fourteenth of this month." He looked for her and found her. "Mrs Roos. It is a good rule and it has cost you a charter and I am sorry, and I am telling you that I am sorry as a man and not as an officer, and you should not tell anybody on my account that the officer part is different, because it is."

Kellan Rusk was last through the gate, and he stopped, and he did not turn round, and he said to the middle of the yard, at a volume that carried perfectly:

"He will not read the rule the way you want him to read it. He will read it in three years in a room in Fennmere and he will have forgotten this morning, and the page will still be there and it will still say the same thing, and that is the whole of what I have to say to any of you, and I have been in this yard for an hour and a half and I have said it to no one in particular."

Then he was gone, and the gate was shut, and the well head was still open, and there was a cloth over the sacking on the seal that had not been moved since the middle of the morning, and the charter in the book of the cooperative said it expired at midnight the eighteenth, and the sky over Tarrow Fen was the color of a wet road and was going to do about the same all day.

Marek went down off the pit rim on the ladder with a lamp in his teeth and put two fingers on the sacking, at the six o'clock corner, and lifted it and looked underneath and put it back.

It was dry.

That was the whole of what he had, at two o'clock in the afternoon on the eighteenth day of the season, and it was dry, and it was four pounds of willow and sacking and withy and a stake with five names cut into it, and it was the best work he had ever done in his life, and the charter was gone at midnight, and the next crew due on that branch was in nine days.
