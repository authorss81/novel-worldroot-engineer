# Chapter 16

## Ninety Rows And No Headings

Dunmar Fitch sent the renewal out at eight in the morning on the seventeenth day of the season, by the cooperative's own boy on the mill-keeper's horse, and he sent it in a sealed wrapper with a sheet of his own paper inside it, and Marek read the sheet of his own paper before the boy got to the gate.

*Filed to the district: return branch as built falls short of the specification in this cooperative's work book; cause of the shortfall not established. Node condition: wound to a living conduit in the housing, unmaintained at the date of filing. Emergency repair in hand by unlicensed hands under a named licensed engineer.*

"That is a true cause," Marek said.

"It is the cause you gave me in my own seed house on the third day, in front of the meeting, and I would not enter it."

"You would not enter it in our book."

"I would not enter it anywhere," Dunmar Fitch said. "In our book it is a minute of a meeting and it is contradicted by a vote of that meeting and a hearing officer reads minutes the way a man reads a will. In a filing it is a statement by the charter holder's clerk to the district office, and there is no vote attached to it, and once it is in their hands it is true whatever this cooperative thinks about it." He put the pen down. "That is the difference between our book and a filing, and I have been the clerk of this cooperative for nineteen years and I have never had to explain it to anybody before, and I find that I am explaining it to a man who already has a page of it in his own hand."

He had a page of it in his own hand. It was the formula he had spoken out loud in a seed house full of people on the third day, written out afterwards on a requisition because there was nothing else to write it on, and it had been in his coat ever since.

"You understand what this does," Marek said.

"I understand precisely what it does. From today the district knows the return is short, in a document, on a form, from me. On Wednesday they may put that form in front of the cooperative and ask us to explain a number we have known was false since the third day and have never filed, and there is no answer to that question which does not begin with *we chose not to*." Dunmar picked the pen up again. "I am going to write *we chose not to* anyway. I have been that clerk for nineteen years and I have got a file to leave somebody."

The boy got to the gate at ten past.

Perrin Osse was on the road at noon.

She came out eleven miles behind the packet, on her own horse, with a satchel and a folded oilcloth bundle under her arm, and the thing she said first, before she had got off the animal, was that she had ridden out because the application was four days late and because the answer was not a letter.

"A letter is a thing you read," she said. "This is a form with something attached to it, and you are going to want it flat and dry and you are going to want four people in the room when it comes out of the wrapper, and I would rather be here than be asked afterwards whether it was opened before I got to it."

They opened it on the seed house table, because the seed house table was the only flat dry thing in the yard with four people in it, and there were four: Marek, Dunmar Fitch, Tova Reed, and Anneke Roos, who had walked down from the low acre for it and had said on the way in that she was there to be a witness and not to understand, and that those were different jobs.

And Sera Quill came in from the pump shed halfway through and did not sit, and Perrin Osse noted the time.

The application had come back endorsed. Not approved — endorsed, in the sense of a document that has been read and passed on, with a date and a clerk's initials at the foot, and the endorsement said nothing except that it had been received four days late and referred.

Attached to it, folded once, on a separate sheet, was a printed extract.

Six columns. No headings. The paper was the district's and it was not a good paper; it had come off a press that printed forms, and it had a line of type at the top with a form number in it and a place for a date, and the date was filled in by hand and it was the seventeenth.

Under the type there were ninety rows.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Fenmere sub-office," Perrin Osse said. "It goes out on every renewal. The first box on the form asks the applicant to list the works already scheduled on the node, and a form with an empty box in it is a form nobody has read, and the district cannot send out ninety forms a year with an empty box in the first one. So they send the extract. You are not being given anything. You are being spared a letter."

Marek got two fingers to the edge of the sheet and stopped with them there, because the woman at the end of the table was watching what his hands did and he had spent nine days in front of that woman and had learned what it cost him to reach for a thing in front of a room.

"Row forty-one," he said.

*Row forty-one. N-2214/K, and then five more figures across.*

Four of them were dates and they had a small mark against them at the side of the column, a single stroke, the same stroke in all four. The first was eight years back and the second was seven days ago. The third was the twenty-seventh of this month, and the fourth was the twenty-seventh of the year after that.

The fifth was also a date, and it was also marked, and Marek had to stand there and look at it twice before he understood that a row could hold a date that had not happened and still be marked, and that the only way to tell the difference between the two was the shape of the thing in front of it, and there was nothing in the shape of a figure to tell you which was which.

"That is the old cut," he said. "That is the one that healed. And that is the second one, from this season, and that is the one that was done on the night of the tenth with a girl on a plank."

"Then what is the fifth," said Tova Reed.

"I do not know," said Marek, "and neither does this row, and that is the most frightening thing on the page."

The room did that thing a room does when everybody has arrived at the same place by different roads.

"Right," said Perrin Osse, with a pencil out. "I am recording the hour and I am recording that I have seen this document, because I have carried it and I am not going to be one of the people who says afterwards that nobody looked at it." She wrote. "Now the procedural part, because it is the part nobody wants to hear. Mr Vale. You have just been handed the one document in this district that you are not allowed to keep, and the reason I have been told to bring it is that it is not mine to bring and it is not yours to hold. It is produced for the purpose of the application. When the application is determined, it goes back to Fennmere. And if you write down one line of it, that line is yours, and it is in a book, and a clerk can find it, and the district will call it what it is, which is a cooperative with a district works schedule in its own records."

"I will not take a copy," Marek said. "I have one number, one row and one date and I do not need ninety."

"You may not need ninety," she said, "and that is exactly the sentence a man says when he is going to need ninety, and I would rather you needed it in front of a hearing officer than in a seed house with four people who have never seen one."

---

They read it for two hours, and nobody wanted to be the one who stopped first.

Tova Reed was the one who worked out what it was, and she worked it out from the arithmetic and not from the shape, which is why she got there in twenty minutes when the rest of them had not.

"It is not a maintenance log," she said. "Look at the marks. If this were a record of work done, the two marks that are behind today would be the two dates it was done, and the two in front would be two more works. But every one of the four is marked, and a date in the future cannot be marked, and so the sheet is not a record of anything. It is a *forward* table. Somebody prints it with a horizon on it. It is a work programme, and Mr Vale, the two that are behind are not a history. They are the last two steps on a ladder." She put her finger on the line. "Which means that thing is not a schedule that has slipped. It is a schedule that is on time."

"She is right," said Sera Quill.

She had said it from the doorway, and she came in and stood at the end of the table with her case under her arm and did not sit, and Perrin Osse's pencil went back to the page.

"Ms Quill?"

"I have worked with district schedule forms," Sera Quill said.

It was four words in a quiet room and it did the work of a stone in a still pond. Anneke Roos's head came up. Dunmar Fitch put his hand over his mouth. And Perrin Osse wrote it down, and dated it, and read it back aloud, because she read everything back.

"Entered in the district's record at the hour, on the seventeenth day, at the seed house table, in the presence of the charter clerk, a tenant, the engineer of record, the trust's steward, and two others: Ms Quill states that she has worked with district schedule forms."

"Yes," said Sera Quill. "Enter all of it. Enter the hour. And enter that I said it and that I was not asked."

"Ms Quill," said Perrin Osse, "you understand what you have just done."

"I have spent six years in rooms where a licensed engineer who admits to familiarity with a district form is a woman with a history, and I have just stopped having a way of not having one, on a piece of paper, in a district clerk's hand, and I could have said nothing and I have said it." Sera Quill came the length of the table. "Because I am going to be in a certificate on Wednesday morning, and this morning I have found out that the reason the wound is where it is, is that a table says it is due on the twenty-seventh. I cannot certify a node in front of a district officer with that sitting in a drawer in the room and no word of it in my own report. If I hide this and it comes out in February, everything I have signed in twenty years comes out with it."

"Six columns," she said, and put two fingers on the sheet. "The first is the item. The second is when the work was ordered. The third, fourth and sixth are executions. The fifth is not a date at all, whatever the shape of it says. The fifth is a hold."

"A hold," said Anneke Roos.

"A hold. Suspended. Not to be executed until released, and printed in the same hand and the same column as a date, because a form is a form and the printer is not thinking about it. Neither of the two orders in that row has a hold against it, or I cannot see one, which is the same sentence. And the fifth figure is a hold, and it is marked, and I do not know what that means and I am not going to guess at it in a room with a clerk in it." She took her fingers off the sheet. "Now. Here is the part that will cost you something and I would like it written down while the clerk is here, because I am not going to say it twice and I am not going to say it to a man in a corridor in a month."

"Write it," said Perrin Osse, and wrote.

"An order is not a permission," Sera Quill said. "An order is a man in an office, on a date, deciding that this cut is due. Which means the date it was done is not the date it was necessary. Mr Vale dated those two faces himself, as ranges, out of lichen and mineral bloom, and he wrote both candidates side by side and signed a line underneath them saying *both, ask which.* That is the correct way to date a scar and he is the only man in this fen who does it. And neither of his numbers is in that row. The first order in it is eight years old, and the cut healed over in six to eight years, so the order is older than the cut by whatever the two of them were. The order is not in the wood. The order is in an office, and it was written before the cut, and it will be there in ten years with nobody's name on it at all."

She stopped, and her hand went to the case under her arm and stayed there.

"That is the second thing," she said. "And I am not going to tell you where I saw a form like that, or what office issues them, and both of those refusals stand and I would like them in the record with the rest."

"Entered," said Perrin Osse.

Marek stood at the table with both hands on the edge of it and looked at row forty-one for a long moment without seeing it.

He had spent nine days building a case. He had put a hand on a living branch twice, on a night, in a storm, on ground he had no right to stand on, and he had opened a healed seam with a crowbar and his own graft shears and marked that branch permanently, and he had spent his father's fieldbook on a district table to get eight days he already had — every hour of it, to prove that a branch on a fen embankment had been cut by somebody careless.

It had not been cut by somebody careless. It had been cut by somebody on time.

The pad was still a pad. The log was still a lie. The wound was still open to the air with nothing over it. None of that had changed. All of it had changed, because the wound was not the result of a mistake, it was the result of a *programme*, and a programme has no end, and the next execution on this node was in ten days and the local holder was the cooperative and had never been told and the notification was not in the schedule as drafted.

"They are going to come back on the twenty-seventh," he said. "Not somebody. A crew, with a die. That is what the two marks mean — that is a crew of two coming here with a stamp on a piece of wood and doing the same cut again because a table says it is due, and they will not ask the cooperative's permission because the cooperative is not the holder of that branch, and they will not tell the cooperative because notification is not in the schedule as drafted. Bray said that on the tenth in front of thirty people."

"Then somebody in this fen is going to be cut twice on the twenty-seventh," said Anneke Roos.

"Yes."

"And it will be the same cut. On the same branch. Two hundred yards from my family's road and a slab with a dish in the top of it."

"Yes," Marek said, "and I have been up there and I know exactly where the third one lands, and I have the drawing, and I have a lamp-keeper's signature on it, and none of that is any use at all, because the drawing proves when it happened and not whether it should happen, and I have spent nine days proving a thing that was not the thing."

Tova Reed said, "You have proved when. It is a different thing from what you thought, and it is the first useful thing this fen has had, and I would like to be careful, because I am about to be the one who says the next part and the next part is a gift and I do not give gifts."

"Give it."

"You have a book that you have refused to file, and a page in it that you wrote in the margin, and a section of the charter schedule that says an entry by an officer, dated and signed, is evidence of what the cooperative intended and is not evidence of what it did." She put her hand on Dunmar's closed book. "Mr Fitch has been carrying a bypass in the margin since the eleventh of this month. He is going to have to carry a second note, and the two of them are the same kind of note, and I want both of them in the same hand, and I want them on the same page, because in a year somebody is going to read that book and they need to see that this cooperative makes notes about things it cannot afford to do and is honest about which ones they are."

Dunmar Fitch opened the book to the margin, and to the page number, and wrote the date at the top of the next one and gave Marek the pen.

He wrote it, and it was short, and it was the second time in a week he had written a plan nobody had money for, and his mother's rules for the margin had been learned on the eleventh and had cost him nothing then and cost him a charter on Wednesday.

*Seventeenth. Item N-2214/K appears in a district works schedule produced to this cooperative on the seventeenth in answer to our application for renewal, with a further execution entered against it on the twenty-seventh of this month. Two earlier executions are entered as done and correspond to the two stamped cut faces on the branch at Weir Row, of which the cooperative holds a measured drawing and a witness signature. The cooperative was not notified before either execution, notification is not in the schedule as drafted, and the district has confirmed in the record of the tenth that no local holder exists for that branch. The cooperative holds the node and does not hold that branch. The cooperative has no right to stop the execution of the twenty-seventh and no notice that it will occur. This is recorded as what the cooperative knows and not as what the cooperative has done, and it is not a filing, and nobody has signed it but me.*

*This is a note, and it is worth nothing against a cut.*

Dunmar entered the page number and the hour and the fact that the entry had been made by the engineer of record and by nobody else, and then he said, without any pleasure at all, that the sub-office clerk's own rule about writing one line of it down was the rule he had just watched Mr Vale break, and that he would carry the number, and that a number in a cooperative book is a number a district clerk can find, and that he was going to find it himself the first time anybody came looking; and then he said that he had filled in the first box of his own application — *works already scheduled on this node* — with the words *none known*, on the seventeenth of this month, at seven in the morning, in a form that went to the district, and that it had been true when he wrote it, and that as of noon it was not, and that he would have to say so to whoever asked, and that he was not going to be asked, because Inspector Bray had spent an hour on the tenth reading work and not paperwork, and that a cooperative's whole survival in this district rested on one inspector happening to be interested in the wrong thing.

Nobody said that aloud. It went in the margin anyway, in his hand, as a second line, because it was the sort of thing this book now had in it.

Perrin Osse gathered up the sheet and folded it once and put it in her satchel and stood.

"Mr Vale. You did not copy it and I am going to say in the district's record that you did not copy it and that it was produced by me and read by five people and returned to me."

"Return it," Marek said.

"I am returning it. And I am telling you, because I have been a clerk for twenty-two years and I have never yet seen a cooperative hand one back faster than I have offered it, and I would like somebody in Fennmere to hear that it happened here." She got her satchel on her shoulder and stopped at the door. "The twenty-seventh is a Friday. I looked at the calendar when I read the form, and I have said it out loud in case anybody in this room has a different one. If a crew comes on the twenty-seventh they will come on the twenty-seventh, and if they come they will not come for a conversation, and there is no form on this earth on which a cooperative can say no to it, because the form on which it might be said is the form that says *no local holder*."

"I know," Marek said.

"You do not, quite," said Perrin Osse. "You know it as a wrong. You are going to know it on Friday as a thing that is happening in a field you are standing in, and those are not the same, and I have watched men be surprised by the second one after being entirely right about the first."

Tomorrow was the eighteenth.
