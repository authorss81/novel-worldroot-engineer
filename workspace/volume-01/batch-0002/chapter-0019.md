# Chapter 19

## What He Went To The Table For

He asked for it at twenty past seven in the morning, at his mother's table, with five people in the room, because there was no other way to ask for it and she had told him there would not be.

He did it badly the first time, and that is on the page as well.

"The fieldbook," he said. "I would like it."

Lissa Vale did not put her pen down. "Say what for."

"To read the head during the load test."

"No." She said it immediately, and before she had finished saying it, and Marek heard how fast it came and understood that she had had the answer ready since the eleventh, when she had written the use log out on the first page of a new book and said *I will say no more often than I say yes, and I will not tell you which before you have said it.*

"No," she said again, and then she stopped, and put the pen down. "I have said no twice and I will say it a third time if you keep talking. Now the other thing, which you have not said, and I have been waiting for it since twenty past seven, and that is the whole of what is wrong with you and has been for six years."

Marek stood at the table in the seed house with his hands at his sides and had the answer ready and did not say it, because what he had come to say was *I need it*, and what he should have said was that he did not need it and could not be trusted with it.

"I cannot read it," he said. "I have had it twice and I have four seconds each time and I cannot tell afterwards whether a thing is mine. Ms Quill is the licensed reader and the fieldbook has to be on the seam and somebody licensed has to have the book in her hands, and that somebody cannot be me, because the last two times it was me I was alone in a pit when it happened, and if I do it a third time I will be alone in a pit a third time and I will come up with a page and no way of having taken it."

The room had been extremely quiet.

"Thank you," Lissa Vale said, and her voice was not gentle and it was not unkind, and it was not the voice of a woman who had been waiting for something. "Now write down what you actually said, and write it in the form, and do not write *read the head*."

They did it at the table, with the book open, in front of the five.

Lissa Vale wrote the line in her square hand and read it back.

*Twentieth day, seventh hour and twenty minutes. Fieldbook, produced by Mrs Vale from the press, at the request of M. Vale, at the seed house table, in the presence of T. Reed, D. Fitch, A. Roos and G. Senn. Purpose: to be used by Sera Quill, licensed engineer, at the copper seam of the well head, under supervision, for a load test on this day, and for no other purpose. Licensed person present: S. Quill. M. Vale will not touch the instrument and will stand outside the housing.*

*Out. Back.*

"Sign under it," Lissa said.

Marek signed. Tova Reed initialled it as a witness, because Tova Reed initialled everything, and Dunmar Fitch entered the page number and the hour, and Anneke Roos read the whole line out loud before she put her name to it, which she had done three times in a week and which everybody in the room now treated as an ordinary thing about her.

Then Lissa Vale got up, and went to the press, and lifted the ledger and the oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the table in front of all of them, and the strap had gone stiff again in nine days and had to be worked loose with a thumbnail, and eleven pages and a bead in a copper collar and a dead man's hand at the bottom of the last page with a year in it that could not be true.

"Four o'clock," she said. "Back on this table, whatever happens, and if the head goes or the weather turns or the district comes up the road, it comes back on this table and you come and tell me and I enter the hour it came back and not the hour you had time to bring it. And Marek—"

"Yes."

"I said no twice before I said yes and I am not going to apologise for the two, and I want you to sit with the fact that the first two were the right answer to what you asked and the third was the right answer to what you meant, and that you did not say what you meant until I made you."

---

Sera Quill was on the boards in the housing at ten past nine with the fieldbook open on her knees, the copper clip fitted at the seam, and Marek on the step outside the collar with his hands in his pockets and his back to the wind.

The wind was off the north-east again and it was colder than it had been any day that month, and the water in the pit was clear for two feet and black under that, and the sacking on the seal had been dry on Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday and it was going to be tested at ten.

That was the choice, and it had been in front of Marek since a licensed woman put the words *a load test* into a price in a pump shed on Sunday, and he had got to it at last at a quarter to ten on the twentieth with a licensed woman holding his father's book and four people in a yard waiting to be told what the pump was doing.

"You can hold it at seven hundred and twenty," Sera Quill said, without looking up. "That is what the ground will take, that is what the log should have said, and a seal that holds at seven hundred and twenty is a seal that will hold for the use this place is going to make. Write that and I will sign it."

"And eleven hundred?"

"You take the head down by something close to a third below the working point and you hold it there for as long as you like to hold it there, and the wound in that housing will be under more pressure than it has been under since the flood, because there is less water above it and the same thing pressing at it." She read a number off the fieldbook's dial and entered it in a column. "It is the load this node has actually been carrying for six years according to every number in your charter. Nobody has ever tested a seal at the load it will be asked to carry. Every Crown pad I have ever seen was tested at nothing."

"And if it goes?"

"Then it goes in front of five people, at the tenth hour, in a pit, and I have no tar and you have no compound and I will be there with a cloth and a lamp and a sheet of paper saying that the cooperative's hand-built holding seal failed at a full draw, and there will be nothing in this fen to put over your wound until somebody buys something, and they will not, because there is no charter to attach the bill to."

Marek put his hand on the rail of the well house.

"Read it," he said. "I cannot do it. I cannot do it, and I want to be in the record that I said so, and I want you to read it in front of the four of them and not in front of me, and I want to know what it says before I choose, because that is the only time I am ever going to be able to do that again."

Sera Quill looked up at him. Then she put two fingers on the page, and her hand stopped, and the yard went quiet in the way a yard goes quiet when something is about to be read out.

> The dressing is holding.  
> Full load will show you the wood.  
> Choose.

He never touched the book. He heard it a moment later, in her voice, flatly, with the numbers in, the way she read everything — and the yard heard it after that, all four of them, because she read it out because a reading taken in a pit is not a reading until somebody else has heard it.

"Full load will show you the wood," Sera Quill said. "Choose. I am required to leave the choice with you and I am leaving it with you, and I would like it entered that I said so, and I would like it entered that you asked me for it before you chose, which in thirty years is the fourth time, and the first three men did not ask."

"Enter both," said Marek.

"Full load," he said. "Seven hundred and twenty is a lie about a load. If the node is ever asked for eleven hundred again it will be asked on a day when nobody is watching, and the seal will be on the head in a pit in a flood with nobody in it. Eleven hundred today, and it is written down, and if it holds then it has held at the load."

Anneke Roos wrote the hour and the load and the reason in one line, and read it back, and the licensed column was filled.

---

The load came up in three stages and it ran from half past ten until one.

Sera Quill logged it. Gael Senn read the hours out. Jonas Byre, who had not been down a ladder in two days and was standing on the boards above the pit with his arms folded and his chest wrapped in three thicknesses of wool, read the dial and called it, and called it out of habit in the voice he had used for the mill tally for thirty years, and Anneke Roos wrote it down and did not have to look at the dial to check him, and nobody in the yard said anything about the fact that the man who was not going down into the pit was the one whose numbers the load test depended on.

At seven hundred and twenty, which they held from the half hour after ten, the head came to a steady pressure and the sacking stayed dry and the clamp on the return line was singing.

At nine hundred the head went soft and the pitch changed and the water in the pit came up a quarter of an inch and stood there.

At eleven hundred, which was reached at about twenty past eleven, the whole four-foot frame of the well house went tight, and the pump laboured, and Marek had his hand on Ilsa Kern's arm because the frame was humming in a way the frame had not hummed before, and he wrote down *frame humming at full draw, from about nine hundred and fifty* and *no audible change at the wound*.

And the beat came up about ten minutes into the full draw, with eleven hundred held and the pump labouring.

It came up through the boards and through the soles of everybody standing on them, one about every four seconds, and this time it was in runs of seven and eight, and it was in the frame and it was in the housing and it came up through the pit water. Gael Senn counted it and put the count in the log and did not give it a name, because the one name in this business is *pressure pulse* and that is the licensed word for it and it is a finding, and a lamp-keeper was not going to enter a finding. The licensed engineer wrote the times and the counts and the words *cause not established* underneath, and initialled it, and that is the only reason the sentence exists anywhere in the world.

Nobody stopped the pump. It was run to eleven hundred and held there until one, and the reading that follows was taken at the load, and Marek wrote the load into the log before the reading and not after, in his own hand, with the hour against it, and that is the only reason the rest of the page can be trusted.

At half past twelve, with the load still at eleven hundred and the frame still humming, Sera Quill lifted the sacking at the six o'clock corner.

She did not touch the seal. She lifted the corner of the cloth and held the lamp down into the gap and looked for a long time, and then she let it down and looked at Marek, and Marek had already seen the dark on the underside of the sacking and had known for about a minute and had been waiting for her.

"That is not a leak," she said.

"No."

"It is a weep. The pack has gone in where the pack went in last time, at the low point, and the face is sweating because the face is working." She got a rule out of the roll and held the lamp down into the gap and measured it, twice, and wrote it down before she said the rest of it. "A quarter of an inch. Half an inch is where I stop calling it a weep, and at a full draw a leak under a sacking does not go into the pit, it goes into the housing, and I want somebody in this building to know that before it happens in February and not after." She entered the width, the load, and the hour, and wrote *not a leak, at the load and hour stated, on the twentieth day* underneath it, and signed it and turned it round and gave it to Marek. "That is a document, and it is dated, and it is the only piece of paper in this whole business that is worth anything to anybody except us."

Then she did the other thing, and she did it in the open pit with the pump running at full draw and five people in a yard, and she did not do it because she wanted to.

She opened the fieldbook to the last page, and read out the line of figures and the column head and the figure and the year, in a voice with nothing in it, and then she read Finding 2 and Finding 3 out of the nine pages in her own case, in her own hand, taken from the operator by dictation on the eighth of the month, and then she read the thing about a shaft and a lamp and a pair of hands.

"That is what the book said on the eighth, and it is on the district's record, and Mr Vale read it out in a seed house in front of thirty people and told them it was the least reliable thing anybody had said that day." She turned the book round so that the page faced up in the light. "Now I am going to tell you what it says today, at the same seam, at full draw, on the twentieth, and I am going to read it out because five people have to hear it, and I am not going to write it down as a finding, because it is not a finding. It is four seconds and it is mine this time, and I have had four hundred of them, and I have never once written one down for anybody else. And I am going to say the thing about it first, because somebody will notice it in about a year and I would rather they heard it from me.

"On the eighth it gave a man a sequence of working actions on a well. Valve, hand, wait, valve, lamp, valve. That is maintenance. That is somebody doing a job to a machine on a schedule, and the shaft and the lamp and his father's hands were not in it, they were in him, and I have told him so twice and I will tell him so again if he asks. Today, at the same book, on the same node, at eleven hundred turns, it gave a working sequence with no well in it at all. No shaft, no lamp, no hands, and nothing in the order that belongs to anything above ground."

She stopped and waited until somebody asked, and Anneke Roos asked, because Anneke Roos understood what a silence at a well head is for.

"Cut," Sera Quill said. "Wait. Cut. And I recognised the order before I had finished it, and I recognised it because I have been reading about the Weir Row branch for three days, and that is the order that branch is in, and the wood has had it done to it twice."

"That is the finding I am not writing down," she said, and closed two fingers on the page. "Not that it means anything. That a fieldbook reads what a node has recently had done to it, and that on the eighth the node had recently had maintenance done to it, and today the node has recently had something else, and I cannot tell you what the something else is, and I am not going to, because I have been asked twice already this month to say where I saw the shape of this and what it is, and I refused twice, and I am refusing a third time this morning, in a yard, with a book open on my knees, and the third refusal is the same two refusals and I would like all three entered."

"Entered," said Dunmar Fitch, and entered them, in the cooperative's own book, with the hour, in the clerk's square hand.

"Write one more line under them," Sera Quill said, "in my words, and get it right: *a licensed reader at the same seam, at the same node, under full draw, on the twentieth, did not obtain the sequence obtained on the eighth. Both sequences are logged. The difference is unaccounted for.* That is not nothing. It is the only reason I would put my name under either of them."

"What matters this morning," she said, "is that the certificate is on the satchel and it is on a piece of paper and the load was held at eleven hundred turns for two and a half hours with five people watching and a licensed woman's name at the top of it. I cannot certify a node in front of a district officer today, because there is no charter to certify it against. I can certify what happened this morning, and that is all, and I have written it, and Mr Vale is going to have to decide whether that is worth what it cost."

---

She wrote the certificate at the well house rail at two in the afternoon, and read it out, and then she came to the bottom of it and stopped with the pen on the page.

"Every holding repair requires a return," she said. "I told you in the pump shed on Sunday night. Something goes back in, on a day, in an amount, from a name. I have not got compost, I have got a pound of fiber off the cooperative's own pad, and I have got a signed return for the work in the log. So the return I am writing into this document is the maintenance, and it is monthly, and the charter does not apply and I am writing it anyway because it is the only thing I know that will keep that wound closed. It needs a day and an amount and a name. The first of every month. An examination, and something put into that return main, and it gets entered in your standing pressure log and it gets initialled. I am not writing anybody's name into it, because I do not know which of you four will still be standing here in January and I am not going to guess in a document."

There was a pause of the length of about a breath.

"Miss Senn," Anneke Roos said.

Sera Quill turned round. "What?"

"The lamp-keeper is twenty-three and she is the only one of us who has ever said out loud that she wants to do this work for the rest of her life, and if you put her name in a document in January with a hole in it about compost, she will be the one who carries it, and she is the one who ought to carry it, and she is the one who can be trusted to write down that it was not done if it is not done." Anneke Roos said it without any softness at all. "That is not me being kind. That is me putting the job where the person is who will not lie about it in February."

Gael Senn stood in a yard in the cold at two in the afternoon and did not say anything for long enough that Marek got a step toward her and had to stop himself.

"I have not seen the cause," she said, "and I am not asked to, and I will put my name to the first of every month and I will write down what I find and if I find nothing I will write *nothing found* and not *all well*." She looked at Sera Quill. "Write it that way, please. *Nothing found* is a thing you check. *All well* is a thing you did not."

Sera Quill wrote *nothing found* into the certificate, and signed it, and then put the pen down in front of Gael Senn and let a twenty-three-year-old lamp-keeper sign the first holding-maintenance entry in the history of the Lantern Reach Work Cooperative. Then she turned the page round again and gave it to Marek, and he signed under both of them without being asked, and Lissa Vale, who had come out of the seed house to ask what the noise was and had stayed, entered the hour at the foot of it because that is what she does.

Three names on it. Sera Quill's first, Gael Senn's second, and his own third and shortest, and there was no instrument anywhere in the world that he was allowed to open by himself.

At four in the afternoon the fieldbook was back on the seed house table, wrapped, and Lissa Vale entered the hour it came back in the form she had written out on the eleventh, and Marek signed under it, and the eleven pages went into the press under the ledger with the oilcloth over them.

Sera Quill caught him at the pump shed door on her way out and took his sleeve and turned his arm up into the light without asking, the way she had done on the eighth day and on the twelfth, and looked at the inside of it from the wrist to the elbow for a count of about four.

"Nothing," she said.

"Nothing."

"Nothing," she said, and let go of his arm, and then she said the thing she had said twice before, and it was worse the third time, because the third time meant it was coming. "Look at it twice a week for a year and write down the first time it is not nothing, in your own book and not in mine. I have said it three times now and I am going to stop saying it, because the fourth time it comes out of my mouth it will be a nag, and you are twenty-nine and you will live to be seventy."

The sacking on the seal in the pit was lifted again at ten that night by a lamp-keeper and a tenant and an engineer of record, because a day like that one is not a day you leave a wound unwatched until morning. The dark place at the six o'clock corner was a quarter of an inch, the same quarter of an inch as at half past twelve with eleven hundred turns on, and it had not gone anywhere. Anneke Roos wrote *one quarter inch, tenth hour of the twentieth, load off, no change since half past twelve* and signed it, and the licensed person present column said *none*, and nobody wrote anything under it, because there was nobody to write anything.
