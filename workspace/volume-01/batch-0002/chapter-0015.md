# Chapter 15

## A Letter With Somebody Else's Name On It

Bram Oskell was at the gate at eight with a saddlebag and a bad coat and the expression of a man who had ridden eleven miles before breakfast and would do it again inside a week.

The four of them had been in the well house since first light, because the wound had been open to the air for eighteen hours and there was a licensed woman standing over it who was going to leave at half past four, and the pit had a brazier in it and four withies cut and drying on the pit rim and a board laid across with a tray of clay and a sieve on it. Marek came up off the boards and wiped his hands on his coat and went out to the gate, and Oskell held out a folded paper without saying good morning.

"District seal," Oskell said. "Sub-office. Served this morning on the charter holder and copied to the cooperative, and the sub-office clerk was on the road behind me with the register and will want an answer in her own hand, so if you have one, write it now and give it to me and not to her."

"Read it to me."

"It is a page and a half and I have read it twice on the road and I can read it again for a living." Oskell turned it round. "The Well Directorate, Tarrow district, offers the cooperative of Lantern Reach, as a monitored repair under the emergency-maintenance schedule: material from the Fennmere yard delivered on the day, a two-person crew for six working days, a licensed engineer of the district's choosing retained by the district, and a monthly inspection for twelve months at the district's cost. In consideration, exclusive access to the head, the housing, and the node for the term, the term being the life of the charter, the term being renewable only by the district."

Marek read the paragraph twice himself.

"Who signed the second paragraph," he said.

"Nobody. It is in the second paragraph that Mr Rusk says he has nothing to do with it, in writing, with his name on it." Oskell shifted the bag. "I have been a contract clerk for nineteen years and that is the first time a charter holder has written that sentence to me and it cost him something to write it."

"And the compound."

"If you mean tar, yes. Fortyweight, and a proper seal specification, and a licensed man to lay it, and the layout is a Crown layout and it will read as a Crown layout on any form the district chooses to put in front of anybody."

Marek folded the paper back up and stood with it in his hand for a moment, in the yard, in the cold, looking at the housing with the collar off and the wound open and a wire cradle with four unused sockets in it that had been there since before anybody alive had any idea what they were for.

"Oskell," he said. "I want a thing from Mr Rusk and it is not in this."

"Ask."

"His compound book. The batch weights and the pot temperatures and the four seasons of dressing dates and what he put on it in each year and in what order. I am going to lay a holding seal today with no tar in it out of the fiber I took off that wound yesterday morning, and I have never in my life seen a pad come off in layers, and whatever that man laid on this wound for four seasons he wrote down, and I cannot do this without it, and I know exactly how little that will get me."

Oskell looked at him for a second.

"That is Mr Rusk's whole life," he said, "and I have been in the pump house nine years. I will take it and I will be back today, because he will know in about a minute what you have asked for and he will want to answer it himself and I would not stand in the way of that for a fee."

---

The sub-office clerk, Perrin Osse, came in at ten with the register, and the four of them were in the pit by then, and Marek came up and read the offer out loud from the rim with the wind taking the corner of it, because he wanted the people who were about to go into the hole to hear what the alternative to the hole was.

"Fortyweight of tar, six days of a two-person crew, a licensed man of the district's choosing, a monthly inspection for a year, and the head, the housing and the node to the district for the life of the charter."

Gael Senn said, "That is everything," from about a foot down the ladder.

"That is everything," Marek agreed. "And it is not a favour and nobody thinks it is, and I am not going to insult Mr Oskell by pretending it is. What it is, is the whole of the district's money and the whole of the district's licensed hands, in exchange for the thing this cooperative owns."

He got a stub of pencil and did it on the face of the page, in the margin, in his own hand, where it could not be mistaken for a clerk's entry. He struck out the words *exclusive access to the head, the housing, and the node for the term*. He wrote under it: *Refused. The cooperative will not grant exclusive access to a node it is asking to be shown it maintains, and a monitored repair under exclusive access cannot be shown to be maintained by the people who live on it, because the people who live on it are not allowed to touch it.*

Then he wrote the second line, which cost more, and which he wrote in the same hand: *Refused on the sixteenth day, in the presence of the district's clerk, and of the four hands who are doing the work instead. The material is not the reason. The condition is the reason. The material would be accepted and paid for if the condition were not attached to it.*

And he signed it, and turned it round, and gave it to Oskell.

"Read that back," he said, "and if I have got a word of it wrong, say so now, because in an hour it will be a copy in Fennmere and I cannot get at it again."

Oskell read it back. Perrin Osse read it over his shoulder, and then took it, and entered in the register, in her square hand, the hour and the place and the names of the four who had been in the pit, and said, without looking up: "I will record that the offer was declined in the presence of the district's clerk. It is the third refusal this cooperative has entered since the third day. You may want to think about what three refusals in a fortnight reads like in a file."

"I have thought about it," Marek said. "It reads like a cooperative that would rather be wrong than held."

"Enter that if you like," said Perrin Osse, "but I shall not, because the district does not admire it."

She shut the register and walked out to the gate and got on her horse, and Oskell went after her with the paper, and the yard was quiet for about a minute.

"That was well done," Anneke Roos said, from the ladder, in a flat voice. "And I want to say one thing about it and then I never want to hear it again. That is the last tar in this fen that was going to be laid over this wound by a man who knows what he is doing, and we have just given it back. We are going to lay green withies and a piece of seed-house sacking over a live face this afternoon, and if it goes in February it goes because we said no to the only person in this district who could have stopped it going."

"Yes," Marek said.

"So write it in the book, in the working notes, not the open book, the working notes, with the hour. I do not want it in the open book where somebody reads it and thinks we are proud of ourselves. I want it where we will find it in February."

He wrote it in the working notes at the hour, and Anneke Roos read it back and initialled it, and it was the first entry in the whole season that she initialled instead of signed, and the reason she did not sign it is that she was not doing the work in question and she was not going to put her name on her opinion.

---

The woman at the gate with the chain came at two in the afternoon.

She had a tool roll over one shoulder and a crutch of new chain over the other and she was somewhere near fifty and she was not from the fen. Marek knew she was not from the fen because nobody from the fen stood at a gate and asked permission.

"Chain's for the mill sluice," she said, before he had got to her. "I mend implements. Locks, hinges, chains, hasps, well-covers, cart-tips, anything with an eye in it. I go round the tool-roads, which are the old peat roads, because that is where the people who need mending are and not where the tanners are."

"Mend it and I will pay you."

"You will pay me in bread and you will tell the mill-keeper to stop telling people I was here." She dropped the crutch by the gate post. "I'll do the sluice first and your well-house hasp after. I have had a look at the four eyes in your frame and I am not mending those, because a thing with four eyes in it is not a thing you hang a chain on and I would not know what to tell you to hang there."

So Marek sat on the gate post and let her work and did not ask her anything at all for long enough that she stopped waiting for him to.

She gave her name on her own, out loud, in the middle of it, the way a tradesman does when he is going to be asked to stand behind something: Marda Vole. He wrote it in a working notebook that evening, and he did not enter it in the cooperative's book until the nineteenth, with a witness, and the reason he waited two days is that a warning from a stranger is worth nothing whatever until somebody in a room has said they heard it.

"You are going to ask me about the number," she said, to the chain.

"I was not going to ask you about anything."

"You were going to ask me about the number and you have been not asking me about it for twenty minutes, which is worse, because it means you have worked out that I came to say it." She turned a link with a thumb. "I am going to say it once, and then I am going to mend your hasp, and then I am going to go to Cray and mend a lock for a woman who pays in wool, and if you want it in a book you can put it in a book, and I will put my name to it, and I will not be here again this year and you should not look for me."

"Say it."

"N-2214. District N, item 2214, K cycle. That number is die-struck into branches in three other provinces, on four different holdings, and the die is the same because a die is a thing you buy once." She put a new link in and closed it with her thumb. "I have not seen the two threes-one-eight, so I am not telling you about that one, and I am not going to say whose branches or what the branches are doing now, because I have not been there and I do not report on a thing I have not been on. Four men in four provinces have told me the same number, three of them had never met each other, two of them said it to me for no money, and one of them wanted to know if I had heard it before and would not tell me anything else afterwards."

Marek had the notebook out and his pencil had stopped about an inch over the page.

"Why me," he said.

"Because you copied it out in block capitals in front of a lamp-keeper on a Monday and told a man in a bad coat you wanted it on paper, and he told somebody, and the somebody tells people who mend things." She tested the link with her boot. "And because you are the only person in this fen who has ever written down a thing that was not his to write down."

He wrote it, and put the date, and put *reported by M. Vole, mender, at the gate, second hour* and *not witnessed by any member of this cooperative*, and read it back to her.

"That will do," she said. "Do not put my trade in it. Put *mender* and put the hour, and if anybody ever wants to know whether I was believed, they can come and find out."

Then she said, "One more, and it is not about the number, and it is the only thing I have come for, and I will go if you are not interested."

"Go on."

"You are going to be refused a table." She said it the way she said everything, without weight. "Nobody gives a cooperative the schedule. Not the clerk, not the inspector, not in ten years. If you petition for it you will be refused on the ground that it is not germane to a charter condition, and the refusal will be correct, and Perrin Osse will write it in her square hand and mean it."

"Then how does anybody see one?"

She got up off the gate post and shouldered the crutch.

"You do not ask for the table," she said. "You fill in the renewal. And when they send you the renewal back they send you the schedule with it, because they have to, because the first box on that form says *works already scheduled on this node* and a man in an office who sent you a renewal with an empty box would be sending you a form he has not read. They send you the extract so that you do not duplicate work somebody else is already doing. That is the whole of the reason. It is not a favour and it is not a leak. It is a clerk saving himself a letter, and clerks will save themselves a letter every day of their lives."

Marek looked at the housing, and at the open collar, and at the four unused sockets in the wire cradle.

"How many rows," he said.

"I have not seen it," said Marda Vole. "I have been in four provinces and I have never seen a table. I know the shape of the number and I know the address of a man in Cray who died in the winter, and I have told you both, and that is the whole of what I have." She picked up her roll. "Now I will do your hasp, and I will do it properly, and I will not charge you, and you will not say thank you for it either, because the chain is what I came for and the rest of the afternoon is what I charge for. Is that fair."

"It is fair."

"There." She got a hook out of the roll. "And I will say this once and then I will not say it again. If a form ever comes to you with a number in a box that is not a number, you fill it in as the number says and not as you would like it to say, because that box is the only part of that form anybody in your position will ever be believed about. Everything else on it is your opinion."

---

Oskell came back off the fen road at dusk with his bag over the saddle and a face like a man who had ridden twenty-two miles.

The four of them had finished the seal at ten past four and got themselves out of the pit and stood about in the yard in the dark looking at a thing that looked like nothing, and Sera Quill had read the working notes and initialled the last one and gone up to the road at half past with her case, and the four of them had come in out of the cold to hear what Oskell had brought.

"Mr Rusk says no," he said. "He says it in writing and he would not let me write it for him, and he stood at his own bench and wrote it himself, and I watched him do it, and I will tell you the odd thing about it, which is that he read it out to me twice and corrected it both times and it got shorter both times."

Marek took it inside and read it by the lamp at the pump shed bench with four people standing about.

It was four lines.

*Mr Vale. You ask for my compound book. The compound book is the only record in this district of how that pad was built, and it is the record that the pad is what I said it was, and it is the record that the acceptance column in your charter is not a lie about a seal, because there is no seal in it and never has been. If I give you the book, then a district officer has a document in which the cooperative's engineer of record used my base coat specification to lay a holding repair, and there is no way on this earth for that document to say what it actually is. You will take what you have taken out of my pit and lay it with withies, which will be worse than what I laid and cheaper than what I laid, and you will call it a seal because you need the word, and in February one of us will have to say the word out loud in a room with a coat in it.*

*I will not hand you the book and I will not come back and lay one. Not because of the accusation in your district file, which is that I emptied a pit before an inspection and which is true. Because a man who has dressed a wound once a year for four years for nothing is not a man anybody should be able to turn into a witness for a coat, and you have been trying to do that to me since the fourth day, and I have not said so before because saying so would have helped you.*

*Read that last sentence again.*

*Marek put it down. He read it again, because Oskell was watching him, and then he said, out loud, to four people in a pump shed, "He is right. That is what I have been doing since the fourth day, and he is the only man in this district who has had the nerve to say so to my face, and he is right and I am not going to argue with him in front of you."

"You are not going to argue with him because you agree with him," said Gael Senn.

"I am not going to argue with him because he has written it down and dated it, and that is more than I have done about it, and if I argue with it now I am doing it for free and he gets to be the only one who said anything." Marek folded the letter. "Oskell. Tell him it came in a book, and that I have entered it, and that I am not going to answer it this week. And tell him this, in front of these four, and write it down if you want to write it down: the cooperative will use its own pad and its own withies and its own hands, and it will not call it a seal, because he is right, and it is not a seal, and the word is going to be somebody else's problem on Wednesday."

Oskell wrote it down. He put the book in the bag and said, at the gate, "He will be here on Wednesday, and he will be here because he is the charter holder of record and has to be, and he has been telling everybody he does not intend to come for a fortnight. I would not tell him I said that." He got his leg over.

---

Marek got the levy paper out of the cooperative box at nine that night, which was the only time he had, because everything else about the day had gone on the road or into a pit.

It was not a motion. He did not put it to a vote and he did not consult the meeting, and he wrote that down on the paper in the top corner, in his own hand, and dated it, so that nobody could later say he had decided it in a room: *Not a motion of the cooperative. A paper, at the seed house table, at the ninth hour, signed by anybody who wishes. The name of anybody who does not sign is not to be recorded.*

Sixteen households. Fourteen pounds. He wrote the number out in words and in figures and then he crossed out the figures, because a figure is the one thing people argue with.

*Fourteen pounds, to pay a licensed engineer fourteen pounds, on Friday, in advance, for a finding on a wound and a signature on a page. The finding is worth everything or nothing. If it is not raised, she does not come, there is no certificate, and on Wednesday a man from the district finds an open wound with nothing over it.*

Then, because that was a paragraph and not a sentence, he wrote the sentence too:

*Fourteen pounds does not buy a week of compost. Compost is eighty pounds a month. If you give this and it is right, you will have made the seed house colder in February, and you should know that before you sign.*

Anneke Roos read it standing up, twice, and then went to the end of the table and picked up the pen.

"Add one line," she said. "And do not write *inspection*, because an inspection is a word for a thing the district does to you. Write what it is."

"What is it?"

"It is a woman's fee," said Anneke Roos, "and the woman is putting her own name on top of his, and we are raising it out of sixteen houses to pay her, and that is the sentence. Write that one and I will sign it and I will not feel like a fool at the end of the week."

He wrote it, and she signed it, and it went on the well-house door where anybody coming in could see it, and by the light of the lamp at ten o'clock there were three more names on it, and the mill boy had asked whether his house counted, and was told that it did not, and had gone off to tell his mother anyway, and the mill boy's mother came out into the yard at twenty past ten to say that it did count and that it was fourpence, and Dunmar Fitch entered fourpence separately and told her so, and she went away satisfied.
