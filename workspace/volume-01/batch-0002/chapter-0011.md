# Chapter 11

## What Her Name On It Costs

The water in the pit had not gone down in four days, and Sera Quill stood at the head of the ladder with a lamp hooked to her own belt and her coat folded over the rail and said, before Marek had got his boots off, "You are not going down there."

"I was going to ask."

"You were going to stand there until I said something, and I have been watching you do it since the seventh day." She went past him and put one boot on the second rung and tested it with her weight. "The board across the pit is the fourth one along and it is the only one that is not rotten. Do not stand on the third. Do not put your hand on the copper seam. And do not tell me about the seam."

"I was not going to touch the seam."

"You looked at it, which is worse, because looking at it is how it starts." She went down. "Get the other end of that plank and hold it level. If it is out of level I will come up and say so in a voice you will not enjoy."

Ilsa Kern came out of the mill shed with the plank on her shoulder and set it across the pit, and Marek held the near end while Sera Quill got herself down onto the fourth board with a lamp in her teeth and both hands on the conduit.

She was in the pit a long time.

He watched from the top. Twice she said something to the wood that he could not hear. Once she stopped for a while and sat back on her heels and put a thumb on something and left the thumb there long enough that he thought she had forgotten what she had come down for. The water in the pit was clear enough to see two feet down and black below that, and the pit was four feet deep and a foot of it was water, and she was standing in three inches of cold in iron water in a coat she had folded over a rail.

Ilsa Kern stood at Marek's shoulder with her arms folded and did not say anything at all, which from Ilsa was a comment.

Then Sera Quill came up, and got out of the pit, and dried her hands on a cloth she produced out of her own case, and did not sit down, and said:

"Two things. You will not like the second one."

"Go on."

"The first is yours. The pad is not a seal, the log is a lie, and you have known that since the first morning, and you have written it in three books, and it is correct." She put the cloth away. "The second one is Mr Rusk's, and it is also correct, and I have been standing in that pit for twenty minutes trying to decide whether I am allowed to say it, and I have decided that I am, because you are going to find it out in about a fortnight and it is better that you find out from me with a witness."

Marek waited.

"There is a callus on that wound." She turned and looked at the housing, not at him. "There is a new lip of wood across the top of the cut, and it has been growing in the wet for four seasons under four seasons of tar, and it has closed about a third of the face. Not the whole face. A third. And the third is on the top, where the water runs off, and it is clean and it is pale and it is the strongest wood in the structure."

"Then it has been closing."

"It has been closing," Sera Quill said. "It has been closing for four years, and it has been closing because a man dressed a coat of tar over it once a year, and the coat is not a seal and it is not good work and it is four coats, and it is the only reason that lip is a lip and not a stain. If that wound had been cut back to sound wood and dressed properly in the first season it would have closed by the third. If it had not been dressed at all, at any point in four years, this morning it would be a foot longer than it is and you would be looking at the shoulder of the conduit instead of two hands of it."

Ilsa Kern said, "Say the rest of it."

"I am going to say the rest of it because nobody else in this yard is going to and I am tired of being tidy." Sera Quill picked up her coat and did not put it on. "So: your engineer's log is a lie. It has been a lie in the column that matters for six years and a lot of people signed it, and I am going to put that in a document this afternoon. And the man who wrote it dressed a wound he was not paid to be near, once a year, for four years, for no reason anybody could put in a sentence except that he did not want it to be worse, and he has never once said so out loud, and I do not know how to write those two facts in one line of a page and I am going to have to try."

Nobody in the yard said anything for a moment. Somewhere out on the fen a crow went off and came back.

"That is the finding," Marek said.

"That is the finding as I will write it, and the second half of it is going to be the half that gets quoted." She looked at him then. "Are you satisfied?"

"No," Marek said. "But it is true, and I would rather have it true in a document than kind in a conversation. What is the bad half?"

"There is no bad half."

"Ms Quill."

"There are two things in that pit, Mr Vale, and both of them are on the page, and you have got six days. One of them is going to get worse on its own and one of them is going to get better on its own, and neither of them is going to wait for you." She put her coat on. "The bad half is the other thing I came down for, and I am going to say it now, and I would like you to stand still while I say it."

He stood still.

"You are not going in that pit again."

The wind came off the mill race and went round the housing the way it did.

"I was not going to be in it," he said. "I was going to look at it. There is a difference and you know there is a difference, because you have got your hands on eleven hundred of these and I have got my palm on one."

"Your palm on one is not the same as your arm in it." Sera Quill's voice did not rise. "I have had this conversation four times. Twice with young men who are alive, and once with a man who was better than you and who is dead, and I am not going to have it a fifth time with a man I trained the father of. You have touched a living branch twice. Eight seconds in total, four each, and the second one cost you an hour you cannot account for and a handprint on a stone you do not remember touching, and you have no mark on you and you will not be able to repeat either of them. That is the position. That is a man who has used an instrument twice and cannot go on using it and does not have anything to show for it except a pain in the place where a hand used to be."

Marek found that his right hand was closed. He opened it.

"Suppose I could," he said. "Suppose there were a reason."

"There is always a reason. That is not what I am guarding you from." She took the case off the rail and buckled it. "If you put that arm in a wounded conduit a third time, with no bead, no licensed reader and no return, then one of two things happens and I cannot tell you which and neither can you. Either you come up with something, and you will be certain it is true, and it will be a thing you read with your own blood, and no clerk in Fennmere and no hearing officer in this district can use it, and you will spend the rest of the season trying to make other people believe a man who was alone in a pit. Or you come up with a piece of yourself that is not yours, and you will not know which piece, and you will write it down anyway, because that is what you do, because you wrote down the four seconds on the eighth day in a pit with nobody in it and called it the worst condition there is and then did it again on the tenth with a flock on the embankment." She stopped. "You cannot be a witness at your own well if I let you do it. That is the whole of it. I am not protecting the wood. I am protecting the only witness you have got."

Marek did not have an answer, and the thing he felt about that was not anger, which he had been ready for, and was not shame, which he had earned twice already this week. It was the specific vertigo of a man being told, accurately, by somebody better than him, that the best thing about him is a thing that has to be taken away.

"Right," he said. "Then tell me the job."

Sera Quill took the drafting board out of the case and put it on the rail of the well house and squared it to the edge of the collar, and uncapped her ink with her teeth.

"Here is the job, and I am going to price it out loud, because you will otherwise have it later in writing and be able to pretend you did not know. The charter wants a node *maintained*. Maintained means three things and the third one is the one that is going to cost me. Dated records. Licensed hands. A return path that works. You have no licensed hands. You have not had licensed hands since your charter was granted, and you have been signing a column against another man's figures for six years, and the reason your figures are wrong is that nobody in this building ever laid a hand on the return that the numbers depend on."

"I'll lay a hand on it."

"You will not, because the return is 22 rods of eight inch and you have known that since the third day and you have not been down it, and if you go down it with a shovel you will find a stone at the eleventh rod that I already know about because a woman from Cray put her boot in it in the first week of the season." She dipped the pen. "So. Licensed hands. I am the only licensed hand within a day's ride, and I am a freelance inspector and I am not on the district roll and there is no office behind me. If I put my name on a certificate that this node is maintained, and if this node fails in February, then the first question anybody asks is not what happened to the well. It is who signed it. And the answer is a name, and the name is mine, and yours is under it, and mine is on top of it, because mine is the one that carries the license and the one that a district officer can put in front of the Rootwright Assembly."

"And if the work is sound?"

"Then it costs you fourteen pounds and you have a document, and the document will still be on a district file in ten years with a cooperative's name at the top of it." She wrote without looking up. "If the work is not sound, it costs you fourteen pounds and the district has a licensed engineer who looked at it and will say so, and you cannot un-see that either. I said that to you on a road six days ago and I have not improved on it."

"Write it down anyway."

"I am writing it down anyway, and I have written down the part I have not said, which is that the charge is not the Assembly's. There is no Assembly charge. Nobody from the Assembly will ever look at your well. The charge is that a licensed engineer's name on a node is a promise that the node is somebody's to answer for, and the somebody is me, personally, in a way that has nothing to do with anybody in Auberron, and I am fifty-six and I do not have an employer and I do not have a district that would stand in front of me at a hearing. If it fails I will be a woman who certified a cooperative's well. That is what I have left. I have got a license, four hundred students, and that."

She wrote three lines and turned the board round and put it face down on the rail, which is what people do with a document they have not finished arguing with. The wind moved the lamp.

"Now the other half, and then I will stop." She turned the board round. "I cannot certify what you are about to do. I want that in a line at the top of the page. What you are about to do is not a repair in the sense the charter means and it is not a graft and it will not be a graft, and nobody is going to be able to call it one. It is a hand-built holding seal, laid over a dressed wound, by five people who have never laid one, in three days, with no compound, on a node that has a Crown maintenance schedule against it. I can supervise it. I cannot sign it. What I can sign, and what I will sign, is this: that a repair attempt was made, on this date, by these five hands, before this licensed engineer, and logged, and returned, and that the condition of the node after it was *this*."

"And what is the condition after it?"

"That is what the three days are for," Sera Quill said. "I will not tell you now, because if I tell you now you will build for the answer instead of building for the wound."

Marek looked at the housing, and at the open collar, and at the black water four feet down with a foot of it in the bottom and the wound under it.

"What do I sign," he said.

Sera Quill put the pen down on the rail, and squared the second sheet on top of the first, and wrote at the top of it in her own hand: *Charter condition, fourth part, second clause — node maintained after a documented repair attempt. Certificate of the licensed engineer of record, and of the cooperative's engineer of record.*

Then she signed it.

Then she turned it round, put it on the rail against the housing, and put the pen in the space under her own name, and held it out to him handle first.

"Under me," she said. "Read it before you sign it and if you do not understand a word of it, do not sign it and I will explain the word. That is not a formality. A man who signs a thing he has not read has turned a document into a rumour, and I have been on the wrong end of that once and I do not get a vote in it."

He read it twice. He had read it twice, and he understood it, and he signed his own name under hers, and the order of the two names on that page was the single most expensive thing he did all day.

"There," Sera Quill said, taking the board back. "Now I have told you the price and you have paid it in ink, and the ink is cheaper than the fourteen pounds. The fourteen pounds I have not asked for yet and I am going to ask for it on the fifteenth, and I am asking for it in advance and not afterwards, and I would like you to spend four days failing to find it and to come back to me on the twentieth with the money and not with a reason."

"How much."

"The schedule fixes it. Fourteen pounds for a second visit at the head with a finding. The schedule assumes the Crown is paying and the schedule has not been amended since a war." She capped the ink. "And Mr Vale. When you raise it, I want you to raise it honestly. I do not want a hat round the yard and a speech about a man's pride. I want sixteen households told in plain words what fourteen pounds buys and what happens if it is not there, and I want the people who say no to be allowed to say no to my face, because I have spent this whole week watching what happens to records in this building when people are not allowed to say no to a face."

Then she went to the pump shed, because there was a cot in there, and Marek understood that she had taken a room over a pump shed in a fen in the last week of the season and was going to sleep in it for a fortnight, and that nobody had asked her to and that his mother had said nothing about it, which was worse.

Ilsa Kern watched her go and then said, to Marek, in a low voice, so that it would not carry:

"You are going to have four other people in that pit on Monday, and not one of them has ever seen the inside of it. Say so tonight at the table and not tomorrow morning, because a man told at the table is a man who came to a decision, and a man told at the pit is a man who was informed."

"How many will say yes?"

Ilsa Kern looked at the open collar, and at the four feet of black water below it, and at the lamp.

"Three of them will say yes because they are frightened and they would rather be busy," she said. "One will say yes because he wants to see what you do. And one of them will say yes so that she can be the one who stops you if you are wrong, and you should be careful, because she will do it, and she will be right."

She turned and went to the embankment, because it was Thursday and the water in the yard ditch was still a foot high and embankments do not wait for anybody's paperwork.

Marek stood at the well head alone with a signed board and six days, and did the only arithmetic available to him, which was that a wound two hands long with a third of it closed in four seasons under four coats of somebody's tar was going to be a hand and a half by Monday, if four of them were still standing on Monday at dawn.

Then he went up to the seed house to tell five people the truth at a table, which was the only way Ilsa Kern had said he would be allowed to do it.
