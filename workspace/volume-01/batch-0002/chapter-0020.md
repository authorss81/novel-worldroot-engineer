# Chapter 20

## The Last Line Of The List

The carter came at seven in the morning and waited all day, which cost him a guinea and which he charged for, and he was a red man from the Fennmere road who had quoted for a cart in the spring and had held the booking since the summer, and had said *she'll be ready on the twentieth* at the time, and who looked at the yard for about ten seconds when he came through the gate and then got on with his work and did not comment on any of it.

He wanted the cart loaded by three. The fen road is not a road after three in the afternoon. The causeway below Weir Row goes under on a wet week and he had been stuck on the far side of it once, in the spring, for nine hours, with a load of lime, and he said so to Nia Vale once, unprompted, in a voice that was not complaining, and then never said it again.

Nia Vale had the book and the columns on the loading bench all day.

*Item. Crate. Quantity. Condition. Where it is going. Who is standing there when it goes. Witness.*

She read every line out loud before it was entered, and then she read the whole page out loud again from the top, to the yard, standing on the bench so that people at the seed house door could hear her, and more than thirty people heard it, which was not what she had planned and which she did not shorten for.

Twenty-two lines.

The Longneck, eight trays, living, two and a half crates, condition *alive and cold and going to Cray to be compared against nothing*, destination Cray, standing there Nia Vale, witness Lissa Vale. The trust's own material in the tin, which was not going, which was *retained at Lantern Reach by the seed steward's decision, at risk, and the trust knows where it is*, standing there Lissa Vale, witness Tova Reed, and the word *at risk* in a line of her own writing under two lines of somebody else's, and Tova Reed initialled it, which was the second time in a week that woman had initialled something for a woman she disagreed with.

Six lines of the cooperative's own winter appropriation and a bolt of cloth, which were going, and a hundredweight of the thinnest bought compost the water-meadow farm would sell, which was a hundredweight at four shillings and which was a fortnight, and which was *not* going, and which Nia entered in the columns anyway, in the same voice as the rest, because a hundredweight of compost is not a thing a seed consignment carries down to a counting house; it is a thing that goes into a return main on the first of every month, and it was the only line on the page the other twenty-one were in service of, and a line like that does not come off a list for tidiness.

Three lines of seed off the north annex, which was Tova Reed's own and which she put in herself and which Nia entered in the same columns and in the same voice.

Two lines of the cooperative's paper records going to Cray, because the Dryfoot Trust's fourth condition had been a copy of everything sent to Cray whether or not it helped them, and it was a condition and not a favor, and the March's copy went out on the third day with the trays spoiled and it went out again on the twentieth in two bundles, and the two bundles were 6 and 11 pounds and the carter was paid for the second one out of the trust's money and not out of the cooperative's, which Nia Vale wrote on the line in full, with the amount, and which Tova Reed watched her do.

One letter. Sealed, addressed to K. Rusk, Fennmere pump house, to be delivered by hand to a person and not left in a box, and the reason that it was on a seed-keeper's list at all was that Nia Vale was going to Fennmere and Cray and Fennmere was on the way and nobody in the Lantern Reach Work Cooperative had any business riding to a gate in Fennmere, and the reason it was on the list was that it was a thing leaving the building, and Nia Vale's list was of things leaving the building.

And the last line, which she read out on the bench at four o'clock in the afternoon with the cart half loaded and the light going and the whole yard standing in it.

*Item: one person. Nia Vale. Bag, one. Box, one. No crate. Destination Cray, by the Salt March road, and Fennmere on the way, seven days. Condition: going. Who is standing there when it goes: Nia Vale. Witness: Lissa Vale.*

There was a silence in the yard that had a different shape from every other silence that had happened in it that month.

Lissa Vale came out of the seed house with her sleeves rolled and put her hand on the back of her daughter's neck, once, for about a second and a half, and did not say anything at all, and then went to the book and read the line, and signed the witness column, and capped the ink.

Nia Vale waited until her mother was inside before she said anything, and what she said was:

"If you are going to do that, do it in the book and not in a yard, because I have spent nine days making a document that can be read by a stranger in five years, and I am not going to have it be a thing somebody did in a yard to make themselves feel better about. I will take the signature. I will not take anything else."

"That was in the list," Lissa said, from the doorway.

"Yes it was," said Nia Vale. "I put it there on Thursday because I could not think of a column for it and because you would have refused one."

---

The trunk went on at half past three. The two bundles of paper went in under the seat. The tin did not go anywhere. The eight trays went in last, in two crates, packed in wet sand with the seed-house lamp lit on them for the first hour of the road so that they would not shock, which is a thing a seed steward knows and a cart driver does not, and which she explained to him and which he wrote on the back of his hand.

At four o'clock they shut the gate, which took a man on each side, and Nia Vale got up on the bench beside the driver and did not look at anybody.

"Vale," she said, to the yard in general, to every head in it. "I have been in this cooperative's book for nine days and I am going to be asked in Cray what this place is, and I am going to answer it the way it is, and I want you all to hear me say the answer now so that nobody in this yard can be surprised by it later."

"It is not a success. Nothing worked. The pump works and always worked. The well is worse than it was on the first and better than it was going to be. The charter is gone and there is a wound in a pit with a piece of sacking on it and a woman's name against the first of every month, and that is the whole of what we have. The pad was a coat of tar and a man told the truth about it in a yard and it took nine days to get him to, and the cut up the fen is a schedule and nobody has ever seen the schedule and it comes back on the twenty-seventh, and the flock is alive somewhere in the fen and will be dead by the spring, and the compost is a hundredweight a fortnight instead of four hundred a month, and three trays of the only dry-land rye anybody has left are dead because I said no to a man in a shed and he did it anyway."

"That is what this place is. That is what I am taking to Cray. Not a good season. A *documented* bad season, which is a different thing, and which is the only thing anybody in the March is actually going to be able to use, and there is one man in this yard who started that and will not take any credit for it, and I have watched him try twice this week and stop both times, so I am not going to say his name and he can go without it."

Somebody at the back started clapping and she told them, without heat, to stop, and they stopped.

Then she said: "One more thing and it is the only one I am actually leaving behind, and it is for my brother and he does not need to hear me say it."

"Put my name in the monthly report. The report of root changes, the one that goes to the sub-office. I am the one who will be asked for it in Cray, because I will be the one standing in somebody else's counting house with a copy of it, and I am not coming back to be asked twice about a thing I have already written out once. So the first monthly report, which is due on the first and is about the first of the month, is written out by me tonight on a seed order, and it goes in Marek's name and it carries Nia Vale under the figures, and if anybody ever wants to know who made a number in this cooperative's book they will be able to find out, which is the only inheritance I have any intention of leaving anybody."

"Written out by you tonight," Marek said.

"I have got an hour and the seed house press is in the next room and it is my handwriting you want on it, not a clerk's."

She wrote it on the back of a seed order with a broken chisel, on the seed-house table, in the dark, with the lamp hooked on a nail, and it was two hundred and forty words and it took her fifty minutes, and it said that the cooperative's node carried a recurring pressure of about one beat in four seconds arriving from below the sump, unaccounted for; that the housing carried a wound dressed on the sixteenth with a willow-and-withy holding seal and a sacking cover, load-tested on the twentieth at eleven hundred turns; that a dark place one quarter inch in width appeared at the six o'clock corner under full draw and is not a leak, and did not increase between half past twelve and ten o'clock with the load off; that a Crown maintenance item, N-2214/K, is scheduled on the branch at Weir Row and will be executed on the twenty-seventh, and that the cooperative has not been notified and cannot object; that the charter lapsed on the eighteenth; and that the return on the node is short and the shortfall is not established.

And it was honest, and none of it was good news, and she signed the last line and initialed the figures, and put it on the table where anybody coming in at six in the morning would find it.

Marek carried it in and entered it in the cooperative book with the date and the hour and *written out by N. Vale, seed house* and *submitted by M. Vale, engineer*, and his mother entered the page number, and then she shut the book and did not say goodnight, and went to the annex to sit with the eight trays' empty space in the cold for a while, which is a thing seed people do.

---

The cart went at ten past five, while there was still light on the crown of the embankment a mile up the fen road, and the whole yard came out because there is nothing else in this world to do on the last Friday of a bad season except watch a cart leave, and everybody in the yard knew it, and nobody said so.

Renn Tallow was not there and had not been expected. Kellan Rusk was in Fennmere and had sent nothing, and had said at the gate on the eighteenth that he would say nothing to anybody, and had said nothing, and had kept his word, which was not a thing anybody had asked him to keep.

The gate was shut by half past five, by two men, and the gate is a good heavy gate and it shuts with a sound like a book closing.

Marek put his hand on it and left it there.

Nia Vale's cart ruts were already in the soft ground of the lane by then, two sets, four inches deep, going out through the gate and up the lane and away, and they would be there for a fortnight and they would be the only thing in Tarrow Fen that looked like anything had gone anywhere.

He counted the crates. He did not know why he counted the crates, and there was nothing in the yard he had meant to count, and he counted them anyway: four, the ones he had stacked against the seed house wall that afternoon, when the cart filled and two of the appropriation lines came back off the list in Nia's hand and the compost had never gone on it at all. The appropriation in three crates, for sixteen houses, and the hundredweight of thinnest compost in the fourth, which is going to stand against that wall until somebody opens the return main with it, and one of them had the lamp hooked over it still, because he had not unhooked the lamp, and he stood in the dark looking at a seed-house lamp burning on the side of a crate in a yard at half past five because he had not had a minute in nine days in which nobody needed him to do something.

Anneke Roos came past with a lamp of her own and stopped.

"Four," she said.

"Four."

"You are going to be here in February." It was not a question and it was not a kindness. "The first of every month, somebody is in that pit, and it is not going to be me, because I am on the low acre and I am fifty-one in the spring and the low acre goes under in the first week. It is the lamp-keeper. You made that happen on Monday and you did not do it on purpose and you have not once asked me whether I was right to say it, and I am telling you that I was."

"I have been not asking you on purpose," Marek said, "because I did not want to have been told that I was wrong about a thing I did not do on purpose."

"That is the first true thing you have said to me this month and I am going to think about it for a week." She walked on toward the gate with her lamp. "And Marek. The report is good and I have read it and I am not going to tell you that it is good, because a document is not good or bad, it is either a document or it is a thing somebody remembered. That one is a document."

"Seven days," Marek said. "It is Friday. It comes back on Friday."

He stood in the gate in the dark with the four crates and a lit lamp and the pump turning over on an unchartered node, and he did not follow the ruts, and the fen went on being flat and enormous and completely silent in the way a fen is silent when a front is coming up out of the west, and the sacking on the seal in the pit was dry, and there were nine hundred and forty pounds of dead pad in four baskets in the pump shed that he had taken off his own wound and had not yet been able to bring himself to burn.
