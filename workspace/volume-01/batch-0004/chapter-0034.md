# Chapter 34

## Eleven Miles And Fourteen Days

He went the long way round, and he told four people why at the gate before he left, because he was not going to be a man who walked through the bottom of the low quarter on a Friday with nothing in his hands.

"The turn off at the second gate is four miles in and the low quarter is on the other side of it," he said, "and there are forty-one houses on that ground and I have not got anything for them, and if I walk down that road on a Friday a man I do not know will come out of a door and ask me when the water comes back, and I have no date and no answer, and I would rather walk eleven miles than be a man who says *I do not know* for the ninth time in a fortnight."

Anneke Roos wrote that down with the hour, because she writes everything down with the hour, and then she said, "And put in that you are walking and not riding, because the fen road is not a road after three and you are going to be walking in the dark at both ends of it, and if you fall in the drain at the eleventh mile a document goes in the water and so do you."

---

It is eleven miles and he did it in a day, in flat winter light that made the whole of Tarrow Fen look like one plate with something written on it, and the notice was in his coat in an oilskin and had been read aloud at the seed house table at six in the morning with four people in the room before he got to the gate.

Lissa Vale had written it. That was not a thing anybody had suggested and she did it anyway, in the seven-column form, because there was nothing else to write it on and because the form was hers, and Marek had to stand at a table and dictate a document to the woman who had been keeping his father's notebook in a press for eight years and who had said *no* to him three times in six weeks and *yes* once and had named the difference out loud.

*Notice of intention to require an independent licensed reading of the node at Lantern Reach, under the rule at the thirtieth day of last month, requiring a second reader, on the same physical node, through her or his own anchor, on his own date, without knowledge of the first reading. Given by M. Vale, engineer of record, on the fourth day of this month, at the seed house table, in the presence of L. Vale, D. Fitch, A. Roos and G. Senn, and delivered in person to Idris Vane at his own house on the same day, at the eleventh hour. The reading is to be given by the named person, unedited, in his own hand, with his own name. Notice runs fourteen days from the hour of delivery. Witnessed in duplicate.*

*Out. Back.*

She made him say *Out. Back* at the end, the way she said it about the fieldbook, and he said it, and did not ask her what the *Back* column was going to mean on a document with no instrument in it.

---

Idris Vane's house is a mile off the fen road up a cart track, and it is two rooms and a leanto and a woodpile that has been split by somebody who is very good at splitting wood, and the woman who lived in it for thirty-four years had left a row of pegs in the back wall with two things still hanging on them, and he had not taken them down.

He was sixty-one. He was a small man, not a stooped one, and he had the hands of somebody who has spent his life in cold water and had a kettle going and two cups out before Marek was through the door, which meant he had known what time somebody on the fen road would come, which meant somebody had told him, and Marek did not ask who, because it did not matter and because asking would have been the first thing he did with a stranger's hospitality.

"Is that yours," said Marek, about eleven minutes in, meaning the book on the table.

It was a fieldbook. gray boards, a strap gone stiff, a living root bead in a copper collar, and about forty pages in a hand that was not a clerk's hand and had never been.

"Yes," said Idris Vane.

He did not offer it and Marek did not ask about it, and that was the entire conversation they had about it, and it was the largest thing that happened in that house all day.

"Sit down," said Vane. "I have thought about you since Monday night. I will tell you what I have thought, and then I will ask you three questions, and I have decided in advance that I am not going to answer any of them before I have asked all three, because a man who takes an answer to his first question and then asks the rest of them out of politeness is a man who has already decided."

---

"I am not going to say no," he said, "and I want you to sit with that for a minute before I go on, because in thirty years I have had two bodies come to me with a notice and one of them wanted me to say a thing and one of them wanted me to say the opposite, and the second one got me and the first one did not."

"Here is what I have worked out about you, and I have not met you, so it is all of it I have. You have a node with no charter. You have a wound with a piece of sacking on it. You have four people who dug a ditch and one licensed woman who signed for it, and she wrote down that the return is short, and you have printed that on a page and sent it to a district office, and eleven days later you have sent a page to my office that is the same number in the same hand."

"That means there is nothing in this for me to say that would help you. If I read that node and it says *below*, the district has two licensed readers saying *below* and you have a hearing in a fortnight and a bill, and I have made your case worse by one. If it says something else, then either Ms Quill was wrong about her own work on a day with four witnesses, or I am, and one of those two is a scandal and it is not mine."

"So I am not being paid to help you. I am being paid to be a second pair of eyes on a page, and the only thing I get out of it is the work and the day rate, and I have not moved the day rate in four years and I am going to move it now, in front of you, because I have decided to."

"Fourteen pounds ten. For a reading, a finding, and an attested copy that a hearing officer may hold. It is one day. I do not do it in one day."

"Fourteen pounds ten," said Marek.

"Yes."

"There is a bill of sixty pounds ten against the same cooperative and four pounds five in the box," said Marek, "and I have walked eleven miles to tell you a number you have already told me and I would like you to understand that I knew the number on Monday, when I did not walk, and I have known it every day since, and I am here because of a notice period and not because of money, and I have now said the money out loud in your kitchen and it has not changed anything, which is a thing I have needed to find out in a room with one man in it rather than in a yard with nine."

"You need to sit down properly now," said Idris Vane, "because you have been standing up for two hours saying things to a kettle."

---

The three questions came after that, and he asked them in the order he had said he would.

"Who pays for the compost."

"Four hundred hundredweight a month at four shillings is eighty pounds a month," said Marek. "One hundredweight went into that main on the twenty-first of last month and cost four shillings, and there is nothing in the bin since the middle of October because a hundred and thirty head of sheep went up the fen road on the tenth and have not come back. The licensed sheet says the return is three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight a month short, in the engineer's own hand. I do not have eighty pounds a month and I have four pounds five."

"That is the first question and you have answered it honestly, which is the only reason I am asking the second. The second is: what is the notice for. Is it for the hearing in a fortnight, or is it for the charter, which is the thing after the charter."

Marek did not have an answer ready and that is how Vane knew it was a real question.

"The charter," he said. "I have not admitted that to anybody and I am admitting it to you because you have asked me directly and I can tell you the reason, which is that in eleven working days a man is going to ask me four questions and I cannot answer two of them, and in a month there is a hearing, and after that somebody decides whether this body ever holds this node again. The first of those is gone. The third one is not."

"That is the right answer and it is the wrong week," said Vane. "And now the third, and the third is the only one that matters."

"What did Ms Quill read."

And Marek told him, because there was no version of this where he did not tell a man who had asked to be told, and he took the paper out of his coat to do it, a sheet of seed-house paper folded twice with three lines on it in his own hand, written at his mother's table at six in the morning before he left the gate.

Vane wrote the three lines out on the back of a seed order in a hand of his own, and read them back, and Marek did not stop him and did not want him to.

"Then you will not read this again before I have read the node," said Vane, "and you will not tell me what I ought to find, and you will not tell me what finding would help. And I want the paper in a sealed envelope with your mother's seal on it and her name on the outside, and I will open it after I have given my figures and not before, and if it is broken before then, I will not read the node at all, and I will say so in writing and you may put that in front of whoever you like."

"And the fourth thing, which I have not written as a question because I am not asking it, I am telling you. My figures go out in my own hand and under my own name, unedited, and they go to the district and to you. Both, or neither. If you take my reading and keep it in a yard until somebody tells you what to do with it, then I have given you a second opinion with the temperature still on it, which is what I am for eleven miles of fen road trying to prevent, and you may as well have saved the walk."

"Both," said Marek.

"Both," said Idris Vane, "and you have said that quickly and I would like you to know what it costs, because you have just agreed that the district will be holding a second licensed reading of this node before the form is even in. If mine says *below* then the district will have two licensed readers saying *below* and a cooperative that has put a return shortfall in its own hand, and that is a file that does not need a hearing officer, it needs a clerk with a rubber stamp. Have you thought about that."

"I have thought about it for two days," said Marek, "and it is the reason I have not signed anything and the reason I am here, and if I could have had a reading that stayed in this fen I would have had that instead, and it would not have been worth the walk."

---

They filled in the rest of it at the kitchen table with the tea going cold, and the three conditions went onto the duplicate notice in Idris Vane's own hand, and then he did the thing Marek had walked eleven miles for and had not dared ask for, which was to read the form.

It took him about eleven minutes. He read both sheets and the box in the corner and he turned it over and read the back, which is blank, and then he put his thumb on the *amount disputed* box and said the only useful thing anybody said to Marek in the whole of that day.

"You have written *amount disputed*," he said, "and in the line under it you have written *cause not established*. Those are two different disputes and you have put them in one mouth, and in a room with a cold fire in it, a hearing officer will read *amount disputed* and hear a body that says sixty pounds ten is the wrong number, and you do not think that. You think sixty pounds ten is right and you cannot say why it happened."

"Cross out the box. Do not write anything in it. Write in the line under it that the amount is not disputed and that the cause is not established, and put a rule between them and write the words *the cooperative does not dispute the amount* at the top of the page in your own hand and not in a clerk's, because a clerk's hand is the district's and yours is yours."

"And understand what you are doing. You are giving up the amount to keep the cause. If you lose the cause in that room, you owe sixty pounds ten, and there are four pounds five in the box, and there is no power of appeal, and you will have made that trade in your own handwriting on a piece of paper in a stranger's kitchen and there is nobody in your yard who can undo it."

Marek crossed out the box.

He walked back the eleven miles in the dark with a folded sheet of seed-house paper in his coat that he had written at six in the morning and was not going to be allowed to see again, and a duplicate notice with three conditions on it in a stranger's hand, and a form with a box ruled out on it and nine words at the top of a page in his own handwriting.

He came in at the gate at twenty past seven and put all three on the well house bench in the dark, and Anneke Roos read the duplicate aloud because the lamp was hers, and Lissa Vale folded the sheet into an envelope with wax off the seed house stove without reading it, and put her name on the outside of it, and put it in the press under the ledger on top of eleven pages and a tin.

"You will not get that back before the middle of next month," she said. "And you have just given away the middle of next month, and I want that written down somewhere by somebody other than me."
