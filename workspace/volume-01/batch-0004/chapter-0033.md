# Chapter 33

## We Cut It First

Perrin Osse came out on her own horse at nine in the morning on the third of the month with a satchel and a form in it, and Sera Quill's case was in the yard already, because Sera had come up the road on the four o'clock cart with nothing in it but a canvas bag and the two of them had not said good morning and had not needed to. She had come up to close the certificate out, which is to say to take her own papers away with her and put the fee on an invoice and go down the road, and she had said on the cart and not in front of anybody that she was not going near that pit, because the load test was the twentieth day's act and there is no charter to hang a second one on and she is not spending a second act on a head in that condition for a district that is going to send somebody else to look at it.

"Three things off the paper and then I am going to help you look at it, because I have read it four times and I do not think it says what it looks like it says."

She stood on the loading bench and read it, and it was a single printed sheet with the district's form number in the corner and a line for a date and a line for a place and the date filled in by hand.

*Well Directorate, Tarrow district. Emergency-maintenance schedule, hazard measure. Controlled burn, south embankment windbreak: nine hundred yards, between the two stakes set at the ends of the willow and elder, firebreaks cut to the mill race, a crew of nine, a licensed man in attendance, carried at the district's cost. To be executed between the sixth and the twelfth day of this month, on a day of the district's choosing. The measure is to be entered on the district's record as an executed item on the node. In consideration of the district's cost, and of the entry, the consent of the body last responsible, in the form supplied, is required.*

*Form EM/2.*

"And the second page is the consent, and the second page is a page and a half, and the second page says one thing and it says it six times in different words," said Perrin Osse. "It says that the body signing it is satisfied that the root line beneath the windbreak is not a line the body relies on for the supply of the node, and that its severance is not a damage to the node, and that the body will not thereafter be entitled to say that the district's entry of the measure as maintenance was made without its consent or against its interest. Six times, because a form is written by lawyers and a lawyer cannot say it once and believe it."

"What do we get," said Marek.

"You get nine hundred yards of windbreak burnt and two years of no wind on the west side of this holding, and the district pays for it, and the district puts an executed item on the node's record with your name on the consent." Perrin Osse folded the form. "That is what you get. And I will tell you the true thing about it, which I have not said on paper and will not say twice, and that is that the district is not offering you a bargain. The district is offering you the only thing a district has, which is a signature. Somebody has to be answerable for the standing crop on a fire line, and the law says it is the body last responsible, and the body last responsible is you, and if you will not sign then the measure is not executed and the windbreak stands and everybody is content."

"That is a threat dressed as a form," said Tova Reed.

"That is a form with a hole in it that has been in every hazard measure since the bylaw was written," said Perrin Osse. "I have no argument with you and I have been in this office twenty-two years. Now I would like to go and look at the thing, because I have never in twenty-two years seen one of these executed on a holding I have taken the walk-through of, and I would like to see the ground before somebody burns it."

---

They walked out to the south embankment at half past ten, all of them, which is nine people on an embankment with a clerk in a good coat on it.

The windbreak is not beautiful and it is not old. It is about four feet of willow and grey elder laid in a single line along the top of the south embankment for nine hundred yards, planted by somebody's grandfather in a bad year, and it has been cut and cut and cut for sixty years for withy, for bean rods, for hurdles and for fence, and what is left of it is a line of stems with a crown on top and gaps in it where a gap has always been.

Ilsa Kern was already at the west end with a hook in her hand, because she is the embankment foreman and the embankment is hers, and she put her hand flat on the crown of a stem about hip high and said what she said to a fence post a month ago.

"This is the windbreak," she said. "It is a good one for the county and it is the only one between the fen and this ground. The fen comes from the west. You have all stood in the lee of it in a gale and you know what it is worth."

"It is worth a great deal to a seed bed," said Tova Reed, and nobody argued with her about that.

They walked the whole nine hundred yards, Perrin Osse with a hand on the crown, counting. The two stakes were already in the ground at each end, and had been since before the district wrote to anybody, and nobody in the yard had ever noticed them because they are the stakes that hold the fence and they are four inches square and sixty years old.

At about two hundred and sixty yards Ilsa Kern stopped and stood in a gap and looked at the ground on the north side of the stems, which is the sheltered side, which is the leeward side when the wind is in the west, and where there is a mat of dead bracken and two feet of standing dead sedge and, coming up through it, about forty feet of low growth that nobody in Tarrow Fen would have called a plant.

"Milfoil," she said. "And a little of the sweet gale, and a whole mat of fen sorrel, and I have cut that ground twice a year since I was a girl and I have never once thought about what I was cutting, because it is a bank and you cut a bank."

"That is the fen's own seed coming down off the high ground," said Tova Reed. "That is not a bank. That is a seed ground, and it is on the leeward side of the only windbreak for nine hundred yards, and it has been collecting everything the fen drops for sixty years, and a burn takes it in an afternoon and it does not come back in four."

"Four years," said Tova Reed. "I am not being dramatic. I am being exact, and the exactness is that in four years there is nothing in this fen to compare a dry-land line against, and the comparison is the whole of what the March bought, and the people who eat the result of the comparison are nine thousand people on the March road who are not in this room and whose name I have never been able to say in a yard without sounding like a woman making a speech."

"You are not making a speech," said Anneke Roos. "You are making a number, and the number is four, and that is a thing I would write down."

---

They argued about it from the third hour and it went on until the light went, and everything that mattered that day happened in the argument, so it is worth setting down in the order it happened.

Ilsa Kern was for burning it, and she said so before anybody asked her, and she gave no speech at all.

"I will say the thing nobody wants," she said. "A controlled burn is a fire with men on it and a firebreak cut to the mill race and a licensed man watching, and it is done in the right month, and it is the cheapest way there is of taking a fen back. And the other way of taking a fen back is a gale in February with a spark off a hearth nine miles west, and I have been on this embankment for forty years and I have seen it done the other way once, and I do not want to be near it again in my own time.

"And I will say the second thing, and I will say it because it is mine. That windbreak is down to the old wood for a hundred feet and a bit along the third stake from the west, because I cut a hundred and six feet of green withy off it on the Sunday of last month for four sets of willow, because there was nothing else to make the lashings out of and the windbreak was the supply. It is in the working notes in Marek's hand and Anneke Roos initialled it, because I would not write it myself about a thing I had done for a thing that might work.

"So the fen is already in this windbreak for a hundred and six feet, and every year I have worked that will be more of it, and in nine years there will not be a windbreak on this embankment at all. It is going to happen with or without a fire."

Jonas Byre was for it too, and gave one sentence, and nobody in the yard had to be told by then what a sentence from Jonas Byre weighed.

"The race read nothing on Friday," he said, "and a fen that burns in the second week is a fen I can mill in the third, and I have not been able to put a load through this mill since the twenty-fourth, and I am fifty next month."

Anneke Roos was against it, and she was against it on the ground she had used on the twenty-seventh, and she used it in the same words, deliberately.

"It is the second Crown-shaped document in a month," she said. "An extract of Ms Quill's licence sheet is in a district file, and it is a Crown-shaped page with a hole in it and her name at the top. If we sign this page then in a month a hearing officer has two documents from this cooperative, both put in front of a district in a month, both shaped like something the district would have written, and one of them is a licensed engineer admitting she licensed an unfed graft and the other is a body with no charter admitting that a live line under its own windbreak is not a line it relies on.

"And the reason there is no money in it is the reason there never is. The trade is always the same. Somebody is being paid in something and we are being asked to be paid in the only currency we have left, which is our own handwriting."

Tova Reed was against it and had already given her number, four years, and said the rest of it standing at the gap in the hedge with the milfoil at her boots.

Gael Senn was the one who found the actual problem, and she found it at about five o'clock when the light had gone orange and everybody else was tired of arguing.

"I am the person who writes *nothing found* on the first of every month," she said. "And I want to say what that means for this and I do not think anybody has thought of it.

"That examination is a return. It is the return on a piece of work that four unlicensed people did under a licence, and it is the only return this cooperative makes that anybody can check, and it is checked by a woman of twenty-three with a rule. And if we burn a windbreak on the say-so of a licensed man who is not going to be here in February, then the first of next month I go down that ladder and there will be a black ground outside and a hot smell in the pit and the pack under the sacking will have gone dry and hard the way things do when the air round them changes, and I will be the one who has to write down what I find, and I will not be able to write *nothing found*, and whatever I write will be about a thing this body agreed to and had no standing to stop.

"So I am not saying yes and I am not saying no," she said. "I am saying that I would like to know what is under it, and I have just worked out that there is no way in the world for anybody in this yard to know, because the district's form is about a fire and not about a root, and the branch under Weir Row has no local holder, and there is nobody else in this district whose trade is a line of wild root, and they are not going to come here and tell us, and if they do come here and tell us, then we have to be careful what we write down about it anyway."

---

Marek made the call at six with the form in his coat and the light off the water.

"I am not signing it," he said, "and I want the reason entered in the same minute so that nobody in this yard has to guess.

"Ms Quill licensed one work on this node on the twenty-first and twenty-second of last month with the return short, and she said on the twenty-seventh that she would not license a second one, and she has been asked to speak about this and she has said the same sentence again and somebody said it was the same sentence and she said yes, because it is the same sentence and she is the same woman and I would like that written down as arithmetic and not as caution.

"So there is no licensed act here, which means there is nothing for me to sign a consent about, because a consent is a licensed person's act wearing a body's clothes, and the only licensed person in this district is standing eight feet from me and has already answered the question for the month.

"The measure stands until the sixth day of this month and the district will choose a day. I am not entering a refusal, because a refusal is a document too, and a document is the only thing I have got and I have got four of them now and one of them is a schedule of damage and one of them is a circular. So the offer goes back unanswered on the sixth, and that is a decision, and it is the worst kind of one, because it costs the same as saying no and buys nothing.

"What I am going to do instead is go and find out what is under the toe of that windbreak, and the only place the answer is likely to be is nine days out in the old forest, in somebody's kitchen, and they are not going to want to tell us and I would not blame them."

Perrin Osse put the form back in her satchel and squared it, and said, at the gate, that the district would write to the cooperative in three days with the day chosen, and that the chosen day would be a day and not a week, because a fire needs a day.

Then they walked the last hundred yards back to the gate in the dark, past the third stake from the west, and there is nothing about the hundred and six feet of old wood that a stranger would notice, and Anneke Roos stopped there anyway with her lamp up and read the working-notes entry out loud in the lane, all nineteen words of it, with the hour on it, the way she reads everything.

"That is the first hundred feet of the damage," she said, "and it is ours, and it is in writing, and the district holds the book it is written in, and the first hundred and six feet of this hedge is going to go into a fire that the district has offered us for nothing, and I would like it written in the log tonight that the cooperative had a hundred and six feet of this windbreak cut for its own graft on the Sunday of last month, and that it did it before anybody offered, and that nobody thought of what a fire would find."
