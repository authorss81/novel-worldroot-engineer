# Chapter 36

## They Were Going To The Low Quarter

He came up the peat road on the sixth of the month in the middle of the afternoon, on foot, with a whetstone in a leather loop on his belt and no cart and nothing to sell, and he had the mud of a peat cutting on him to the knee, and he was somewhere near forty and he was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the walk.

"Two things before I sit down," he said, at the gate. "I am not from the Undercommons, because there is no such a place and anybody who tells you there is such a place is selling you something. I am four men and I am one of them, the other three are on the peat road behind me with a barrow of tools, and we are not coming for you.

"And I have read your book. I am not going to tell you how, because you keep it in a press under a ledger and it is on your mother's table and it was carried to Fennmere in a clerk's satchel on the seventeenth of a month and read out loud at a counter on the twenty-eighth, and a thing that has been read out loud at a counter is a published thing. So I know the ditch and I know the four sets of willow and I know the fence post at the eleventh rod and I know the licensed woman put three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight of a hole in a page in her own hand. And I am telling you that I know it because in about four years, when I have forgotten this afternoon, I am going to need to be able to say that I knew, and I would rather it were said now in a yard while you are looking at me."

"Sit down," said Lissa Vale, and put a cup of tea and a heel of bread in front of him, and wrote the heel of bread in the annex book with the hour, because the rules are the rules.

---

He did not eat the bread until the end, and he did the talking first, and he read it off the top of three fingers because it was a thing he had said out loud many times to people who did not want it.

"The bypass," he said. "Your own note, the twenty-seventh of last month, thirty to forty yards, a new run off the head side into the bed below Weir Row, refused by five names because there is no contractor, no licence and no money. That is the best plan I have read in a long time, and it is the only one anybody in this district has written down that would actually put water back into that bed.

"We can dig it," he said. "Four of us, three nights, and we will not tell you the names, because you would put them in the book, and a name in that book is a name a district can find. There is no charge and no money in it either way. There is nothing here anybody is trying to sell you, so I am going to give you the four things and then stop talking, and one of the four is worse than the other three and you may want to send me away before that one."

"Nobody is going to send you away," said Ilsa Kern. "Say the four."

"First. It is a return, not a favour. It goes in a book, in ours, and yours has to go in ours, entire, once, and ours gets pasted into yours, and the two books have to say the same thing or one of them is a lie. That is the only condition about the work. The other three are about what the work does to you.

"Second. We keep it. Four men dug that ditch and four men are answerable for it, and in three years one of us will be the one standing in front of it saying why it has gone soft, and the answer will not be that nobody came.

"Third. It is for the low quarter. Not for you. Forty-one houses and the water in the ground under them, and the branch that turns it is dead and has been dead since the spring before last. We are going because that is where the water is, and if your field takes a cup out of it in three years' time I will be the one standing in front of your field, and I would rather stand in front of that than in front of a house.

"Fourth." He put his cup down. "You cannot get it back."

Nobody said anything.

"There is no one," said Ivo Tarrant. "I know how that sounds, and I have thought about the words for a fortnight and there are not any better ones. There is no office. There is no man to write to. There is nobody in the world who can be told that the Lantern Reach cooperative has had seconded help put into its own log by four men on a peat road, and who can then say *that was irregular, remove it*. There is no one at all.

"Once your book is in ours, the undertaking runs one way. We can be called on. You cannot refuse us. And the only way it ends is if all four of us stop coming, and I have told my three that we are not going to stop coming, because a line somebody keeps is kept, and that is not a threat and it is not a favour, it is a thing you agree to when you let four strangers have a line.

"And I will tell you the part that is in it for me and not for you. I have dug six of these and every one of them has gone into a ground that is somebody else's business. This is the first one I have ever offered before the work was wanted. That is because of the number on your licence sheet, and I will not say whose number, and you do not need me to, and I would rather be asked twice than once."

---

The refusal took about nine minutes and it was not a vote and Marek did not allow it to become a chorus, because a chorus in a yard is worth nothing in a book, and because he had watched four people refuse a bypass one at a time on the twenty-seventh and knew what the shape of it was and did not want it twice.

Anneke Roos went first and she went straight to the middle of it, which was not the work.

"Your fourth thing decides this and I am not going to pretend the other three are equal to it," she said. "I have spent four weeks in this yard teaching a room that a document you can check is worth more than a document you have to believe, and I have a monthly examination signed by a woman of twenty-three that turns entirely on the difference. And you have just told us we cannot get a page out of a book once it is in it, and there is nobody we could ask. That is the arrangement I have been arguing against since the fourteenth without knowing what I was arguing against.

"Our log is a body with no charter keeping a record that a district may look at, copy and carry away, and may not certify. It has one thing going for it and the one thing is that it is ours. On Monday a clerk is going to copy it out by hand in the sub-office and Lissa Vale is going to read every page back to her, and that is the only reason that copy is a true copy and not a version. If that book goes to a place nobody can name, then the copy in Fennmere is a copy of a document whose other half is a thing a man with a barrow said in a yard. That is how a record stops being a record and starts being a rumour with a witness in it. And I have read that sentence in our own book on the thirtieth of last month, in a licensed engineer's mouth, and I watched the man who wrote it sign it."

Tova Reed took the second, and she took it from a completely different direction, and she had it out before anyone else understood what she was doing.

"The Dryfoot Trust's fourth condition," she said, "is a copy of everything sent to Cray, whether or not it helps them. That is a copy. A copy is a copy of a document, and a document is a thing that exists in one place and is the same in every other place, and the March can hold a copy of it because the March can produce its own copy back and say *that is the one and it is the same one*.

"Your book cannot be checked against anything. There is no second copy in the world of what four men dug on a peat road in the dark, and if I send the March a copy of *that*, then the March's guarantee is a guarantee about a document whose origin cannot be established by anybody standing in a field in four years' time. I cannot send a trust's guarantee into a place with no name in it. I would be signing for nine thousand people on the March road a thing I could not prove if I were asked by a man I did not trust, and I do not know a man I could not trust yet, and I am not going to find out this month."

Sera Quill took the third, standing up, and she said the same sentence she had said on the twenty-seventh of last month in the same yard, and when Anneke Roos said *"that is the second time you have said that sentence"* she said yes.

"It is the same sentence because it is the same arithmetic," she said. "A graft needs a living anchor, a compatible path, and a return, and I have given this node two of them in a month and a hole where the third one is in my own hand. If I license a second work here before the first one is fed, then the shortfall is twice and both halves are under one signature, and in six weeks somebody will ask me in a room which of two things I was thinking of on the second day of last month, and I will not be able to answer.

"Three men's nights on a peat road is a return. It is a return for the work they do. It is not a return for work that I have licensed, and if your ditch goes into their book and mine goes into mine, then there are two documents about a system with a hole in it and one of them is signed by a person who is not in this fen and can be produced by anybody who has ever met him. And no. I have said it before this month, in this yard, on a different morning, to the same four people, and I will not say it differently for a better reason."

Gael Senn had said nothing for four minutes and then said the thing that was actually the situation.

"It is not the licence," she said. "It is that I am the one who goes down that ladder on the first of every month and I am twenty-three and I have asked for this work since I was eighteen, and Mr Tarrant has just offered me, in the plainest possible words, the hardest thing anybody has offered me in five years, and I have to say no to it, and I have to say no to it *for a reason that is about the book* and not about me, or I will not be able to live in this yard.

"Put it in those terms," she said. "Put the reason in the terms the four of you have got, because otherwise this is about me and I will not be able to stand it in a month."

Marek took it last, and he had it written on the back of a seed order before he said it, which is what he does.

"It is the third exchange requirement," he said. "That is the whole of it and it is the fifth reason and it is mine. A return has to go back in. Labour from four men on a peat road goes back into their ditch and not into our node, and if I take it and put it in our log as though it went into the node, then I have written a return on a piece of paper that did not happen, and I did that once already this season with a line about a seal and it cost this cooperative a charter, and I am not doing it a second time for a better reason.

"And there is a fourth thing I have not said and I am going to say it because I would rather say it than be asked. Thirty to forty yards, three nights, four men, and not one pound of it charged to anybody. That bypass is worth nothing against a cut. It is worth nothing against a cut and it is worth nothing against a bill, and it is going to be worth nothing against a flood if there is a flood, and the three notes about it in our book are all in the same form and all say the same sentence, and if in four months a man in a room asks me why a ditch that would have helped was not dug, the honest answer is going to be *because four men offered and we would not let them*, and that answer is true and it is going to be the worst thing anybody in this yard has ever said in public."

---

Tarrant did not argue with any of it. That is the thing about him that Marek thought about for two days afterwards, in the way he thought about things.

"I have had this in four counties," he said. "I have never once had anybody say yes and I have never once had anybody say no for a better reason than money, and I have got four men on a peat road and a barrow of tools and a fortnight, and the work is going to happen on the ninth of this month, at night, because that is when a ditch on ground nobody holds is dug, and because I have never in my life dug one in daylight in case somebody comes and asks me why.

"We come up your lane on the ninth because the low quarter is on the way and there is nowhere else to come up. I am telling you that now so that you are not surprised and so that you can decide in advance what you are going to find at the gate, and the only thing your body is going to decide is whether there is a spade in your yard or a word."

He stood up and put the whetstone on the table.

"One thing, and it is a practical thing and I am not going to be dramatic about it," he said. "There is a dog in that quarter. A collie, old, one ear off, and it stands in the dead channel at the bottom with its feet in the water and it will not let a goat through, and a boy of about eleven sits on the bank with his hand on it and brings it milk. If your ditch puts water back in that bed, that dog is not going to be standing there in three months. I do not know what it will be doing instead, and neither do you, and neither does the boy, and I have seen two of these move in my life and both times the other thing was worse.

"So if you dig it, look at the dog. That is all. I am not telling you what it means because I do not know and I would think less of anybody who claimed to."

Marek wrote it in the standing pressure log at the fourth hour, in his own hand, with the hour on it, as a thing reported at the gate by a person not named in the book, and the licensed person present column said *none*, and there was nothing underneath it, because there was nobody licensed there to write anything underneath it and because a dog at the bottom of a quarter is a fact about a quarter and not a cause of anything.

Tarrant ate the heel of bread standing up at the gate, which took about four seconds, and put the whetstone in his belt and went back down the peat road, and did not ask for a cup to take away, and did not say good morning, and nobody in the yard said anything to Marek about the offer for the rest of the day except one sentence from a woman on the north row at dusk.

"You let a man give you the one thing this month that you have not got," said Anneke Roos, "because he could not take it back. Write that sentence in your own book and read it back, and I will initial it, because in six weeks somebody is going to say that a cooperative which could not afford a bypass declined free labour, and I would like one line in the record that says the real reason and the real reason is not money."
