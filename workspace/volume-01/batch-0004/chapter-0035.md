# Chapter 35

## A Courier With No Seat

She came in at the gate at half past four on the fifth of the month with a bundle on her back and a knife in her coat and nine days in it, and she was not from the fen, and Marek knew she was not from the fen because she had not asked at the gate. She had walked up the lane, past the gate posts, into the yard, and stopped, and said her name.

"Mear Sallow. I have come from the Assemtry, which you will not have heard of, and I have walked nine days, and I would like to be let in and not talked to until I have eaten something, and then I would like to talk to you and be talked to, and I will tell you when."

That was the whole of the negotiation and Lissa Vale put two loaves and a heel of something on the mill floor within a quarter of an hour, and wrote the two loaves in the seed house's own column in the annex book with the hour, because the rules are the rules and a stranger is a person and a person is fed, and the yard noticed that she did it and did not comment on it, and the cost of two loaves on the fifth of a month, when the mill floor had been cold since the twenty-fourth because the wheel was not turning, was two loaves.

She slept in the annex and was gone before the yard woke, and Marek had not asked her a single question, and at half past six in the morning the yard was in the seed house with the fire going and she was at the table with her hands round a cup.

"Before anything else," she said. "Because if I do it in the wrong order I will have wasted the nine days and you will have wasted a night and neither of us will get it back.

"I am here to refuse."

---

She put the form on the table. It was the district's own hazard measure, and it had come to her because a hazard measure is a fire notice, and a fire notice is posted at the head of a drain and at the ends of the ground and at any house on the line, and the Assemtry's ground takes the line.

"Thursday," she said. "A man put it on a post at the head of our drain and nailed a second one to a gate and went off with a spare. It is a good copy, it is the same sheet, it carries the number of a form at the foot of the first page and there is no stamp on it anywhere, and I looked for the stamp, because a thing with a stamp on it is a notice and a thing without one is a form, and it matters which of the two you are being asked about." And on the second page, six times in different words, it asks the body last responsible to be satisfied that the root line beneath the windbreak is not a line the body relies on for the supply of the node, and that its severance is not a damage to the node."

"We are not the body last responsible," she said. "We have never been a body of anything. We are a settlement of about ninety people on ground the district calls Crown-held and has never held, and we have no charter and no seat and no name in any roll, and when your district's man came to the gate on the Friday to ask us for a signature on the second page, he was asking us the question we cannot be asked.

"Here is the refusal, and I will say it in the form I would like it repeated in, because you will repeat it. A consent is an answer. An answer has to be given in a place where the answer is heard by the people who asked the question and by the people the answer is about, and there is no such place for us at the Fennmere hearing, because there is no seat at that hearing for us, and there has not been one since it was constituted, and we have asked for none, and we will not ask for one now that there is something to sit in it about.

"So we cannot consent and we cannot refuse and we are not going to pretend to either, and the measure is void as against us from the moment it was posted, and we will not be asked again. That is the whole of it. It is not a threat. We are not threatening to do anything. We are telling you that a piece of paper has asked us a question we have no place to answer, and that the answer it gets is the answer you have just read, and that it will be the same answer in a month and in a year."

Anneke Roos said, "By whom."

"By us, which is nobody," said Mear Sallow. "That is the point of it and I have been walking nine days to make sure somebody heard it in a room with a floor in it rather than in a ditch."

---

"And now the thing you actually came about," said Marek, "which is the toe of the windbreak."

She looked at him for a moment. "Yes, and I am going to be angry about the order of those two things, and then I am going to stop, because I walked nine days and it is the same order in our own house and I have watched a woman lose a settlement's standing by putting her grievance first."

"We had a form in a yard on the third and we read it out and we argued about it for five hours, and we have been asking everybody we can find what is under that windbreak, and there is nobody in this district whose trade is a wild root line, and I said that out loud in a yard on the third as a thing that was the end of the road, and I would like to know whether I was wrong."

"You were right and you were early," said Mear Sallow. "The line goes under about two hundred yards of that windbreak and it comes up out of the ground at the toe, on the fen side, in about nine places, and it runs off north-west to the weir and on past the weir into ground that is ours.

"We do not call it the long line because it is long. We call it that because of what it is. The fence's feet are the willow. The long line is under it. They are two different things and a fire takes both, and there is no way on this earth to replant a long line, and I have watched a settlement try to replant one and I was not in it, and it is not the same shape afterwards for about forty years.

"Which is the whole of what I came to tell you, and I have told you, and now I am going to tell you what it costs you to have been told."

"You are not going in the book," said Lissa Vale. It was not a question.

"I am not going in the book," said Mear Sallow. "My name is not. The Assemtry's is not. Nothing I have said is to be entered by you anywhere, and I am not asking for that as a favour and I am not being mysterious about it, so I am going to give you the reason, because you have all been very correct about reasons all week and I have noticed it from nine days.

"You have a rule. Your licensed woman said it on Monday in your well house and somebody in your yard has been saying it ever since about a second reader and a reading taken inside a notice, and the rule is that a reading is not a thing one person did inside themselves, and a thing one person did inside themselves cannot be used against somebody else.

"We have the same rule and we have had it for four generations and we have never had a district to be used against. A name in a book is a thing a clerk can find. A settlement with no charter and no seat and no roll is a name that cannot be found, and that is the only protection we have, and the moment one of us is in a book that a district can carry to a hearing about somebody else's water, we have lost the only thing that is ours.

"So I am not in your book. And what I have said is not in your book. And in seven days you are going to file a form and write *not established* in a box on it, and you are going to do that knowing what is under the toe of that windbreak, and you are not going to be able to say so, and I have come nine days to make sure that the knowing is yours and not theirs, because in six weeks somebody in a room is going to say that a cooperative which knew the cause had never heard of a long line, and it will not be a lie, and you will not be able to argue with it, and I would rather you were told by a woman with a knife in her coat than find it out in Fennmere, and I would rather you were told it on the fifth than on the sixth."

---

Marek did the thing he does, which is to get to the words, and he got to them, and then he stopped dead and the yard saw him stop.

"Can I write one word," he said. "Not your name. Not theirs. One word, in the form of a fact about a hearing, and it is true and any clerk can check it, and it is about the shape of a room and not about a line. *The settlement holds no seat.* Five words. It is not a name. It is the fact that makes your refusal possible, and if it is not written down anywhere then in a month I have refused a document on the grounds of something a woman said in my kitchen and I have got nothing but my own word for it, and my word is worth what my word is worth."

"No," said Mear Sallow.

"You are refusing me the shape of my own refusal."

"I am refusing you the shape of your own refusal," she said, "and I am going to tell you why, and it is the same reason and not a better one. The shape of my refusal is that a settlement of ninety people has no seat in a proceeding. If you write that in a book, then somewhere in that district there is now a record that a settlement of ninety people has no seat, and you will have written it, and a settlement of ninety people is not a thing that anybody in that district can find, and the day it is findable it is because one of us wrote down in a book that we had no seat, and I would rather that the fact of our standing stay in the only two places in the world where it is safe, which is my head and yours.

"You are not being given a fact. You are being given a debt, and it does not come with a receipt, and you have to write it down, and I am sorry for that and I am not going to soften it because softening it would be a kindness and you would not be able to use a kindness in a cold room in six weeks."

---

The yard in the first week of a month with no charter and no money is a place where everybody can see everybody's arithmetic, and somebody in the seed house said the thing the morning had been going towards since she put the form on the table.

"You have walked nine days to tell us a fire is worse than we thought," said Ilsa Kern. "And I am the one who cut a hundred and six feet of that hedge for withy on Sunday of last month and entered it in a book with the hour on it, and I have been arguing since yesterday afternoon that a controlled burn is cheaper than a gale, and you have not said one word about the gale. So I will ask the question a foreman asks. If the district burns it without your answer, what happens to you."

"Then the long line goes," said Mear Sallow, "for about two hundred yards, and it does not come back in any of our lives, and the ground on this side of it will be different, and we will have to be different about it, and we will hold that. Not against the district. Against the day, and against everybody who was in the yard when it was offered and did not have standing to be asked and did not have standing to refuse it on the record. We will hold it for a long time, and we are not a people who say things loudly, and holding it is the only thing we have, and it does not need your permission and it will not need your name to survive your grandchildren."

"And if the cooperative writes a word in its book that a settlement has no seat," said Tova Reed, "then the Assembly will have a document about a cooperative in a district water record that has put a Thornwild settlement's standing in issue, and the Assembly's second clause will be about the licence, and there is a circular going round this month with a president's name on it, and a word in a book by a body with no charter about a settlement with no seat is the exact sentence somebody in an office is waiting for."

"Yes," said Mear Sallow. "That is what it is, and you have worked it out in a morning, and I have watched two settlements work it out in a generation."

---

Marek made the call at about nine, at the seed house table, and it took ninety seconds and there was no argument, and the absence of an argument was recorded because there ought to have been one.

"I will not enter it," he said. "Not her name, not theirs, not the two hundred yards, not the word seat. And I will not repeat it to the district, and I will not use it in the form, and if a hearing officer asks me on the forty-second what I know about the ground under the windbreak, I will say I know there is a windbreak and I have signed nothing about it and a settlement with no seat was not asked and would not answer, and that is the whole of what I will say and I will say it in those words and no others.

"And I want the refusal itself entered, because a thing I have been told and have obeyed is the only kind of document I am going to be able to write this month that is about anything other than money. One line. *Fifth day, ninth hour. A refusal was received at the seed house table from a person not named in this book, concerning a measure posted at the head of a drain, and the terms of the refusal were that nothing received is to be entered, and the terms were given and were understood. Recorded by the engineer of record. Witnessed: A. Roos. No name given, no name asked for, no name taken.*"

Anneke Roos read it back and put her initials on it, and then she said the thing that had been sitting in her all morning.

"That is a mark, not a record," she said. "A record has a thing in it. That has a shape and a silence, and in a year somebody will read it and know that something happened and not know what, and they will not be able to use it, and neither will we."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Then I want one sentence under it saying so, in your hand," said Anneke Roos. "I have spent four weeks in this yard teaching people that a document you can check is worth more than a document you have to believe, and you have just written the first one in this cooperative that you have to believe, and it is about the only honest thing anybody has said to us in a month. Write *this entry records the fact of a refusal and not its terms, and the terms are not held by this body*, and let the rest of the fen work that out for itself."

Marek wrote it and read it back and Lissa Vale entered the page number and the hour and shut the book.

Mear Sallow was at the gate at seven with her bundle on her back and the knife back in her coat, and she did not say good morning and neither did he, and she said one last thing at the gate post and it was not a speech.

"The offer stands until the sixth and then the district picks a day," she said. "You have until the sixth to decide whether you are going to sign. You cannot decide it on a fact of mine and you cannot decide it on a fact of theirs, and if the district's licensed man comes to a yard with a second page and a pen, the only thing any of you will have is the fact that you do not know what is under it, and I have now given you the reason that that is not a good enough reason, and it is that a foreman who does not know should stand in front of a fire and not sign anything.

"You will not sign," she said. "That is not a reading of you. That is a reading of the form."
