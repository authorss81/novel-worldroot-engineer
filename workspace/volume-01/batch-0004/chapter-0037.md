# Chapter 37

## A True Copy

They went up the fen road on the seventh of the month in two carts, at ten, so as to be inside by two, and the four of them were Marek and Dunmar Fitch and Lissa Vale and Anneke Roos, because the thing being done on the seventh was a book being read aloud, and a book read aloud needs the people who read it, the man who wrote most of it, and the woman who copies it.

Perrin Osse had the counter clear, one clerk sent out of the room and the other kept in the back, and she said so without being asked, which Marek noticed and wrote down.

"I am going to say the terms first, before I touch anything," she said, "because on the seventeenth of a month in this year I gave this room a document to hand back and you handed it back faster than I offered it, and I have thought about that for a fortnight and I have decided I am not going to be clever in this room ever again with any of you."

"First. The district will copy the standing pressure log entire, at the counter, in my hand, page by page, with the pages numbered as they are numbered, and I will certify the copy as a true copy of that book as it stands at the hour of the certification. Not a true copy of the node. Not a true copy of the cause. A true copy of the book. There is no fee. There is a box on my form for a fee and I am not going to use it, because a copy of a body with no charter is not a document of the district and the district does not charge for the privilege of holding one."

"Second. The copy goes into the file and it cannot be recalled by anybody who is not an officer of this office. Not by you, not by me, not by Mr Fitch, and not by the Assembly, and not by anybody who comes with a form. It is the district's document after today. You may keep a copy of the copy if you have paper and a willingness to be asked about it."

"Third. I will not certify the cause and I will not enter the cause, and if anybody in this room has come to this counter hoping for that I would rather they said so now and went home."

---

Dunmar Fitch said the thing that had to be said, and he said it standing up, in a clerk's voice, to a clerk.

"The claim in that log was moved on the fourth of last month in a full room and was lost nine to twenty-two with four abstentions. The book on your counter is a standing act of a meeting and not a filing, and it has never been a filing, and there is no corrected figure in it because this cooperative has never been given a figure to correct. What is in it is what a body of thirty-five people decided to write down about itself when it was frightened, and one of the things they decided not to write down was the number."

"The only thing this cooperative has ever put in front of the district is a cause of one line — the return branch as built falls short of the specification, and the cause of the shortfall is not established — and that is the only true statement in a file that has got a great deal in it. Enter that I have said it, with the hour, and enter that I am the clerk of the applicant of record and that a clerk of the applicant of record is not a witness and is not offering to be one."

Perrin Osse wrote it in her register and read it back and did not soften a word of it, and then she squared the log on the counter and opened it at the first page and said, "Right. Let us go."

---

They copied from eleven until half past three, and that is the least dramatic thing in this business and the hinge of it.

Perrin Osse wrote in a small square hand and did not hurry, and read each entry twice before she began it and once after, and twice in the afternoon she asked somebody to repeat a figure and both times it was Dunmar Fitch and both times he had it right. She stopped twice to ask whether a page was to be skipped and was told no both times. She ruled her own lines and carried the original's page number to the head of each, and at the end wrote, *end of copy, this being the whole of the book as it stands at the hour of certification*.

Lissa Vale stood on the public side of the counter and read every line of it back as it was made, all afternoon, and did not once raise her voice, and when Perrin Osse got to a figure wrong on the first attempt she said so before the sentence was finished.

The first thing she copied was the rule on the inside cover, all nineteen lines and the attribution and the two names in pencil round the edge of the last page, and she copied the attribution as an attribution and added nothing to it. The second thing that mattered was the thirtieth of last month at the ninth hour, in a licensed engineer's hand, and Marek's name under hers, and Gael Senn third, and Lissa Vale in the witness column: that the account this cooperative holds of the cause of the damage in the low quarter is a rumor with a witness in it.

And then the third, and Perrin Osse read it out before she wrote it, which is not what she had been doing all afternoon.

"This is the twenty-fourth of last month, tenth hour and forty minutes," she said, "and it is the entry of the three lines, and I am going to say to the room that I have been a clerk for twenty-two years and I have never copied a licensed reading into a district record in my own hand, and that I am going to copy it entire, in full, without elision, and I would like the reason entered, because the reason is that a copy with three lines left out of it is a copy of something else, and I would rather be holding a thing I can be asked about than a thing I have made."

She wrote them in. *The new run is fed. The new run is fed from below. Below is not yours.* In her own hand, in a district register, in a form that goes into a file that cannot be recalled.

Anneke Roos looked at it going in and said nothing at all, and Marek saw her not say it, and understood that the reason she was not saying it was that anything she said would be a reason to take the pen out of the clerk's hand, and she is not a person who reaches across a counter.

---

At about half past two, in the middle of a page, Perrin Osse put her pen down and said, "Stop. I am going to do something now and I would like the four of you to understand what it is before I do it, because it is not routine and I have not done it before for this body."

"Item N-2214/K is on a district works record in this office. It is Crown work. It is not held by anybody in this district. It has been executed three times and each execution is recorded in this office as completed work, and the record of completed work is a district record and I am the officer of it, and I can certify it to a hearing officer as a fact about the district's own file without producing a single page of the schedule, because the schedule is not mine to produce and the completion record is not a schedule."

"That fact is in your file for the asking. It is the only fact in this business that both of us can hold. And I am not going to put it in for free, because the district does not do things for free, and because I would be a poor clerk if I did not tell you what it costs."

"The district will certify that three stamped faces stand against item N-2214/K, and it will certify the dates at which the district's own record shows them made, on condition that the applicant of record discloses the source of its knowledge of the third. And your source, Mr Vale, is on a page in your own book, in your own hand, written in a margin at the sixth hour of the twenty-seventh of last month, and it says that a crew was expected on the crown, and it says *no contractor, no license, no money*, and it ends *this is worth nothing against a cut*."

"So that is what goes in. Your own note. Written before the cut, at the hour, by you, in a book you cannot file, certifying that the district knew about it too."

Marek stood very still for about four seconds.

"And what else goes in with it," he said.

"Nothing goes in with it that is not already in your own book," said Perrin Osse, with the pen squared. "But a hearing officer who holds that note will read it beside the inspector's statement of the twenty-eighth, which I am told says that on the twenty-seventh this cooperative was two hundred yards from a Crown work party and did not say. And both of those are true, and I am telling you that they will sit on the same page, and I cannot stop it, and I would not stop it if I could, because the note is the only reason the fact is available at all."

"Then put it in," said Marek, "and put the whole of it in, and put in beside it the reason the cooperative gave for not saying anything to a work party two hundred yards off, which is that the cooperative holds no charter over the branch and no right to stop the execution and had no notice, and that it objected on the crown on the morning of the day and was not on the list, and that a man with a board and a clip read the item number out loud on the crown once, and that the other one looked at the branch for a while and then said *you are welcome to stand there*, and that Mr Vale said *I am not on the list* and that man said *no, sir*."

"Entered," said Perrin Osse, and wrote it, and it took about four minutes and it is the longest single entry in that register for the year.

---

Dunmar Fitch then did the thing he had been carrying for three weeks, and he did it at a counter, in a cold room, with a clerk's pen and a witness, and nobody made him.

"There is a box in the district's file on this application," he said, "in the first box, which says *works already scheduled on this node*, and on the seventeenth of last month at the seventh hour I wrote the words *none known* in it in my own hand, and at the twelfth hour of the same day I entered a margin note saying that it had been false by noon."

"I did not tell you about the margin note in the twenty-eighth because I was not asked and because I have been carrying it since the seventeenth, and I have priced it twice and I have not spent it, and I am spending it now, and I am going to tell this room why before I do it, and then you can all say whatever you want."

"If I open that box in front of this counter, then a clerk of the applicant of record is a man who has put a false statement in a district filing. And a man who has done that is a man who is spent. He cannot be a witness to anything else in this file. He cannot be a witness to the standing log, or to the license sheet, or to the certificate, or to the statement of the thirtieth, and every one of those four documents is true, and I am the only person in this building who can lay them in front of a man in a room."

"So the true one line I filed in this office on the seventeenth is about to become the sentence of a clerk who lies, and it will be read as a lie, and the man who filed it cannot testify that it was not, because he will be the thing being complained of. That is what happens. I have watched it happen to a clerk in Cray in about nine years and I have not been the same afterwards."

"I am not protecting myself," he said. "I am protecting four documents, and the only way I can protect them is by staying a clerk who files true causes, and the box has to stay shut for that to be worth anything."

He looked at Marek.

"You may say that we should have opened it in the first week of the season, when there was no hearing and no file. You may say that a box that is kept shut is a box that is spent later at a worse time. I have thought about that for three weeks and I do not know the answer and I have not got anybody to ask, and I have come eleven miles to a counter to ask a clerk, and I am asking you. What is the date of the sitting."

"I do not know," said Perrin Osse. "The hearing officer has not been in this district yet. I have a list of dates and hers is not on it. I have had it in the tray since the twenty-fourth and I have not been able to move it, and I am not going to tell you that I have not been able to move it for thirteen days, because I have, and I moved it up, and it came back."

"Then the box stays shut," said Dunmar Fitch, "and I have said why, and put the hour on it, and read it back, and I would like it read back to me by somebody other than the clerk."

Anneke Roos read it back. He had asked her to and not the clerk, and she did, standing in a public part of a district office, in front of a fire, to a man from a cooperative that has no standing.

---

At four o'clock Perrin Osse certified it, and the certification is a page and a half and the operative words at the foot of it are five, and it took her about a minute to get them right.

"Here is what I am writing at the foot," she said, "and it is the whole of the bargain and I want it in your own book as well as in mine."

"*Certified as a true copy of the standing pressure log of the Lantern Reach Work Cooperative, so far as it goes, at the fourth hour of the afternoon of the seventh day of this month, in the hand of the undersigned, from the original produced by the custodian.*"

"So far as it goes," said Lissa Vale.

"So far as it goes," said Perrin Osse. "It is the only honest phrase available and I have used it twenty-two years. It means the copy is true of everything in the book this afternoon. It does not mean the book is finished. And it means that from this afternoon, if a page goes into that book, the difference has to be reconciled in this copy, and the only person in the world who may reconcile it is me, and I will only do it from the original with the custodian standing there."

"Which sounds like a small thing and is not," she said. "It means that book of yours can no longer be completed without a district officer in the room. That is the price of the copy and there is no other way to price it, and I have thought about a cheaper one and there is not one."

Lissa Vale copied the six words out into the cooperative's own book in her own hand, and entered the hour, and signed the line, and then she had to stand there for a second with the pen capped and the ink drying, in a public part of a district office, and Marek did not help her and she did not want him to.

The copy went into a satchel at four o'clock and the satchel went into the back room and the back room door shut, and Perrin Osse came out and took off her spectacles.

"That is half of it," she said. "The other half is a man with his own anchor, and he is sixty-one, and his notice runs from Friday, and I do not have a sitting date for you to put it in front of, and I have tried three times this month to move it up my own list."

"From this afternoon," she said, "half of what your body knows is in a room in this building in a hand that is not any of yours, and cannot be got back, and I have told four people that and I am telling you because the four of them already knew it and I would like you to hear it from a clerk rather than work it out at four in the morning."
