# Chapter 23

## Two Hundred And Fifty-Four

Gael Senn walked the ditch at six in the morning with a stick and found that the water had gone the whole thirty-one yards overnight.

It had not gone down the ditch. The ditch was the way it had gone and it had done the thing that water does in a new channel with a good bed in it, which is that it stops running along it and starts lying in it, and the ditch bottom from the tap to the head of the field was a continuous wet line with a shallow pool in every hollow and the four willow sets standing in it up to the lashings.

"Thirty-one," she said. "It is wet to the head at the first one and it is wet at the last one and I did not have to put my hand in it once."

Twenty-nine yards of that thirty-one had been dry ground on Saturday.

---

The root line came back into the field overnight and the field came up on the Monday.

Marek was at the head of the field at half past seven with the sun not properly over the embankment and he could see the difference from the top of the ditch, because the difference was not a color. It was a line. There was a hard edge running across the twenty-two acres about ninety yards below him, and below the edge the rye was standing up and above the edge it was not.

It had not come up evenly. It had come up from the ditch and stopped.

He went down to the edge. The plants below it were two feet tall and green from the ground to the last leaf, and the stems were stiff, and he put his hand into a row and pushed and the row moved about an inch and came back, and the soil under it was not dust. It was dark and it was damp to the depth of his knuckles.

Above the edge the crop was still lying along the row where it had lain since the first week of the season, and the heads were still white, and where he knelt down and looked at the ground it was grey and it had a crust on it the way a crust forms on a field that has been drinking nothing for a month.

"Twenty-nine," Ilsa Kern said, behind him. "Not twenty-two."

"Nineteen," said Marek, looking again. "That edge is at the far end of the ditch head scour and the scour took the water nine yards on the Sunday and the rest of it in one night, and there are nineteen acres below that line and there are three above it."

"Three of them are yours."

"Three of them are mine," Marek agreed, "and the three of them are the highest and driest ground on this holding and they have never needed a ditch, and they will come in last and they will come in on their own, and I have been waiting eleven weeks for them to do something and I am not going to admit to four people in a field that I am glad about it."

He was not entirely joking and Ilsa Kern did not entirely take it.

Then, from the bottom of the ditch, Gael Senn shouted, and it was not a shout about a field.

"Mr Vale! The beat. Come and feel this."

---

They got the pit open at eight and Marek was on the boards and did not go down.

Gael Senn was down there with her palm flat on the coping stone, which is how she felt it, because nobody in that building had ever got a better instrument for it and two of them had tried.

"It is fainter," she said. "It is coming up through the stone and I can feel it through the stone, and I could feel it through the pit rail on Friday. Come and put your hand on the rail."

Marek put his hand on the rail and could not feel anything at all, and neither could Ilsa Kern, and neither could Anneke Roos, and Jonas Byre put both hands on and swore, which is what Jonas Byre does instead of admitting that a man of forty-eight has been able to feel a thing in a stone slab that four other people in the same yard cannot feel.

"It is there," Gael Senn said. "It is just not through the rail."

"It is there and it is not through the rail," Marek said, "and I am going to write *present at the coping stone, not at the rail, eighth hour, second day of the month* and then I am going to write *cause not established* underneath it, because that is the rule and because Ms Quill is not here and because the alternative is that four of us put a cause in a book in a morning when we have just watched a field stand up, and a man who has had a good morning is the worst witness in this trade."

He wrote it on the spot, on the standing pressure log, at the hour, and read it back, and Gael Senn signed it and the licensed person present column said *none* and nobody wrote anything under that, because there was nobody to write anything.

It was back by noon. It was back at the rail by noon and by the afternoon it was in the frame again on the twenty-second and the twenty-third hour, and the pump laboured, and Marek wrote the hours down and the words *present again at the rail, twelfth and second hour* and did not write anything else.

---

The pump ran clean.

He tested it at the ninth hour with the collar shut and the pressure logged before the reading, and the head came up to eleven hundred and held it, and the frame stopped humming at nine hundred and fifty and did not start again until a thousand and forty.

"I want that written down the way it is and not the way it sounds," he said, from the boards, while Jonas Byre read the dial out and Anneke Roos wrote. "The head would not hold nine hundred before Saturday. It holds eleven hundred now. That is a dial reading and a pressure and it is not a day of water and I am not going to have anybody in six months reading it as one. Enter it as a dial reading at the ninth hour on the second of the month, and put the load under it and put the hour under the load."

He came up off the boards afterwards and said, to nobody in particular, the thing he had been carrying since the twentieth:

"It is holding at the log figure. For the first time in six years this head is holding the number the log says it gives, and it is doing it because of a ditch four people dug on a Sunday."

Nobody argued with him and nobody cheered. It was too cold for cheering and there was too much in it.

---

The miller sent word at eleven.

It came up the fen road on the mill's own boy, a lad of about fifteen who had walked eleven miles and been given a bun and was not shy about the number.

"Rowan Teale says to tell you the wheel," he said. "Says to tell you it in a straight line, and says you'll know what it means and I won't."

"Two hundred and fifty-four," said Anneke Roos, from the seed house door, before anybody had said anything.

The boy looked at her.

"Two hundred and fifty-four," he repeated. "And he said — he said it out twice, which he does not do for anybody, and he said, *it is seven short of the best this board has ever read, and it is one hundred and thirty-one on Friday.*"

Nobody in that yard had said the number out loud and it came into the middle of all of them from a mill boy with a bun in his hand.

Marek got the letter that Teale had written behind it, which was four lines, in a miller's hand, and read it standing up.

*Monday, second of the month. Wheel, 254, milling day. It is the highest this board has read since the spring your father went and it is seven short of the best figure on it, and I have read this board thirty-one years and I do not know how to say the next part so I am going to say it. On Friday it read 131. On the Wednesday before that it read 126. I have written both and I have written the time and I have put my hand on the chalk both times, and I am a miller and not a clerk and I do not know what a number means until somebody tells me, and I am telling you first, this morning, before the district, because the district will hear about it from me either way and I would rather you had it at nine than at four.*

*Rowan Teale, miller, Fennmere.*

He read it out and gave it to Dunmar Fitch, who entered the whole of it in the cooperative book with the hour, the miller's name, and the two earlier figures, and then stood with the pen in his hand afterwards and said, without being asked, that he had put three figures from a miller into the cooperative's book, and that a miller's figures were not records of a charter holder's node and never would be, and that he had entered them as a thing reported.

"You are the first cooperative I have been clerk of," Dunmar Fitch said, "that has put a miller's chalk figure in its own book and called it a report."

"It was going in somebody's book by the middle of the day anyway," Marek said. "It is only a question whether it is in ours in our own hand or in a district one in theirs."

---

The field held all that day and the day after, and the whole of the holding came and looked at it, and that was the end of something that had been going on for eleven weeks.

There had been a season in this yard where the standing joke and the standing fear were the same thing, and a man would walk out to the field and look at twenty-two acres of rye lying along the row and say *soon* or *not this year* and come back in. On Monday the standing joke was gone and the standing fear was still there but it had moved to a different object, and about forty people came out through the gate and walked down the ditch path to look at a line in a field.

Then somebody said the thing that made the day go quiet, and it was a drover's boy of about nineteen who had come in from the Fennmere road with nothing in particular, and who stood at the ditch head for about two minutes and then said:

"Is that green stuff for anything? You have got nineteen acres of it."

"It is fodder," said Tova Reed.

"For what?"

And the yard found out at the same moment, all together, standing about a ditch, what everybody else in Tarrow Fen already knew and what this holding had been pretending for a month was a matter of the future.

"For sheep," said Ilsa Kern.

There were no sheep.

The pasture flock had gone up the fen road on the night of the tenth with a drover's dog and a thing walking beside them, and had not been seen since, and not one animal had come back, and the only stock on the holding was four house-cows and two working horses and a mare of uncertain age belonging to a man who had died. Nineteen acres of the best green fodder in the district, fenced for sheep, with no sheep, and no cart, and a winter coming.

Tova Reed said it, because she was the one who said arithmetic in a yard.

"Seventeen hundred and twelve tons standing in that field this afternoon, and the price of hay in Fennmere in a bad year, and a cooperative with four pounds and five shillings in the box," she said. "You cannot cut it and you cannot sell it and you cannot eat it. And you could have cut it for a flock in September and the flock is on the peat roads somewhere and it will be dead by the spring. That is not a complaint, Mr Vale. That is the other half of your nineteen acres and I would like somebody in this yard to say it out loud, because in about a fortnight everybody is going to be looking at that field and forgetting that there is a second thing wrong with it."

Nobody had said it out loud, and Marek had not said it out loud, and it was in his own arithmetic already — the flock had gone on the tenth and the compost had gone with it, and he had spent a month proving that the compost was a number and had not spent five minutes on the other number, which was a hundred and thirty head of animals.

He said so, to the ditch, in about eleven words, and Ilsa Kern told him to shut up, and they went home.

---

Jonas Byre came up onto the boards at the seventh hour that evening to read the return dial for the eighth hour because that was his job and had been his job for three weeks and he did it from above the pit because he was not going down four feet ever again.

He read it twice, and Marek was on the boards with him, and it read forty-one on a scale of forty-eight, which was where the counter had read on the first morning of the season with everything shut, and Jonas Byre read it twice and then said:

"That is the same as the first day."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Then it has not moved."

"It is not supposed to move," Marek said. "It is a static lift with the collar shut. The number on this dial is a number about the head and not about the return."

"Right," said Jonas Byre.

And then Jonas Byre put his thumb on the second dial, which was a new one, a cheap reed gauge they'd clamped on the return main at the eleventh rod on the Sunday because nobody had thought to bring a good one, and he looked at it, and he took his thumb off it, and he said:

"That one has moved. It is on about the peg since Saturday. I do not know what it was on Saturday. There is nobody in this building who does know what it was on Saturday, because we did not write it down on Saturday, and I read it on Saturday and I did not write it down because I did not think anybody was going to be interested."

Marek took his own book out of his coat and there was no figure on any page for that dial, and there had not been one since the third of the season, and the four of them had spent a month looking at a field and a pit and a mill in three different places and had let the one instrument nobody was watching go out of the book.

Jonas Byre stood on the boards in the dark with a reed gauge under his thumb and waited.

"Write what it reads now," Marek said. "Write the hour and the fact that it is on about the peg and the fact that it was not read on Saturday and that the reading for Saturday cannot be supplied. And write it as your reading and not as mine, because it is your thumb on it."

Jonas Byre wrote it in the standing pressure log at the eighth hour and signed it, and the licensed person present column said *none*, and Marek did not say anything at all about the eight feet of return main on the other side of that reed that had come up short since a hundred and thirty-one turned a mill wheel on Friday and before that had been lower still, and neither did Anneke Roos when she read the entry out loud at the seed house door at nine o'clock with the door open, which she did now every night without being asked, because a book that is read aloud in a yard is a book that cannot be quietly altered.
