# Chapter 22

## The Eleventh Rod

They were on the ditch line at half past six on a Sunday morning with four sets heeled in at the gate, because a willow set has to be cut out of the windbreak with the ground still on it and lifted the same day and heeled in before the sun is on it, and because a willow set carried about loose for two days is a willow set that has lost half of what it had to lose and does not know it.

Four of them went into the line and one of them stayed on the boards.

Marek said that out loud at the top of the ladder before anybody was in the ditch, because somebody was going to have to say it eventually and he was not going to let it be said by Sera.

"I am not coming down," he said. "Ms Quill has told me twice and she is right twice, and I am going to hold the boards and read the dial and say the numbers, and if I put a hand in that ditch she will take me out of it by the collar in front of all of you, and I have told her she may and she has told me she will."

"I will," said Sera Quill, from the rail, without looking up.

"Good," said Anneke Roos, and went down the ladder first, because the low acre had taught her to start at the bottom of a job.

The ditch was the north ditch of the outer field, the one that had been cut forty years ago by a man whose name was on a sluice and which had silted to a foot and a half and gone to a dense mat of fen weed from the eleventh yard onward. It ran from the embankment foot down and across to the head of the field, and at the head of the field it opened into a scour the old drainage had made where the field's own root line came up out of the ground and went along the bottom of the ditch for about nine yards before it went back under.

That scour was the whole of it. The field's root line lay in a ditch bottom and it had been lying in a dry ditch bottom for three seasons, and the plant had gone over sideways along the row because a plant lying along a row is a plant that has stopped standing up and started traveling, and a crop travels toward whatever is under it, and what was under it was nothing.

"If we get water into that scour," Marek said, from the boards, "the root line will be in water again in a day and a half. I am not going to promise you the field. I am going to promise you the ditch."

Ilsa Kern was in the line with a spade and had not needed to be told anything about ditches.

"You will get water into the scour," she said. "You will not get it into the eleventh rod. The stone is at the eleventh rod and it is not a stone, it is a fence post with a root round it, and it is a fence post that is going to be a fence post until it is a piece of a fence post, and I am not going to cut a fence post out of a live main with a bar."

"Say what you would do."

"I would take the new run off the main *before* the eleventh rod," said Ilsa Kern, "and run it along the ditch and let it into the scour at the head, and leave the stone where it is, because the stone is not the fault. The fault is twenty-two rods of eight inch and four hundred a month and no charter, and the stone is a thing that is in the way, and those are three different problems and only one of them is in a ditch."

Marek stood on the boards and looked at the diagram he had drawn at ten o'clock the day before, and it was wrong, and she had told him it was wrong in about eleven seconds from the rim, and the diagram came off the board and went into his coat, and there was a new one on the board inside a quarter of an hour with the tap taken off before the eleventh rod and the old main left alone.

"That is the whole of what I have for it," he said. "Say the rest."

"The rest is the fiber," she said. "You have got four baskets of a thing that has been in a tar pit for four years and never once got wet, and a new ditch wants a bed, not a body. Lay it in the bed along the whole run, three inches, and let the ditch do the rest. And you have got four sets of willow and no root plate, because the mill boy brought them up on a handbarrow and a man who cuts a willow set without the root plate on it has cut a stick and would like it counted as a set."

"The root plate is on them," Marek said, from the boards. "He heeled them in at the gate last night under my instruction, in wet sacking, in a hole, and the hole is filled, and they are standing in a hole in the ground and they have been since nine o'clock last night."

Ilsa Kern looked at the four dark squares of turned-up turf in the yard by the gate and then at the man on the boards and did not say anything at all, which was the most she had ever said to him in public.

Then they dug.

They dug for nine hours and they did it in pairs and the pairs changed at the fourth hour because two people with one spade and a bar make a mess and not a ditch, and Marek timed the changes from the boards because that was a number he was allowed to have. Anneke Roos and Gael Senn at the head end where the ground was worst. Jonas Byre and Ilsa Kern at the tap end, and Jonas Byre did not go down four feet, and did not argue about it, and said so out loud once so that it would be in the working notes.

The fiber went in cold and wet and it stank, and it was the worst-smelling three inches of work in Tarrow Fen and it was the best pack in the county, and by the fourth hour they had laid it the whole length of thirty-one yards and had a hand's depth of the ditch left to fill.

That was where the four willow sets went in.

Two at the tap end, in the bank either side of the new opening, driven with a stob and a maul and not hammered, because a willow set driven hard splits its own root plate and a willow set driven into wet ground with a wooden maul closes around it. Two at the head end, in the scour, laid in at an angle so that they leaned across the run and not down it, because a willow wants the water to come and go across it and not along it.

The lashings were withy, cut green that morning off the windbreak, and there was not enough of it, so Ilsa Kern cut forty feet of it and then cut sixty more and then stopped cutting and stood looking at the windbreak for a while with the hook in her hand.

"Forty feet of willow round," she said. "That windbreak is down to the old wood along that forty feet and a bit, and it will not be a windbreak there in a year, and the fen comes from the west."

"Then say it and I will write it in the working notes with the hour," Marek said, "because it is a cost and it is ours and it is today."

"You write it," said Ilsa Kern. "I am not writing anything about a windbreak I have cut four times in my life for a thing that might work."

Marek wrote it in the working notes, in the open, with the hour, and read it back, and Anneke Roos initialled it because it was an opinion and not a record, which was the second time in a month she had initialled and not signed.

---

Sera Quill was on the rim for the whole of the ninth hour and she came down twice — to look at the tap, and to put her own hand flat on the ditch wall for about a minute without saying anything — and both times she went straight back up.

The four hands were in the ditch. The licensed engineer was on the rim. The engineer of record was on the boards with a dial in his hands and had spent nine hours saying numbers to four people who were better at the job than he was and who had not once told him so.

At the fourth hour Ilsa Kern changed the plan twice without telling anybody, and both changes were right, and Marek found out about them at the seventh hour from the state of the ground, and at the eighth hour he said one sentence, which was that the joints wanted packing in before the sets went in and not after, and Ilsa Kern said, "They do," and neither of them mentioned it again.

At half past four they let water into it.

The main was opened at a point twenty-two yards up from the eleventh rod — a low place, in the bank, above the frost line — and the plug was a one-inch willow driven into a hole cut with a taper and then packed with fiber behind it, and the water came out of the hole in a tongue and went down the new run and along the ditch and into the scour, and it took about eleven minutes to reach the head of the field, and Marek timed it twice and wrote both figures down.

At five o'clock the water stood in the scour about a hand's depth and lay along the field's root line for nine yards and stopped at the first hollow and did not go past it.

Gael Senn went and got a stick and put it in the water at the far end of the nine yards and the stick was wet to the height of the water, and then she put it on the bank beyond and it came out dry.

"Nine yards," she said.

"Nine yards," Marek said, from the boards. "And the root line goes along there for thirty-one and it has to have the whole of it, and we have put water in nine of it, and the rest of it is going to want the ditch dug another eighteen inches deeper over four days and I have not got four days and the frost is coming."

"There is a frost in the ditch tonight," said Ilsa Kern. "There is a frost in the ditch every night this month. That is why the fen is where it is."

Marek wrote it down.

Then somebody asked, from the bottom of the line, the question that had been sitting underneath the whole morning, and it was Anneke Roos and she asked it with her back to the bank and her spade in her hand.

"The old main."

"I have got to it," Marek said.

"You have not. Nobody has got to it, because we have been digging a new ditch since six o'clock and the thing that actually feeds the seed house is a fence post." Anneke came up the bank a step and stood on the lip of the new run. "You have made a second way for water to go out to a field. You have not made one way for water to come back. That main is twenty-two rods of eight inch with a fence post in it at the eleventh rod, and it is the seed house's main, and it is the mill race's main, and it is the only main there is, and it is exactly as bad as it was yesterday, and the shortfall on Ms Quill's sheet does not go down by one hundredweight because we have dug a ditch."

"No," Marek said. "It does not."

"Then it is not a repair," said Anneke Roos. "It is a *run*. It is a very good run and I have dug in better ground this year than I dug today, and it will work, and it will carry water to a field. And there is a fence post in the seed house's water in the same ground and it will be in the seed house's water in February, and when somebody asks in six months what we did about the stone at the eleventh rod, the answer is that we dug a new ditch and left it."

Marek took his own drawing off the rail and turned it over and wrote on the back of it, in the middle of the field, *the old main is not touched; the stone is not touched; nothing about the seed house or the mill race is improved by this*, and read it out loud, and then read out that it was going into the working notes at the ninth hour with the hour on it and that it was in his own hand and that this was not a thing engineers usually wrote down.

"Put it on the stake," said Ilsa Kern, from the tap end.

"I will put it on the stake."

"You will not," she said. "That stake says what the anchor is. A stake is not a place for a thing that is not fixed yet. Put it in the book. And if in six weeks the new run is doing what you say it will do, then somebody in a cold room is going to read the book and see that on the twenty-first four people knew about the fence post, and that is worth more to you than a good line on a post."

He wrote it in the working notes at the ninth hour and read it back, and Anneke Roos initialled it because it was an opinion and not a record, and it was the third time in a month.

At six they filled the top two feet with the silt and the spoil and rammed it with the bar, and the new run was a ditch with a new bed in it and a good bed in it and four willow sets in it, and it was the least impressive object on the holding and it was the only new watercourse in Tarrow Fen.

Then they put a willow stake in at the head end, at the four-o'clock side of the scour, and Ilsa Kern cut it with her own knife because the others' knives were blunt and she would not have it cut badly.

The stake carried the date, and under the date the length, and under the length the four names of the four hands, and under the four names a single line, which was:

*Anchor: willow, four sets, south embankment windbreak. Live at the date above. To be cut out and looked at in the spring.*

And no licensed engineer's name on it anywhere, and Marek noticed and did not say anything, and Sera Quill noticed him noticing and said, from the rail:

"There is a name on a piece of paper in the cooperative book with a shortfall in it, and there is my name at the top of that paper, and my name does not need to be on a stake. If that willow dies the next one is somebody else's job and it will be a different man, and I have no business on a stake that might be in a bonfire by the third week of February."

She put her case under her arm and looked at the ditch for a while.

"Now go and stand at the top of the field," she said, "all four of you, and look at it, and do not tell me anything about it until tomorrow. I have seen men look at a thing they have just made and describe it, and there is nothing on the earth that will make a description true."

They stood at the top of the field in the dark and looked at twenty-two acres of rye that had been lying along the row since the first week of the season.

Nothing had happened yet. It does not happen that night. Marek knew that and the four of them knew that and nobody said it out loud, and they walked back up the fen path behind the embankment in the dark with the tools over their shoulders, and the ditch they had opened that day had a wet line down the middle of it and the stick in it, and a hundred and twenty yards away in Fennmere a miller named Rowan Teale looked at his board and could not make the number for the third time running and chalked it anyway.
