# Chapter 26

## A Dog On The Bottom Of The Quarter

They went down at first light with a lamp and a dial and two buckets and a crowbar, and it is eleven miles by the fen road to Fennmere and about nine of them are the road, and they made it in three hours with one stop at the third gate, and Marek made the stop himself and said it out loud, because a man who is late for a thing he has promised is a different animal from a man who is early and has not got there yet.

Sera Quill walked in front. Marek walked where she could see him. Gael Senn carried the log and the lamp. Jonas Byre carried the dial and did not offer to carry anything else, because his chest had been bad since the fifteenth and eleven miles was a great deal further than four feet and he had been honest about the difference since the Sunday in the pit.

They went into the low quarter at about half past nine on a gray morning with no wind at all.

---

The top of the quarter is Fennmere and the bottom of the quarter is nine hundred yards of wet gravel and forty-one houses, and you can see the whole of it from the top because there is nothing to stop you, and you can see the water in it going away from you the whole way down.

The branch comes out of the fen side at the bottom.

It comes up out of a bank in a bed that is about four feet across and has a stone lip on the west side that a man built, and it used to have a sluice and a plank bridge over it and a set of steps down to the water and a name, and the steps are still there and two of them are gone, and the bed is a dead channel with brown water an inch deep in it and the water does not move.

Marek got down to it and could not get to it. That is the thing to say first and it is the thing he wrote down first.

He got to within nine feet of it and stopped, because the ground on the east side of the branch is a fen gravel that will take a man to the knee and a loaded barrow to the axle, and the animal was in it.

It was an old collie. Black and white, heavy through the chest, with a left ear that has been off at some point and healed crooked, and a coat that had gone thin along the spine, and it was standing in the dead channel with its feet in the water up to the hock, and it was not doing anything at all.

That was the first thing that frightened him, and it was not the animal. Nothing was happening. There was no jaw going and there was no line of sheep and no dog working back and forth, and there was nothing in the ten yards of it that was doing anything, and it was still the most occupied piece of ground in the bottom of the quarter.

Then a boy of about eleven came up the bank with a goat on a rope, and the goat came to within fifteen feet of the branch and stopped and would not go on, and the boy pulled and the goat sat down, and the boy said, without any surprise in it at all, "Told you," to the goat, and sat down on the bank beside the dog and put his hand flat on its back.

It had been moving things off that line. It had been moving things off that line for four days, and before that it had been doing something else, and the something else was over, and it had stayed.

Marek stood nine feet away with a crowbar under his arm and read Lissa Vale's rules off in his own head, word for word, the way you recite a thing you have decided to be able to recite, and they all fitted and none of them helped.

It could not be called off. It could not be tied up. It was not a thing that could be got rid of by killing what it stood on, and it was not a nest, and it was not mad, and it was not hunting; it was herding, and the line it was holding was a line in the ground where a branch had run once and had not run for five years and had been dead for longer than that, and the only thing that moved it was a change in where the water went.

He had heard all of that. He had also seen a man walk past a flock of a hundred and thirty animals on a twelve-foot embankment and take four seconds to put his hand on dead wood and a crowbar into a seam, and he had not called it off then either, and a girl had been on a plank.

"Sera," he said.

"No," said Sera Quill, before he had finished the word. "And you have not said it, and I would like it entered that you did not say it, and I am going to say the rest of it so that you do not have to. That ground will take a knee and it will take a bar and it will take a man who is careful and it will not take a man who is in a hurry, and you are not going to be in a hurry in it today because I have your arm and I have your face and I have known you for six weeks and you have the face of a man who is about to do something to an animal."

"I was not going to do anything to the animal," Marek said.

"You were going to do something to the *ground*," said Sera Quill, "and that is the same sentence with a different noun, and I would like you to hear how it sounds."

---

They spent two hours on the four wells, and the wells were the reason for the whole walk.

Corvin Slee's pail: filled from the top of the well, swung in, and emptied into a tub. Marek timed it with a watch and did it twice, and Gael Senn did it four times because she did not trust a number one man had timed.

On the sixteenth day of the month, Corvin Slee said, it came up in a count of ninety. On the twenty-second, he said, it was three minutes. On the twenty-fourth it was four and a half. This morning it was five minutes and forty seconds, and it was five minutes and fifty on the fifth run, and Marek wrote both figures down and wrote the well's depth and the depth of the standing water in it and the depth of the water in the tub, and read the entry out loud, and then asked for the one thing in that yard nobody had thought to ask for.

"How deep is your gravel."

"About nine feet, and then it is clay."

"Nine feet of gravel over clay, and nine feet of a well into it," said Marek, "and you have a man from the water company who says the well is sound."

"He does."

"Then the well is sound and the ground it is cut in is not," said Marek, "and the well is the least of it. The water company has been looking at your pipe and your lining and your bucket and your pump, and every one of those is a well, and none of them is a fen."

He put his hand on the boy's head without meaning to, because the boy had not moved and had not said anything and was still sitting with his hand on the old dog's back, and the boy looked up at him with total indifference and moved away about four inches.

"What is your name," Marek said.

"Joss Pyle."

"Where is your grandmother's dog from, Joss."

And the boy said, without any weight on it at all, as though he had been asked the time:

"This one," he said. "She had it when I was little and she is six years dead and it has been in my yard for four years and it will not go in and it will not come out and it does not care about the goat and it does not care about me and my mother says she is not going to have it put down, and she has not, and I bring it milk."

---

Sera Quill got the branch's bed open in one place with a flat spade and about ten minutes of work, and what was under the water was the worst thing Marek had looked at in a month, which was nothing at all.

The bed was dry.

The branch under a foot of mud and eight feet of gravel had been a live trunk for longer than the charter and it was not live, and its wood was grey and hard and had a mineral bloom on it the way the Weir Row faces had, and there was no wet line on it anywhere, and there was no iron coming out of it either.

"It is not leaking," Sera Quill said, kneeling. "That is what I want him to understand. It is not leaking and it is not cut here and it has not been cut here in five years and there is nothing on this bed that anybody has done. This is a branch that was killed from the top."

"From the top," said Marek.

"From above. Twice, with a die, and the second time eleven days ago, and then opened three feet with a bar and a pair of shears, and every one of those is two hundred yards from here and nine miles the other way, and the reason nothing comes up this bed is that there is nothing coming up it, because the whole column above it is open air now." She sat back on her heels. "I am not going to say what killed it, because I have got no document and no instrument and I am a woman standing in a wet ditch in a town I have not been in for eleven years.

"I will say this. That bed is the outlet for the gravel under forty-one houses, and while it was taking, it turned that gravel over four times a day and the iron came off the fen mud and washed through and out. And it has not turned that gravel over as it did since the spring before last, which is a thing every man in that quarter could tell you and not one of them can say so. What has happened to Mr Slee's well in the last four days is not iron coming off my fen. It is iron that was already in that gravel, standing still, and it comes up when the head goes, and the head has gone because your head is taking more out of this node than it was taking a fortnight ago, and this bed is where the taking stops.""

"There is no licensed draw on that branch," said Jonas Byre, from the top of the bank, in the flat voice a man uses when he is reading something off an instrument or is about to have to read it in a room. "Not since the eighteenth. Mine lapsed on the eighteenth and Mr Rusk's has never been above three hundred and I have seen it and I have said so in this yard before you people came."

Nobody answered him and he did not press it, and he went and stood twenty feet off, and Marek found out later that he had walked back up the bank to get away from a conversation in which he was the only one who had measured something and could not say what it meant.

---

They came up off the bottom of the quarter at two in the afternoon and went north along the mill race to where Rowan Teale had his head race shut, and Renn Tallow was standing in it up to the knee with a bar and a rope, doing a job.

He had been in the mill head race a long time and had done the whole of it and had the gate barrowseated when they came along, and he looked up and saw the five of them and did not stop.

"Mr Vale," he said.

"Renn."

"I have got a line here for a man who has not got a charter to stop me and no reason to." He put the bar down and stood in the race up to his knees. "I am not saying that to be clever and I have not got a speech. That branch has not had anything coming up it since the spring before last and every man in the bottom of that quarter knows it and not one of them can say so, because the man who can say so has a charter and has not been down there in six years and does not want the conversation, and the men who cannot say so are the ones with the wells."

Then he picked the bar up.

"I have got nothing else to say to anybody from that yard," he said, "and I have not forgotten the gate on the tenth, and I have not come up here to make that a thing, and you are going to go on not making it a thing, and I would like that recorded by whoever records things."

---

They came off the ground at the top of the quarter at about half past four and Marek stood in the road with eleven miles behind him and could not write a thing down.

That was the part that had taken him.

He had seen the branch. He had seen the bed, and the dead wood, and the wells, and the head gone out of the gravel, and the old dog standing in a dead channel with a boy's hand on its back, and every part of it was in his own head at the correct resolution and none of it could be put in a book.

Because the branch is the branch at Weir Row. Because the Weir Row branch is item N-2214/K in a district works schedule, and the order date in that row is older than Marek's oldest cut, and the two stamped faces are the two stamped faces, and the third execution was due on the twenty-seventh, which was tomorrow.

And if he wrote the words *the branch at Weir Row* in the cooperative's book tomorrow, in a document intended to go to Fennmere, then a clerk in Fennmere would put that document beside an application endorsed on the seventeenth, and the first box of that application said *works already scheduled on this node*, and Dunmar Fitch had filled that box in at seven in the morning on the seventeenth with the words *none known*, and had entered the margin note himself saying that it had been false by noon the same day.

The schedule was in a satchel in Fennmere. The schedule was not in the cooperative's book. The cooperative's book contained no evidence of any of it but a measured drawing with a lamp-keeper's signature on it, and a section-nine note signed by nobody but Marek, and the drawing proved *when* and not *whether*.

So the one document in the world that would prove the branch was somebody else's work was in a clerk's satchel, and the only route to it ran through a box that said the cooperative knew of nothing.

Gael Senn said it, because she had been carrying the log for three miles and had been thinking.

"Mr Vale," she said. "We can say the water is iron. We can say it got worse on the twenty-first. I can time a pail and you can time a pail and Jonas can time a pail and that is a fact with four witnesses and a number on it and none of us is licensed and none of us is a witness the district has to believe.

"And if you put the Weir Row branch in that book, then the only thing in this fen that says this is not our fault is a piece of paper Mr Rusk is not going to hand you, and a renewal application that says we knew of nothing, and a box in your clerk's office that is a confession in his own hand.

"So what is the document."

Marek looked at her for a moment, and then said, "It is a document that cannot be made," and Gael Senn said, "Then write that one down as well," and he wrote it in the working notes at the fifth hour with the hour on it, in his own hand, and it was the shortest line in the book that month and it was the only one he was sure of.

He wrote: *Walked the low quarter, Fennmere, fifth of the month, six of us. Cause of the change at the four wells on that ground: the gravel under the quarter has stopped being turned, and the branch that turns it is dead at the bed, and I can prove the second half and I cannot prove the first half in a document, and the document that would prove it cannot be obtained by this cooperative, and the reason it cannot be obtained is at the top of this page and is a statement of what we did not tell the district on the seventeenth day of the month.*

Anneke Roos would have read it aloud if she had been there. There was nobody to read it, and he signed it, and five people walked eleven miles back up the fen road in the dark with a log that could not be filed, and at about the third gate Jonas Byre said, out of breath, in the dark, "It is going to be worse before the frost," and neither Marek nor Sera Quill asked him how he knew, because he had been counting the mill wheel in his head for thirty-one years and there was no instrument in Tarrow Fen that could tell him anything he had not already said at the top of the quarter.
