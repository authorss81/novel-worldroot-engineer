# Chapter 30

## A Rumour With A Witness In It

They did it at the well house bench with the log open, because Ilsa Kern would not have it done in the seed house and would not have it done at a table in the yard, and the reason she gave was that the well house was the only room in the building with a stone floor and one door, and a thing said once in a room with one door is a thing that happened once.

Seven of them, and it took until half past four. Sera Quill had her case and did the going through, because the rule is hers and because if anybody else did it they would do it in Marek's order, and Marek's order is the order that makes a cooperative look like the cooperative thinks it is. She went through it as entries: date, hour, name, and what a stranger could check without asking anybody anything, in that order, out loud, with nobody permitted to explain.

---

"Fourth of the month, tenth hour and forty minutes, licensed reader, four witnesses. A fieldbook at the copper seam returned three lines." She read them. "That is a reading. It is a licensed reading, on the fourth, by me, with four witnesses, at the hour, and it is the only licensed reading that has ever been taken on this node, and Mr Vale has never taken one, because he is not licensed to take one and has never said that he was. It is in your own book, in a form your own rule made, and it is dated.

"Now I am going to tell you what it is worth, and I have taught this for thirty years and I have watched four hundred people not learn it.

"A reading is not a fact. A fact is a thing a stranger can walk up to and check without believing anybody. A reading is a thing one person did inside themselves with an instrument, and it is as good as that person is and as good as the hour it was taken, and it is worth nothing to anybody who has to use it against somebody else.

"An unlicensed reading is a rumour with a witness in it. That is the phrase and I did not invent it and I have never improved on it. Your fieldbook reading from the eighth of last month is a rumour with a witness in it. Your fieldbook reading from the twentieth of last month is a rumour with a witness in it. Mr Vale's four seconds in a flooded pit on the tenth is a rumour with a witness in it, and I was the witness, and I have said in three books that I would not stand up and say what he got, and I will not.

"And the fourth of the month is not a rumour with a witness in it. It is a licensed reading with four witnesses and an hour on it, and it is the only thing in this building worth a hearing officer's afternoon, and it is worth it to the district and not to you, because it says *below* and *not yours*, and the district has already read it out loud at a counter in front of a clerk, twice, and carried it away in a satchel."

Nobody spoke for a while.

"Then what is the cure," said Marek, though he had known what the cure was since the eighth of last month and had been not asking it for three weeks.

"Two things," said Sera Quill. "There are only two things, and there have only ever been two things, and people who come to me with a third thing have usually had it invented for them in a room.

"A second licensed reader. On the same physical node. Through her own anchor, which means her own bead and her own clip and her own contact, and not through mine, and not in my room. On her own date, after mine, without knowing what I read, and without anybody in the room telling her what I read before she has read it. If she reads *below* and I read *below*, that is not one reading twice. That is a thing that can be checked.

"And dated records, made at the hour they describe, that a clerk can take away and hold. Not a cooperative's own book that a stranger may come and look at. Taken away. Held. A copy that leaves this yard in a clerk's satchel and is in a file in Fennmere with a stamp on it and a number, and cannot be recalled by anybody who is not a district officer.

"That is the whole of it. Two things. A second reader and a set of records that travel."

She squared the case on the bench.

"Now I am going to tell you what you have, and I am going to do it in front of the clerk, because he is in the room and because he is entitled to hear a licensed engineer price her own usefulness to a cooperative that cannot pay her.

"The second reader. The Assembly's roll for this district has two names on it. One of them is mine. The other is a man called Idris Vane who is sixty-one, lives eleven miles from that counter, and has taken no paid work since his wife died, and who is a better reader than I am and has been doing it longer.

"He would come. He would come in three days if you asked him and read the node through his own anchor in his own time and give you his figures unedited. And he is paid a day rate, and the day rate is more than fourteen pounds, and the cooperative has four pounds and five shillings and a woman's fee in February coming due.

"But that is the money, and the money is not the reason, and I want that written down. The reason is the notice. An independent reader requires notice. The notice is fourteen days. The representations on the schedule of damage are due in eleven working days, and the sitting has not been appointed and will not be for a month. So even if Mr Vale walked to that man's house tonight with the four pounds and five shillings in his coat, the reader could not read inside the notice, and a reading taken inside the notice is not an independent reading. It is a second opinion with the temperature still on it.

"So there is no second reader in this month. Not because you cannot pay for one. Because the month does not have one in it."

---

"And the records," said Dunmar Fitch, who had been waiting for it and had his hand on the closed book.

"Mr Fitch is going to tell you that none of it travels, and he is right, and I would like him to say it himself so that it is in his mouth and not mine."

"It is not going to travel," said Dunmar Fitch, "and I will not have it said kindly. The standing pressure log is the cooperative's own book. On the fourth part, second clause, of a charter that lapsed on the eighteenth, a record is a record in the book of the holder. There is no holder. So that book is a book kept by a body of people with no standing, and a district clerk may look at it and may copy it and may carry the copy away, and may not certify it, and every line in it is the word of a body that has just been billed sixty pounds ten for something it cannot prove.

"The open book is not a filing. It has never been a filing. On the third day of last month the motion to enter the claim in the log was moved and lost nine to twenty-two with four abstentions, in a room of forty, and what it became instead was a standing act of the meeting, and a standing act of a meeting is a thing you do, not a thing you have done to anybody. Perrin Osse may look at it. She may not take it. It goes away with me."

He put his hand flat on it.

"And I filed one thing to the district on the seventeenth. One sheet. It says the return branch as built falls short of the specification and the cause of the shortfall is not established. That is true, it is the only true thing this cooperative has ever put in front of that office, and it is now a thing that is in a file which has had a report on it and a schedule of damage on it, and the first box of the form it is attached to says *works already scheduled on this node* and I filled that box in at seven in the morning on the seventeenth with the words *none known*, and I entered the margin note myself at noon, in my own hand, saying that it had been false by noon the same day.

"So if you want the schedule — and I have not said one word to any of you about wanting it, and Mr Osse has said his piece at a counter and I am a clerk and not a man who wants things — then the schedule comes into a file, and the file has my box in it, and the box has my confession in it, and Mr Fitch of the Lantern Reach Work Cooperative explains to a hearing officer why he certified ignorance of a work programme on the seventeenth of a month in which he read row forty-one with five people in a seed house and wrote two margin notes about it.

"That is the price of the schedule. I have been carrying it since the seventeenth and it is the reason I have not said no to anybody in this yard for thirteen days."

Anneke Roos put her hand on the book too, and there were three hands on a clerk's book on a well house bench, and she took hers off again because hers was not for holding things with.

"There is a third thing," she said, "and it is not a record and it is not a cure and I would like it said in this room because nobody is going to say it in a month.

"The open book has been standing on that table under a stone with a slate on it since the fourth of last month, and the standing log has been on its board, and Anneke reads the standing log out loud at the seed house door every night at nine o'clock and anybody can come and stand and listen to it and any stranger can come and ask for the open book and be handed it without notice.

"Not one person has come.

"Twenty-six days. Not the district. Not a neighbour. Not one of the sixteen households whose levy is in it. They have all read the notice about the four wells in the low quarter, which is on that door, in a green frame, with sixty pounds ten shillings on it, and it is a much more interesting thing to stand and look at than a cooperative's account of its own mistakes, and I have been standing next to that book for the best part of a month saying *anybody* and meaning it, and nobody wanted it.

"That is not a failure of the book. That is the book working. It was never going to be a sword. It is a thing that is there to be looked at, and a sword that nobody will pick up is worth what a notice about four wells is worth, which is nothing.

"And I want it in the minutes, if there are minutes, and I am aware there are not minutes, so I want it in the log, in somebody's hand, with the hour, because in six weeks a man in a room is going to describe this cooperative as a body that published its own errors and I would like somebody to be able to say *yes, and nobody read them*."

---

Nobody argued with her and Marek did not say thank you, because she would not have wanted it and because she had read her own entry out in a yard once already this month and had had to be talked into it.

He said the last thing at about four o'clock and he said it standing up, with his hands flat on the bench, to six people who had heard every number in it before.

"I want it entered, and I want Sera Quill's name on it, and I want it in the cooperative's book and not only in the log, and I am going to say it slowly so that nobody has to stop me.

"The account this cooperative holds of the cause of the damage in the low quarter of Fennmere is a rumour with a witness in it. It is not proof. It is not evidence. It is four unlicensed people with a pail and a watch and a miller's chalk and a licensed engineer who will not say what she read. There is one licensed reading on this node and it says *below* and *not yours* and it is the district's, not ours, and the district is going to use it against us and is right to.

"That is the truth about what we know, and we have known it since the fourth of last month, and we have not written it, and we are going to write it now, in our own hand, on our own page, with the hour on it, and we are going to send it to Fennmere with the form inside fourteen days, attached, where a clerk will read it and a hearing officer will read it in a cold room and a man with a satchel can produce it.

"I am not doing it because I think it will help us. I do not think it will help us. I am doing it because a thing that is entered cannot be lost, and every one of the last three days has been a day on which a document that existed in this yard could have been destroyed by one man with a bad morning and a pencil."

Sera Quill did not say no, and she did not say it was a good idea, and she capped her ink.

"I want three words changed before you write it," she said.

"Name them."

"*Can be lost* is wrong. A document can be lost. What you mean is that a thing that is entered can be denied. I have watched four hundred students learn that lesson in one afternoon and I have watched about nine of them learn it properly.

"And I want it to say who is entering it. Not the cooperative. You. In your own name, under the date and the hour, as the engineer of record, with my name underneath mine and Ms Senn's under hers, and I want it read aloud before it is signed, because I have watched this building learn a habit this month and I am not going to be the person who lets it lapse in the one month it matters."

"And the third thing."

"You are going to want to write that the book told you to do it," Sera Quill said. "I know what you are going to want to write, because you have been carrying it since Tuesday and you have told four people in a room that a book said *not yours*. You are not writing it. Not on that page, not in a letter, not in a representation, not in a room, ever, and if it is in a piece of paper anywhere I will find it and take it out and say in the room that I took it out and why.

"That is not a rule I am inventing for you. It is a rule I invented for myself, nineteen years ago, for a document in a different office, and I have never told anybody what it was about and I am not going to tell you, and it is a different one from the other three and I have not given it a number.

"A book is an instrument. An instrument that has told a licensed reader *not yours* about a piece of living wood has been written to, or is reading a wound. Either way it is not a person and it does not get to be a reason. If you put it in a document, some man in six weeks will read it and believe you or not believe you, and everything I have signed in thirty years will come out of the file at the same time.

"You are going to have to carry it as a thing you do not know, in a building full of people who will help you carry it, and that is the job and there is no other job. I am sorry. You will not be able to use it. Write that on the page too. I will sign under it."

Marek wrote it. It went in at the ninth hour with the date, and Anneke Roos read the whole of it aloud first, and Sera Quill signed under her own name and Gael Senn signed third, and Dunmar Fitch entered the page number and the hour, and Lissa Vale signed the witness column at the foot because that is what she does.

It was eleven hundred words and it was in the book by half past four and it went out with the form on the twenty-eighth's instruction inside fourteen days, and it was the last thing this cooperative entered on that node in that month.

---

Sera Quill caught him at the pump shed door at the seventh hour, on her way out, and turned his arm up into the light without asking, the way she had on the eighth, and the twelfth, and the twentieth, and looked at the inside of it from the wrist to the elbow for a count of about four.

"Nothing," she said.

"Nothing."

"Still nothing," she said, and let go of his arm, and then did not say the thing about twice a week that she had said three times and had promised not to say a fourth.

She stood there in the cold with her case under her arm and looked at the well house for a moment.

"One more, and then I am going up the road, and I am saying it to you and not to them, because they have their own and you are going to carry mine. That reading is mine. I took it at the seam, in front of four people, at the hour, and I will be examined about it before you are examined about anything else, and it is going to cost me the license I have got the rest of. And I will not have you say so anywhere. If a document anywhere says that a licensed engineer warned this cooperative and it went ahead, that document is about me and it is being used by me, and I will be the only person in this fen who can say that I did it on purpose. I would rather be cross-examined on a thing I did than on a thing somebody did to you.

"That is not a defence. Do not let anybody use it as one, including me."

Then she went out through the gate with her case, and the gate was shut by two men at half past seven, and the fen went on being flat and enormous and silent in the way a fen is silent when a front is coming up out of the west.

---

He walked out to the field at about nine.

The ditch at the head of it was wet the whole thirty-one yards, in the dark, with a line of standing water in it you could see because of the sky. The four willow sets were in it, green, holding their lashings, and the stake was in the bank at the four o'clock side with the date and the length and the four names and one line about an anchor that was supposed to be alive in February.

It was alive. That was the point of an anchor. Whatever else had gone in this month, the willow had taken, and it was off the south embankment windbreak, and it would be a hedge there in nine years, and it was green in the first hard week of the month.

The nineteen acres below the ditch head were standing. The heads were still white. There was nothing in Tarrow Fen to eat them and no sheep and no cart and a winter coming.

The three acres at the top end were the colour of a wet road and the crust came up in one piece where he put his boot into it, and the four hundred and ten yards of ditch he had dug by hand across three autumns were a place a beast would put a leg in, and there were forty ewes going on that holding by the third season after this one or there would not be anything.

Marek stood at the four o'clock corner of a ditch he had made, with a dead branch two hundred yards behind him up the fen road and a dead bed nine miles off under a low quarter, and a notice nailed to a well house door with sixty pounds ten shillings and fourteen days on it, and a document in Fennmere with his own name at the top of it saying that everything he knew was a rumour with a witness in it.

He put his hand on the willow for about a second and then took it off, because he was the engineer of record and there was nobody licensed standing next to him, and he had not asked to be in that ditch on the first of the month and he was not going to start on the thirtieth.
