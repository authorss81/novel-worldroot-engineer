# Chapter 24

## Three Hundred And Ninety-Nine

It came out of Tova Reed's own arithmetic, on the twenty-fourth, at the north annex, with the tray of the Longneck's own empty space in front of her and four people standing in a seed house before eight in the morning.

She had asked for the license sheet and Dunmar Fitch had brought it, and she read it standing up in the way she read everything, and she got to the return line and stopped and read it again.

*Return: labor, recorded. Compost into the return main, one hundredweight, the twenty-first, G. Senn. Against a specified four hundred hundredweight a month at the load this node is being asked to carry. Shortfall: three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight a month.*

"Three hundred and ninety-nine," she said. "At four shillings that is seventy-nine pounds sixteen, and the specification is eighty, and the cooperative has four pounds and five shillings, and those three numbers have been in this building since the tenth of last month and until this morning they have not been in the same sentence."

Nobody argued with the arithmetic. There was nothing to argue with.

Then she put her thumb on the word *labor* and asked for the labor column out of the standing pressure log, and Dunmar read it, and it was the mill floor, twice a day, under sacking, two names, since the fourteenth.

"Show me the mill floor," said Tova Reed.

They walked out to it. It is a stone floor at the back of the seed house, four feet by nine, with a firebox under it and a door to the yard and a bin beside it, and the trays go onto it twice a day and come off it twice a day, and it has been the cold end of the bed for eleven years.

Lissa Vale opened the bin.

"Do you know what goes in there," said Tova Reed.

"Everything that comes off this holding," said Lissa Vale. "Manure, sweepings, the two loads of spent bark off the Longneck trays, the ash off the annex fire, whatever the boys bring in. It is what it has always been."

"Who brought it in before the tenth of last month."

Lissa Vale stood with the lid of the bin in her hand and worked it out, standing up, with four people waiting.

"The flock," she said. "The pasture flock, off the north pasture, twice a week, from the middle of September, since I was a girl. It went up the fen road on the tenth with a dog and a thing walking beside it and it has not come back and not one animal has come back, and that is where the manure came from, and there is no other stock on this holding but four cows and two horses and the cows are in the byre on straw and the horses are in the yard and neither of them has ever made a bin's worth in a year."

"How much of the bin was the flock."

"Most of it," said Lissa Vale.

"How much now."

"There is nothing in it," said Lissa Vale. "There has been nothing in it since the middle of October. I have been putting sweepings in and the ash off the annex fire and the two loads of spent bark, and that is the whole of what has gone in that bin for four weeks, and the bed has been going down a degree every nine or ten days since, and I have been putting the trays higher up the shelf and the higher I put them the colder it is at the bottom, and I did not write any of it down because there was nothing to write down. I was cold."

Tova Reed had the bin lid in her hand then, and she handed it back, and she did not make a speech, and Marek understood afterwards that she had made the most economical discovery in the month.

"Mr Vale," she said. "You have written a return on a piece of paper. I have written one in my own hand and ours is the only one in this yard that goes all the way round."

"labor, twice a day, on the mill floor. The mill floor warms the bed. The bed is kept by what goes in the bin. What goes in the bin came off a hundred and thirty head of sheep, and there are no sheep, and the sheep went up the fen road on the tenth because of a thing walking beside them, and the same water that took the compost out of this building is the water that has taken the return out of the node."

"So there is one gap and not two, and it has been in the books since the third of last month and nobody put the two ends of it together because we were all looking at a wound. Every return on this holding goes through that bin. The certificate's monthly examination is a return that goes through that bin. The new run's return is a return that goes through that bin. There is one hole in the middle of it and the hole is the tenth of October and it is a hundred and thirty animals' worth, and it is four hundred hundredweight a month, and there is no way to fill it from anything that walks."

Ilsa Kern had been leaning on the door frame with her arms folded and she took them apart.

"There is nothing to put in it," she said. "That is not a difficulty. I want to say it this morning so that nobody spends the rest of the month calling it a difficulty. There is nothing to put in it. I said the same thing in a flooded pit at dawn on the tenth of this season and I said it to nobody and I have been carrying it for fourteen days."

---

Dunmar Fitch read the license sheet out loud in full at the ninth hour, standing up, at the seed house table, the way a clerk reads a thing he did not write and is going to be asked about later.

It was a single page. It named the work, the four hands, the licensed engineer of record, the anchor, the path, and the return. And at the bottom of it, under three ruled lines and above a signature, Sera Quill had written:

*I license the above work. I have entered the shortfall in the return against my own hand and I have not entered a cause. I am aware that a licensed graft requires a return sufficient to it and that the return named here is not sufficient to it. This node holds no charter. This document cannot be entered as a charter condition and is not offered as one. It is a record of a licensed act and of the arithmetic of the exchange at the date of the act, and both of those are true, and they are on the same page because they are the same act.*

*S. Quill.*

And under it, in her own hand again, because Marek had asked her to and she had done it without discussion, *M. Vale, engineer of record, and the author of the number in the return line.*

Four names read the sheet. The fifth — Gael Senn — was in the well house, because it was the first hour and she was on the pit with the pit fire and the mill boy carrying water.

Nobody put a vote on it and there was no vote available, because a vote needs a charter to vote under and there was no charter, and the sheet went into the cooperative's book with the page number and the hour and was not filed anywhere.

---

He asked for the fieldbook at twenty past nine at his mother's table with three people in the room, because there were three people in the room, and that was going to be the number for the rest of the month.

Lissa Vale said no.

She said it once, instantly, the way she said the first one on the twentieth of last month, and she stopped, and she did not say it a second time, and Marek stood at the table and understood that something had changed and could not work out for about a minute what it was.

"I said no once," she said. "On the twentieth I said no twice and then I made you say what you meant. I have thought about that all week and I have decided it was a way of being right twice instead of being right once, and I am not going to do it again, and you are going to have to get used to a woman who trusts you a little more than she did on the twentieth, and I would like you to notice that it is a very small amount more and that it is not the same as the other thing, because the other thing is not happening this year."

The use-log line was written out in the seven-column form and read aloud before it was signed, which was Anneke Roos's habit and had become the room's habit.

*Twenty-fourth day, ninth hour and twenty minutes. Fieldbook, produced by Mrs Vale from the press, at the request of M. Vale, at the seed house table, in the presence of T. Reed, A. Roos and D. Fitch. Purpose: to be used by Sera Quill, licensed engineer, at the copper seam of the well head, for one reading on this day, and for no other purpose. Licensed person present: S. Quill. M. Vale will not touch the instrument and will stand outside the housing. Out. Back.*

Lissa capped the ink and went back to her bin, and did not watch him carry it out.

---

Sera Quill was on the boards in the housing at half past ten with the copper clip fitted and the book open on her knees, and Marek on the step outside the collar with his hands in his pockets, and four people standing where they could see her and not her knees: Ilsa Kern, Anneke Roos, Jonas Byre and Gael Senn, with Tova Reed behind them on the boards with her arms folded and her eyes shut, listening.

She read it out. She always read it out, because a reading taken in a pit is not a reading until somebody else has heard it, and she had said so on the twentieth and had entered that she had said so.

> The new run is fed.  
> The new run is fed from below.  
> Below is not yours.

Nobody said anything at all for about six seconds, and the pump was on the idle and the beat came up once through the boards, one about every four seconds, out of the sump and below the sump.

"I want it entered that I read it before the choice and not after it," Sera Quill said, "and I want it entered that the choice was not mine. And I want the four of you who are standing there to have heard it, and I want that in the book too, with the hour."

She put two fingers on the page.

"Now I am going to tell you the three things I know about it and I am not going to tell you the fourth one."

"A fieldbook reads what a node has recently had done to it. That is all it is. It is a poor instrument, it reads a short way back, it reads a *sequence* of work and not an opinion, and it will give you a procedure that has been performed on a piece of living wood and it will give it to you in the order it was performed. On the eighth of last month it gave a man a list of maintenance on a well. On the twentieth it gave me three words that are the order the Weir Row branch is in. It is not a mind and it is not a voice and it has never once in my life answered a question I asked it."

"What I have not seen in thirty years is the third line. Not once. I have had a pressure, I have had a direction, I have had a direction that was wrong, I have had a shaft and a pair of hands, and I have had four hundred sequences of ordinary work, and I have never had a book say *not yours* to me, in those words, about anything."

"And I cannot tell you whether that is because this node has recently had something done to it that is *below* your new run and is not the cooperative's — or because something has been written into that wood that is telling you what to do. Those are two different sentences and they are both the same three lines, and I am not going to choose between them in a yard in front of a pit, and I am not going to be the woman who chose."

Gael Senn said, very quietly, "That is an instruction."

"That may be an instruction," said Sera Quill. "Write it down as *three lines read at the seam, tenth hour, twenty-fourth, S. Quill, licensed, with four witnesses present*, and write the three lines under it exactly as I read them, and do not write *the book told us to* anywhere on that page, because the day somebody reads that page in a room is the day everything anybody has said in this yard goes under a blanket."

Marek came in off the boards and stood at the rail.

"Run it or close it," Anneke Roos said. "That is the choice and it has been the choice since Saturday and you have been not making it for three days because every hour you do not make it the field is a day older."

Marek looked at the three lines in the licensed engineer's hand, and then at nineteen acres of standing rye, and then at a bin with a lid on it and nothing in it.

"I am not keeping that run open because a book told me to," he said. "I want that in the record, because it is the true thing and because it is the other thing that gets quoted."

"I am keeping it open because nineteen acres of ground on this holding are standing up this week for the first time in three seasons, and because I have been in this fen since I was nineteen looking for a way to do that without taking it off somebody, and I have not found one, and I did not find one this week either, and I did not look for one this week because I was in a ditch on Saturday and I have been elated ever since, and I am not going to stand in a yard and pretend to have been frightened for three days."

"So the reason is in the field and the reason is not in the book, and the two reasons are going in the book side by side, and neither of them is going to be usable without the other one."

He said it out loud and then he said it in writing, in the standing pressure log, at the eleventh hour and twenty minutes, in his own hand, and Anneke Roos read the whole of it back before he signed it, and Sera Quill initialled the licensed column, and the five of them were witnesses to each other's signatures on a page that said, in effect, that the engineer of record knew something was wrong and did it anyway, and had written down that he knew.

Then the pump was run up to full draw at the twelfth hour, and it held at eleven hundred, and the head did not go soft, and the beat came up at the twenty-second hour in runs of nine, and Gael Senn counted it and did not name it, and Sera Quill wrote *cause not established* and initialled it and closed the book and put the copper clip off.

---

Rowan Teale's boy came up the fen road again at the seventh hour that evening with a second number and no bun.

The letter was three lines.

*Tuesday, the twenty-fourth. Wheel, 231, milling day. Down twenty-three on Monday. I have written both and I have put my hand on the chalk both times and I have not written why, because I do not know why and I have been reading this board for thirty-one years and I have never seen it go down in the middle of a week with the water coming up.*

*Rowan Teale.*

Marek read it and then went out and stood on the boards over the pit in the dark with a lamp and a reed gauge he had not read properly since the third of the season, and the reed was on about the peg, exactly as Jonas Byre had left it, and the beat went through the boards once every four seconds out of the sump and below the sump, and nothing in Tarrow Fen had a name for it and he had written *cause not established* five times in a month now and was going to write it a sixth before the month was out.
