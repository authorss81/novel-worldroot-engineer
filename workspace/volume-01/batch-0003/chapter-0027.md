# Chapter 27

## A Third Face

He said it in the yard at half past five in the morning with the lamp still in, and he said it once, and he did not make a speech about it, because he had written a bypass in the margin of the cooperative's book on the eleventh day of last month and another margin note about the twenty-seventh on the seventeenth, and the note on the eleventh had a figure against it and no money, and now the thing on the twenty-seventh was a day old and there was going to be a die on a cart at eight o'clock.

"A bypass," Marek said. "A new run from above the split, from the head side, down past the top of the branch and into the bed below Weir Row. Thirty or forty yards of ditch and a crossing and four more willow sets and a licensed hand to cut it in. It would put water back into that bed in a week, and the bed is the outlet for the gravel under forty-one houses, and if the bed takes, the gravel turns over, and the head comes back into Mr Slee's well in a fortnight."

He put the lamp on the well house rail.

"I am not asking anybody to go in the water. I am asking for the ditch, the withies, the licence, and the money, and I do not have one of the four and I would like to know which of them one of you can get me before the cart is at the top of the fen road."

The four of them did not answer at once, and that was the answer, and he let it sit for about four seconds, and then he asked them one at a time, because a refusal in a yard that is a chorus is worth nothing in a book.

"Ms Quill."

"No," said Sera Quill. "I licensed one piece of work this month on this node and the return line on that document has three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight of a hole in it in my own hand, and I will not put a second licence on the same node in the same month while the first one is unfed. That is not caution and I would like it recorded that it is not caution. It is arithmetic. A second work on a system that is already short does not make good the short; it makes the short twice as big and puts both halves under one signature, and the signature is mine, and in about six weeks somebody is going to ask me in a room which of two things I was thinking of on the twenty-first, and I will not be able to answer, because you are all four standing here with your faces on and I will not."

"Mrs Roos."

"The money and the paper, and I will do it in that order because I am a tenant and I have a head." Anneke Roos had her arms folded and she had been not folding them for two days. "The money is four pounds and five, and I have seen the box because I read everything in this building out loud. A ditch is a hundredweight of clay and forty rods of withy and three days of five people and it is the smallest part of it. The paper is the part that kills it. There is no contractor in this district who will come up this road without a charter to hang the work on, and there is no charter, and if a man did come he would lay it Crown-fashion and it would read Crown-fashion on any form anybody chooses to put in front of a hearing, and that is the second document with a Crown shape on it in the same month, and Ms Quill already has one of those in a district file in her own name.

"You are not asking for a ditch," she said. "You are asking for the last thing in Tarrow Fen that nobody in this yard has been able to get anybody to give us for free, and it is not going to arrive on a cart for free, and we have said no to the only two men in this county who have offered."

"Miss Senn."

Gael Senn had her hand flat on the pump shed door and she did not take it off.

"I would do it," she said. "That is the difficulty and I want it in the book in those words. I would like to do it more than I have ever wanted anything, and I have been in a pit twice this season and been trusted with a rule and a name and a certificate, and I have thought about a bypass every day since Saturday. And that is exactly why not, because I am twenty-three and I want it, and wanting a thing is the whole of what is wrong with a young hand, and if I am the one who says yes to this I will say yes to the next one as well, and somebody with a district badge is going to write *Gael Senn, unlicensed, in a live conduit* on a piece of paper one day and it will be true and I will not have been able to argue with it."

"Mr Byre."

"I will not go down four feet," said Jonas Byre.

He did not say anything else and nobody expected him to and nobody thanked him, because it was the second time he had said it and it was going to be the third.

Marek wrote it in the working notes at the sixth hour with the hour, in the order they had said it, with their names against it, and read it back, and Sera Quill initialled the licensed column and did not soften one word of it, and then he went into the seed house and got Dunmar Fitch out of bed for it, and Dunmar Fitch came in his coat and put his spectacles on and ruled a line in the margin of the cooperative book and gave him the pen.

*Twenty-seventh, sixth hour. A bypass of the branch at Weir Row, a new run from the head side into the bed below the cut, thirty to forty yards, proposed by M. Vale, engineer of record, and refused by S. Quill (licensed engineer, on the ground that a second licence this month on a node whose first licence is unfed makes the shortfall twice and carries one signature), by A. Roos (tenant, on the ground of no contractor without a charter and no money, and that a Crown-format repair would put a second Crown-shaped document in the file in the same month), by G. Senn (lamp-keeper, on the ground that she wants it and wanting it is the objection), and by J. Byre (tenant, on the ground that he will not go down four feet). No contractor, no licence, no money. The plan stands as a plan. It is not a filing. It is evidence of what this cooperative intended and not of what it did, and nobody has signed it but me.*

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*

Dunmar entered the page number and the hour and the fact that the entry had been made by the engineer of record and by nobody else, and then he said, without looking up, "That is three in a month, all of them the same instrument, all of them the same page of the same book, and in six years nobody has ever been able to say this cooperative wrote down what it wanted and did not get it." And then he said, "Go to your cut, Mr Vale. You will be late and the cutting does not wait for a clerk."

---

They were on the crown at eight o'clock and the cart came at nine.

It came up the fen road with a man on the near horse and a man walking, and a handcart behind them, and the handcart had a die in it under an oilcloth, and the die was about the size of a fist and looked like a thing for making biscuits, and Marek had seen one seventeen days before and again eleven years ago, when he was eighteen and had watched his father's crew stamp a face on a branch and had not understood for another three years that stamping was the part that mattered.

He did not go to the cart. There was nothing to say to a handcart.

The two of them got a chain saw out and a mallet and a wedge and a folding rule, and one of them produced a schedule sheet on a board with a clip, and read the item number out loud on the crown, once, the way a man reads a number off a gate.

"N two two one four, slash K. Yes. That is the one."

He was about fifty and he was not unkind and he was in a hurry in the way that a man is in a hurry when there is a list and nine on it.

"Mr Rusk's man said you would be up," he said, to Marek, pleasantly. "You are the co-operative, are you? You are not on the list. You are welcome to stand there. Do not stand in the fall."

"I am not on the list," Marek said.

"No, sir."

The man with the board did not stop reading. The other one looked at the branch for a while the way a man looks at a job, and then he said, to nobody:

"There is a hold on this one."

"Released Monday," said the man with the board, without looking up. "It is on the job sheet."

"I am not asking to see the job sheet."

"You are welcome to see the job sheet."

"I am not on the list," Marek said again, and the man with the mallet said, "No, sir," and that was the entire exchange between the Lantern Reach Work Cooperative and the district works schedule on the morning of the twenty-seventh of the month, and it took about four minutes.

Then the saw went in.

Marek stood twenty feet off with his notebook out and his hand on the page, and he did not have to guess where it would land and he had known since the seventeenth of last month and had written a measured drawing with a scale bar and a lamp-keeper's witness signature to prove that he knew.

The specification put a fresh face a hand's width below the living collar, at the bottom of any healed or opened ground, clean wood, square to the run of the branch.

The bottom of the three feet of opening was clean wood and it was square to the run of the branch, and the face came off at the mark like a slice off a loaf, and the round of it went over the berm and into the old drainage channel with a noise that made the drover's dog start somewhere two miles away.

"Mark it," Marek said, to nobody, and Gael Senn said, "I have it," and she was already down on one knee on the crown with the notebook and the stub of pencil and the time.

The stamping took eleven minutes. The die went in on a hardwood block with three blows from a short maul, one for the letter, one for the number, one for the cycle mark, and it came up bright and it was **N-2214/K**, and it was the third time in a decade that the same three letters had been cut into the same branch, and it was cut in a hand nobody in the fen had ever had to read.

Then they packed the sawdust into the channel and put the chain on the saw and got on the cart and one of them said, to Marek, on the way past, not unkindly at all:

"You are welcome to look at the face, sir, if you want to. It is a good clean face. Somebody did us a favor by opening that seam, you know. Took all the dead wood off. You would not have got that off with a chain."

And then they were gone up the fen road at about ten minutes to eleven and the cart wheels went through the standing water and the sound of them went away for a long time.

---

Marek went to the open seam at the fourth hour of the day, with a lamp, on his hands, and put his ear six inches off the wood, and it was not hissing any more.

For seventeen days it had been hissing. He had heard it through the boards of the well house and along the crown in the dark and had put it in the working notes as *a steady loss at the opened seam, continuous, unchanged* and had used the word *unchanged* four times because it had been true four times, and because a man who is going to be asked in six weeks whether the bleed in that seam was steady is a man who wants four words that say it was.

It was quiet.

He put his palm flat on the scarwood on either side of the opening and there was nothing at all in it. No vibration, no pressure, no warmth, no iron on his fingers. The wood was the temperature of the air and it had the mineral bloom of a thing that has been open to the weather for a week, and the water in the channel below the cut was brown and moving at the speed of a ditch, which was the speed of the fen coming up out of it, and the fen was up because the branch was cut, and the branch being cut meant that everything that had been standing in that column for a hundred years had just gone out onto the ground in one morning and gone down the fen.

Somewhere about nine miles down that fen, in a low quarter of forty-one houses, the water in the ground had about four inches of head left in it, and four inches on a fen gravel under forty-one houses is about a month of drinking water and not one day more.

He sat on the crown of the embankment with the lamp beside him and did the thing he had come out here to do, which was write it down, and he wrote it slowly, because it was the only entry in that book that nobody would ever want to make and it had to be right.

*Twenty-seventh, second hour of the day, at the branch. Execution of N-2214/K, third face, cut to specification at the bottom of the opened ground. Face stamped N-2214/K. Two men and a handcart and a die, from the district, without notice, on a branch for which this cooperative is not the holder and has no standing. The face is marked and the position is as on my drawing of the ninth, and the drawing is in the working notes with a witness signature, and the drawing is a record of where the face came and not of whether it should have come.

*The bleed from the opened seam, continuous for seventeen days and entered as unchanged four times, had stopped before the second hour of this day. It is not reduced. It is not. Cause not established.*

He read the whole of it back to the four of them standing on the crown, out loud, the way it had to be read, and then he said the last part, and he said it to the embankment and not to them.

"Write the first word of the last line the way I have written it, and do not let anybody change it, and do not let me change it either. It has stopped. If I write *reduced* then in six weeks a man in a room will come with a document that says it reduced, and a reduction is a thing that happens to a wound, and this is not a thing that happened to a wound. In six weeks somebody is going to ask that man what made it stop, and I do not know, and neither does the district, and the only honest sentence anybody in Tarrow Fen can put in a book about that is the one I have just written."

Gael Senn wrote it out in her own hand and signed it and read it again, and Jonas Byre witnessed the hour, and Anneke Roos witnessed the hour, and Ilsa Kern did not sign anything and stood on the crown with her hand on her hip looking up the fen road the way a woman looks at a road when the thing she was afraid of has gone past and come back.

They walked down off the crown at half past two.

Halfway down the bank, above the ninth yard of the channel, in the exact place where the water was now moving instead of standing, there was a dog.

An old black-and-white collie with one ear gone, standing in a channel that had water in it for the first time in five years, with its feet in it, not moving, not herding anything at all, because there was nothing on the line to move.

It was a different animal. It was older, and it was in a different place, and it was not doing the thing the other one had done on a crown in a storm with a hundred and thirty head of sheep behind it.

It stood in the moving water for about a minute and a half, with the whole of its weight on the channel and its head low, and then it turned off the line, out of the water, up the bank, and went away into the reed on the high side — away from where the water was going, and not toward it, which was what Marek had been told in a seed house in a storm by a woman who had not said the word in thirty years, and which he had not believed, and which he had watched for seventeen days from a board on the far side of a yard.

It was the same dog. It was nine miles away on Thursday morning in a wet gravel bed under a boy's hand, and it was here at half past two on Friday in nine inches of moving water, and Marek stood in the ditch and did not move for a while, because his mother had said the word in a seed house in a storm seventeen days ago and had said that the only thing that moves it is a change in where the water goes, and he had not believed her, and there is water in that channel this morning for the first time in five years.

Then he said, to the four of them, in a completely ordinary voice, as a man stating a fact about a piece of ground:

"Well. There is your change in where the water goes."
