# Chapter 41

## The First Figure On The Peg

They went down to the eleventh rod at the fourth hour in the dark with one lamp between the three of them, because a fourth person holding a lamp in a wet ditch is a man standing in the water holding a lamp.

The gauge was where it had been since the twenty-first of last month: a length of cheap reed, a foot and a bit, let into a strip of tin, screwed to the ferrule of the return main at a point about waist high, with a brass mark scratched into the tin at the bottom. Jonas Byre had put it on himself, in a coat, in a storm, because it was four shillings and the district's own gauge was in a box in Fennmere and nobody in Tarrow Fen could produce it on demand.

"You read it," Marek said. "You put it on. I am not going to stand over your shoulder at four in the morning and have you think I doubt the number before you have read it."

"I have read it once," said Jonas Byre. "On the morning I put it on. And I did not write it down."

"Then read it and write it down."

Gael Senn got the lamp onto the mark and held it there while Jonas Byre crouched, and the three of them stood about eleven seconds looking at a thing that had been in a ditch for twenty days.

"A quarter inch," said Jonas Byre. "Just under a quarter. Call it three-sixteenths and be honest about the light."

"How high is the mark off the sill of the main."

"Nobody has ever measured that either."

"Then measure it," said Marek, "and write down the number, and then we have two numbers instead of none, and the second one is a thing we did this morning."

---

They wrote it at the fifth hour in the seed house, and the writing is where the whole of the last month comes to a head, because there were two books on that table and only one of them could take a page.

The standing pressure log could not. It had been certified on the seventh at the fourth hour of the afternoon, in a clerk's square hand, *so far as it goes*, and a book that a district clerk has certified can never have a page put into it again by anybody but a district officer in the room. Perrin Osse had said so at a counter and had not softened it, and the words had been read back into this cooperative's own book in Lissa Vale's hand on the seventh, and Lissa Vale had written the five words out in her own book and signed the line and stood there while the ink dried.

So the standing log has nothing in it after the seventh of the month. Not because the log closed. Because it cannot be added to.

"Everything from today goes in ours," said Dunmar Fitch, from the end of the table, where he had been listening for a quarter of an hour without moving. "I have been the clerk of this body for nineteen years and I want it said out loud in a room once that everything this body has done since the seventh of the month is written in a book a clerk can look at and cannot certify, and that is what a body with no charter is, and there is no way round it and no fee for it."

He ruled a line in the cooperative's own book and put the hour on it, and Marek read the entry back and signed it and Lissa Vale entered the page.

*Eleventh day, fourth hour, to the fifth hour. Reed gauge, cheap, at the eleventh rod of the return main, clamped on the twenty-first day of last month and not read into any book since. First figure taken: three-sixteenths of an inch above the mark, pump on the idle. Mark measured from the sill of the main this morning for the first time: nine and a quarter inches. No earlier reading exists. The one entry concerning this gauge made at the time states in its own words that the reading for that morning cannot be supplied, and it stands. The standing pressure log cannot be added to without a district officer in the room, and this entry is made in the cooperative's own book, which is not a filing.*

"No load under it," said Marek. "Write the load. Pump on the idle. And then say what would be in the book if the load stated had been applied, and say that it has not been."

"It has not been," said Gael Senn. "I have the certificate in the press and I have read it eleven times this month and the load test is the twentieth day's act, on a certificate, and there is no charter to hang a second one on, and Ms Quill said on the sixth that she is not spending a second act on a head in that condition. So the number in this book is a number at an idle, and if anybody ever puts a full-draw figure beside it I want them to have to come to this page and read why it is not here."

She read the sentence back before she let him sign it, which she does.

---

The trouble with a first figure is that everybody in a yard wants it to be a beginning, and it is not one, and Marek said so out loud at the well house bench with the log open and the page turned round so that a stranger could read it, which is what he does with everything.

"Here is what we have," he said. "Three-sixteenths of an inch on a reed that cost four shillings, at a ditch, at the fourth hour, with the pump on the idle, on the eleventh of a month. That is the whole of it."

"And it is not a measurement of anything, because to be a measurement it wants two things and it has got one of them. It wants a figure from before, and there is none, and the one entry that exists about that morning says in its own words that the reading cannot be supplied. And it wants a load, and there is not one on it, and the reason is written on the same page, and the reason is not that a licensed woman is eleven miles away, because there is one in this yard as of two o'clock this afternoon. The reason is that the load test was the twentieth day's act, on a certificate, with three names on it, and there is no charter to hang a second one on."

"So I am not going to stand in a yard tomorrow morning and say the node is taking what the log says it takes, because I have got a reed and no baseline, and I have told this body that a number without a method is a thing somebody will use against me in a year, and that was the miller talking and he was right."

"Then what is it for," said Ilsa Kern, who had been standing at the well house door with a hook in her hand the whole time.

"It is for having it in a book on the day before," said Marek. "That is all. That is a smaller thing than I would like and it is the thing there is."

Anneke Roos put her thumb on the page.

"Then read that back to me in front of these four and initial it," she said, "because in six weeks a man in a room is going to say this body had an instrument on that main from the twenty-first of last month and used it for three weeks and a half, and the answer to that is a page that says in its own words that it was not used, and I want that page in this book and not in a speech."

He read it back and she initialled it, and the last line of the working note for the eleventh says, in Marek's own hand with the hour on it: *A number with no baseline and no load is not evidence and is not a claim. It is a mark, and the next figure will mean something and this one does not.*

---

Sera Quill came up the fen road at about two in the afternoon on the eleventh of the month on the four o'clock cart, with her case and nothing else, and she had not been asked and had not been told she was wanted, and Lissa Vale had put two loaves and a heel of bread on the table in front of her and entered all three in the annex book with the hour, because the rules are the rules and a person is a person and a person who has walked up a fen road in a flood warning is fed.

"I am here for the book," Sera Quill said, before she had her coat off. "Not the pit. I told the clerk on the third I was not going near that pit and I meant it, and I have come up this road to sit in a warm room and read a use log to a hearing officer's clerk on the twenty-first, and to answer for the reading I took on the twenty-fourth of last month, which is a thing I have never had to do in eleven years and which I am going to be examined about before Mr Vale is examined about anything else."

"Then you will be fed and you will sleep here and you will be at the sitting," said Lissa Vale, "and I am not going to argue with a woman about a pit in a doorway."

"I would not take it in a doorway," said Sera Quill. "I would take it in a yard, in daylight, in front of the people who have to live with it."

---

And in the middle of the afternoon, while the glass was still holding and the west was a color that had no business being there in the middle of a day, Ivo Tarrant came up the lane with four men behind him and a barrow of tools, and did not come to the gate.

He came to the gate. He stopped at it. He said four sentences, and then he turned the four men round and went back down the lane with them, and nobody in that yard said anything for about a minute afterwards.

"The window on the district's measure shuts tomorrow," he said. "The sixth to the twelfth. It is the twelfth tomorrow and I have read your form as well as your book, and the measure was never varied, and there is no new form, and there is a rain date on it. So whatever anybody in this yard was going to decide about a signature, it decided itself at midnight, and I would like it entered that I said so before anybody could say they had decided it."

Lissa Vale said, "Nobody in this yard has signed anything and nobody has refused anything, and both of those are going in the book with your name against the fact and not against a decision."

"I am not here about the measure," said Ivo Tarrant.

"I know what you are here about."

"Then you know I am not going to ask again," he said, "and you know why, and it is not pride. It is that four men who were refused on five correct grounds on the sixth of this month cannot turn up on the eleventh of this month and dig it, because the day we did it would be the day somebody wrote down that a cooperative which could not afford a bypass took free labor off a peat road in a flood, and that sentence would be true, and it would be in a district file in about six weeks, and it would be the last true thing anybody could say about you."

He looked at the yard, at the seed house, at the well house, at nineteen acres of white-headed standing rye with no sheep on it, and at the ditch at the head of the field with four willow sets in it, holding their lashings, green, on the eleventh of a month in a year when nothing had gone to the seed house since the middle of October.

"You have got a license woman," he said, "and I have never in my life known what a license woman is for, and I have found out in one afternoon standing at a gate, and I am not going to be the reason there is not one."

"Mr Tarrant," said Marek.

"No."

"You came up this lane and you have four men and a barrow of tools."

"I came up this lane to turn them round," said Ivo Tarrant, "which is the whole of what I came up this lane for, and I did it before anybody in this yard had to ask me to, and I would like somebody to write that down in a book, and I would like it written down by a person who will still be here in a year and not by a man on a peat road."

Anneke Roos wrote it in the cooperative's book at the fourth hour with the hour on it, and read it back, and initialled it, and Ivo Tarrant read it standing at the table with his hat in his hands and then went out through the gate and down the lane and did not take the bread, and the four men were not asked in and were not asked anything, and the bypass was not dug.

---

The last dry day went out at about four in the afternoon and it went out quickly, the way light does when there is a front behind it.

Gael Senn went down the ladder at five with the lamp on the hook and the rule in her coat, because the first of the month is ten days away and the certificate does not care about the weather and she wanted the figure in the book while the pump was on the idle and the light was going, and because a woman who has decided she will not write an entry in advance goes down the ladder twice as often as she needs to.

She lifted the sacking at the six o'clock corner, held the lamp into the gap, and did not touch the seal.

"Quarter of an inch," she said, from below. "It is a quarter of an inch, and I have measured it with my own rule, and it is a quarter of an inch, and it has not moved since the thirtieth day of last month and it is not a leak at any load anybody has applied, because nobody has applied one."

Gael Senn came up out of the pit with the lamp still in her hand and the rule in her coat and a piece of news.

"And the beat was there," she said. "Nine in the quarter hour, and four of them together at the end, and I cannot tell you where it was because I was on the ladder with both hands, and I have written the count and the hour and *cause not established*, and the licensed person present column says *none*, and I have written nothing underneath it, because I have been in this building since the sixth day of a month and I have worked out that there is nothing to write underneath it that would be a cause and only a guess, and a guess in a book with a name on it is a thing that gets read out in a room by a man who did not feel it."

Marek wrote the count down and did not improve it, and Lissa Vale entered the page and the hour, and nobody asked her to.

At the gate, in the last of the light, the west had gone the color of a wet slate and the barometer had gone down another tenth, and Marek stood at the low corner of the south embankment and looked at nine hundred yards of willow and gray elder with a hundred and six feet missing out of the middle of it because Ilsa Kern had cut it for withy in a month in October and entered it in a book with the hour on it.

Tomorrow the rain was due, three days of it, on the main drain, and the district had no instrument that would measure the low quarter nine miles down the fen and did not pretend to one, and the four men were walking down a peat road with a barrow of tools and would not be back, and the drain at the head of the field was already standing in its own water, and there was a number in a book for the first time in twenty days and no number at all to compare it with.
