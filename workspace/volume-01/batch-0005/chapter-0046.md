# Chapter 46

## A Work Order

The branch at Weir Row came up out of the water at about the fourth hour of the morning on the seventeenth with the mill boy's lamp on it, thirty or forty yards from the gate, and it was the best piece of work that night produced and none of it was planned.

The new cut had taken the hedge and a corner of a fence and gone into the old channel, and the water in it was making a noise like a mill race, which it was. And where the ground had gone out from under the crown in the night, the crown of the branch lay across the cut like a spar across a road, and the third face of it — cut to specification, square, at the bottom of the opened ground — was out of the water on one side and nine inches under it on the other, with a die-struck mark on it the size of a thumbnail.

Ilsa Kern said, "That is a branch the size of a scupper," and Marek went down into the cut to it, which was against everything Sera Quill had said to him on six separate days, and she was on the bank above him and did not stop him.

He put his right hand flat on the cut face and he got four seconds, and they are dearer than four seconds, because the second contact of this season cost him an hour he cannot account for and a handprint on a stone at a weir, and this one cost him the ground under his feet.

The branch itself told him nothing; a branch dead at the bed since the spring before last has nothing to say about water. The second thing was a sequence, and it was **cut, wait, cut**, at a scale that made the cut face under his palm about the size of a thumbnail. And the third thing, with the water past his knee and a girl of twenty-three shouting his name from a bank, was the *order of it*. Not a record of a cut. The arrangement a man works in when he means to bring a thing about rather than write down that it had happened.

He came up out of the cut sideways and went down on the bank on his hands and knees and was sick, and Ilsa Kern got his collar and turned him away from the water.

Sera Quill was on her knees beside him with a cloth and did not ask what he had got.

"You are not going to tell me," she said, "and I am not going to ask you, and that is the correct state of affairs and it will be the correct state of affairs for the rest of your life. Tell me when you can count to seven."

He counted to seven twice.

---

The four records came together at the low corner of the south embankment at about the seventh hour of the morning on the seventeenth, on a bench somebody had carried out of the well house and put down in the mud, and none of them sat down.

Lissa Vale had the fieldbook. She had gone into the seed house press at four in the morning, in the dark, taken the instrument out from under the ledger with the tin still on top of it, put it in the oilcloth, written the hour on the back of the use log and initialled it herself, and carried both out under her arm in the rain. She said nothing about it and nobody asked her to.

"Madam Vale," said Ansel Bray, from the bank above, "that instrument is ordered produced on the twenty-first on a form in front of an appointed officer, and the officer ruled on the eighth that nobody in her room may open it, including her. It is the seventeenth, and you have brought it to a bench in a flood."

"Yes," said Lissa Vale.

"Then I have to ask you to put it back, and I am asking as an officer and not as a man, and I would rather be the man who did not ask and I cannot be, because there is an application on a file with my name on it."

"Put the question in the record," said Lissa Vale, "and I will answer it, with the hour on the answer.

"The instrument is not being opened by an unlicensed hand, and it is not being opened in an officer's room, and the officer's ruling binds this office and her successor and me. It is being read by a licensed woman at a node whose head is failing, and not by a man on a bench.

"And the last thing, which is the only thing I am here for. On the tenth of last month, at the seventh hour and ten minutes, this instrument was in the district's hands for fifty minutes at a counter in Fennmere with no licensed person present, and the district holds the receipt, and I have never said in a document that the district and this cooperative were in those fifty minutes together. You may minute all of it, and you may stop the reading if you can put a rule behind it, and if you cannot, then I am going to say the thing I have carried up and down this fen for five weeks, which is that the district's claim is the parent of this document and not an attack on it. That was said on the eighth in your own office. It is not mine. Enter it twice."

"The use log comes to this bench entire and I am going to read every line of it out loud with the hour on it, and Mr Bray is going to hear it, and a district clerk is welcome to come and take a copy, and it goes into a file on the twenty-first, and I am going to read it there too. That is the second time and not the first, and I taught that officer what is in it on the eighth in her own room and she wrote it down."

Bray looked at her for about four seconds, then stepped down into the mud and took out a pencil and a wet notebook, which he had not done on any previous occasion in this season.

"I could not have stopped it," he said, "and I want the pencil out of the book. There is no holder on this node, the emergency clause is live, the three things are on the table, and to use them I would have to name a body last responsible, and the caution on your own door says in the district's hand that there is no holder. And in six weeks a man in Auberron who does not know me is going to ask me why a charter officer stood on a bank in a flood and did not stop this, and I am going to have to answer him with this morning and not with a form."

---

She read the use log entire. It took eleven minutes and it is two lines, because there have only ever been two lines in the whole of it. She read the first line, and then read the two hours on their own again, because the hours are the whole of it and a room does not hear a number inside a sentence. She read the second line, the entry of the fourteenth, and the entry of the sixth, which was a refusal to open it, entered with the reason. And then she said the one that was not in it, before anybody had looked for it.

"The first line of this book was made on the eleventh day of a month and describes the tenth, and the lateness is one day, and I entered it in an officer's room before anybody looked for it, and it is in the district's register in the same words and not in a summary, and I am not going to have it softened tonight either."

Then she undid the oilcloth on the bench, in the rain, and nobody opened it except Sera Quill, who put her own clip on the copper seam herself, tested the contact with a plain wire gauge, and wrote the reading down before she went on.

She read it, and it took her about eleven seconds, and then she said the thing.

"Three lines, the same three she read on the twenty-fourth of last month with four witnesses. Those three lines are already in a district register and I cannot get them out of it, and I am not going to say them again on a bench in a flood. What there is not going to be is a second copy carried about, or a document in this district or in that file that quotes them as a reason for anything, or a finding of mine or of Mr Vane's that quotes them as an instruction. An instrument that has told a licensed reader a thing about a piece of living wood has been written to or is reading a wound, and either way it is not a person and it does not get to be a reason.

"What I am going to put in this book is my name, the finding and the hour, and the finding is mine and not the instrument's.

"This was set, and not remembered. It is a pattern to keep to a season that has not come. And the keeping of it is the work, and nobody is coming."

Nobody said anything. The water came through the cut eleven feet away with a noise like a mill race.

"I am saying the rest of it because Mr Vale has his hand on a cut face at Weir Row this morning and I am not going to have him carry it into a room by himself.

"A node does not remember a season that has not happened. Wood does not hold a record of a thing nobody has done. What is in that bead, and what is in that cut face, is a *work order*. It is a shape set down in advance, in the order a man works in when he means to bring a thing about and not the order in which a thing happened to occur. It is a target pattern, and the word for a target pattern is not a prophecy. It is a specification.

"I have seen the structure of one before. I have not wanted to say so for twenty-three years and I am saying it now, on a bench, in the rain, with my licence on it, and I am not going to say where I saw it or what wrote it or why a pattern of that shape would be put into living wood at all. Put all three of those in the record as questions. I will answer them at a hearing and not on a bench.

"And Mr Vale cannot corroborate one word of it. He is not licensed, and what he got this morning is worth exactly what it is worth in front of anybody, which is nothing. That is the trade."

She wrote her name under it, and then put her name under the other three records, ruled a line, wrote the hour and the place, signed it, and gave the pen to nobody.

---

Kellan Rusk came up the fen road at about eleven in the morning on the seventeenth with a crew of six, a chain truck and his foreman, and he did not come into the yard. He stood at the gate in a coat that had been on him for two days and put his own log on the bench in the mud.

"I am not here to help you," he said. "I have come because the water on your ground is coming off my branch and my side draw. Argue about it if you want to. I would rather be argued with in a flood than in a room in six weeks."

Renn Tallow was behind him with a folded paper in his inside pocket and did not put it down for forty minutes, and when he did he put it face up on the bench.

*District works docket, one sheet, torn from a nail in a yard at Fennmere on the eighteenth of last month. Item N-2214/K. Tools drawn against the item: chain, block, dies, grafting set. Order date: not on the docket. Work description: none.*

"That is not the schedule," said Dunmar Fitch, "and I am saying so before anybody else does, because if it is left on a bench for two minutes somebody will call it the schedule. It is a sheet of paper that says a set of tools went out of a store against an item number on a day. It does not say what the tools were for. The district's table is still not produced and nobody in this fen is going to make a schedule appear this morning by wanting one."

And then they did the sum, because Marek would not let it go, and the water was up to the middle of the bench by the end of it.

The log, in the cooperative's own hand, six seasons, never audited: the head gives **eleven hundred turns a day**. The ground takes **seven hundred and twenty**. The difference is **three hundred and eighty**. Kellan's own log, six years, his own hand, weekly: his side draw **never above three hundred**.

"Eighty a day," said Marek. "Eighty turns a day that the log says went down a private branch and that the private branch's own book says did not. Eighty a day for six years is a hundred and seventy-five thousand two hundred turns that this body has carried in a book it cannot add to, and that a man has carried in a book he does not show anybody, and nobody has ever put the two on the same line of the same page.

"And the rest of it, because the figure is mine and I would rather it were said by me than found in six weeks. The return main as built is twenty-two rods of eight inch against a specification of forty-one of twelve, which is rather more than a third. The wheel on the return race counts the same water in turns, and the highest figure on Rowan Teale's board in thirty-one years is two hundred and sixty-one, and that was in the spring of the year my father went.

"And at the eleventh hour last night a mill boy of fifteen walked nine miles in the dark on a road that was under at the second gate and brought me the figure. Four hundred and nineteen, on a board that had been empty for six days, and the highest on it in thirty-one years, and the main it came through can pass about three hundred and sixty.

"So sixty came up by something that is not the return main, and I do not know what it came up by, and the reason I do not know is that the only instrument that has ever been on this head has been in a yard in Fennmere, above the split, in a book that is not a filing, on a gauge built to report what the node is giving and never where it goes."

He put his hand flat on the two books side by side.

"And the seed house's own ledger says the mill floor was fed twice a day for eleven years, and the bed fed it, and a hundred and thirty head went up this fen road on the tenth of October and have not come back. That ledger is in Lissa Vale's hand, it is dated, and it is the oldest book in this yard and the one document in this business nobody has ever asked a question about.

"Eleven years of twice a day, on a main that passes a third. I have been an engineer nine years and I am telling a bench, in a flood, that I do not know how that reconciles. Read it back before I sign."

Bray read it back in the mud, in the inspector's own voice and not as a summary, and then he asked the only question anybody asked that day that was about the future and not the past.

"What do you want me to do with it."

"Enter it. All of it, including the eighty, which is ours as well as his. And I am aware that a cooperative with eighty turns a day unaccounted for in its own book, and a second reader saying *below*, and a miller's chalk figure of four hundred and nineteen, is a file that does not need a hearing officer.

"And I would still rather it were in a book than in a man's head. It has cost me a field and it is going to cost me three acres tonight, and if I do not put it in a book then in four years the only place it exists is in a man who is ashamed of it, and a thing in a man dies with him and takes a fen with it."

Kellan Rusk said, "You have just made my book the district's exhibit and your own the worse of the two, and I have come eleven miles to hand you a log and not to be handed one, and I would like it noted that I came."

"It is noted," said Dunmar Fitch, and entered it, and read it back, and it is in the cooperative's book on the seventeenth of the month at the eleventh hour in a clerk's hand with the hour on it, and it is the only entry in that book all season that is against both parties.

And then Kellan Rusk said the thing he had come the second eleven miles to say, standing up, in the mud.

"You want the name that goes in the box."

"I did not know there was one."

"There is a name in the box," said Kellan Rusk, "and I have never seen the man and never seen a face that goes with it, and there has been a name in that box on every order I have had in nineteen years, and it is not his name, it is the name of the office that fills it. Schedule clerk, fen drains. The post has existed longer than I have and the man in it has changed four times that I know of, and the only thing I could tell you about him is that he does not come out. He signs the paper. The paper goes to a store. The store gives out the kit.

"And I have said that out loud for the first time in nineteen years, on a bench, in a flood, in front of a district inspector, to a cooperative that cannot pay me sixty pounds ten, and I want the officer on the twenty-first told that the last four words of it are the only four words in this business that no man in this district can check. I have not said them to be helpful. I have said them because if a thing is a shape and not a man, the shape is public, and if the shape is not public the man never has to answer for it."

Sera Quill heard it and said nothing.

She did not say nothing the way a woman says nothing in company. She said nothing the way she had said nothing on the third day of this season at a counter in Fennmere, and on the seventh, and on the twenty-seventh of last month in a yard, when she was asked by name and refused twice; and what she did not say was not a sentence she was holding back, it was a whole answer, and Lissa Vale wrote under Renn Tallow's docket, with the hour on it:

*Twenty-third hour. A name was given at the bench, and it is the name of an office and not of a man. It is entered in full. The licensed person present heard it and said nothing, and I am entering that she said nothing, and I am not entering why, because she has not told me, and because a woman who has said nothing three times in front of a clerk and once in front of her own book this season is not going to be asked a fourth time by a seed house.*

Nobody asked her a fourth time. And the name sat in four books in four places by the end of the month, and it had a shape and no man in it.

---

The figures from Idris Vane came into the Fennmere sub-office at about four in the afternoon of the eighteenth of the month, in duplicate, and the clerk stamped both and sent one up the fen road in a hand that was not the cooperative's and not the district's.

Fourteen pounds ten, received on the fifteenth, entered on the annex against the Dryfoot Trust. One day, not a day, on the fourteenth day of a notice served in person on the fourth. His own bead, his own clip, his own contact, his own date, told nothing. Three lines, in his own hand, unedited, under his own name, attested, and the same three lines, and a line at the foot of the copy that said the applicant's copy was not shown to him first.
