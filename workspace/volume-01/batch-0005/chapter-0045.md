# Chapter 45

## Thirty Or Forty Yards

The crest came up at about six in the morning on the sixteenth of the month and it came on the corner, and the whole of Tarrow Fen was doing one thing at the same hour, which is what a crest is.

The water at the low corner was four yards inside the bank at midnight and by six in the morning it was against the face, and it had stopped being water standing in a place and had become a head, about two feet, pushing, with the chalk under the toe making a noise like a man chewing.

Ilsa Kern was on it at ten past five because she had not gone to bed and had said so.

"The mark is on the slope and it is on the cut," she said, when Marek got there with a bar. "It is inside the bank now. It is not over. If it is over then it is in the field and the field is done and there is nothing anybody can say to that, and if we go now it is a decision, and if we wait it is a thing that happened to us, and I have spent nineteen years telling people the difference between those two and I would like it kept."

Marek went and told the seed house.

They took it at the table in about ninety seconds, and it was not a vote and no motion was moved, because a vote on it would have made it the body's loss and it was not the body's loss, and he had been told so by the only person in the room who had the standing to tell him.

"Right," Marek said. "I am flooding my three acres."

And then he said the rest of it, because the sentence was six words and the rest was not an excuse, it was a method.

"Ilsa is going to open the toe of the corner. It is a ditch nine feet long and four feet wide and four feet deep, cut through the toe and down through the chalk, and it is not a bypass and nobody is going to be able to call it one afterwards, because a bypass is thirty or forty yards at the head side into the bed below Weir Row and this is nine feet of ditch at a corner, and it is the oldest piece of engineering on this holding.

"It takes water off the bank and puts it into the top of the field. The top of the field is three acres of gravel over flints and it is two feet higher than the field and it is the only gravel on the place, and it will take about four hours' worth of this before it is full, and then the corner is no longer a corner, it is a hole, and everything that was going to be in the field is going to be in the gravel.

"And that is the whole of the design, and the whole of the risk, and I am going to write it down at the hour with the hour on it in the margin under section nine, and the margin note is evidence of what this body intended and not of what it did, and I want it read back to me before I sign it."

Ilsa Kern had the corner open by a quarter past eight.

---

Dunmar Fitch read it back and Marek signed it and it is the fifth of the five notes in the same book on the same pages, and it is the only one of the five that is not a plan.

*Sixteenth day, sixth hour. A cut nine feet long, four feet wide and four feet deep, at the toe of the low corner of the south embankment, to be cut by hand by the embankment foreman and four others on the morning of this day, for the purpose of taking water off the bank and putting it into the three acres at the top end of the outer field, which are the engineer's own ground and are not the cooperative's. It is not a bypass. A bypass is a new run of thirty or forty yards at the head side into the bed below Weir Row, and that is unbuilt and remains unbuilt, and four men offered it for nothing on the sixth of this month and were refused, and they came up the lane on the ninth and were not asked in, and on the eleventh their own man turned them round at the gate. The nine feet of ditch is not a new run and is not a return to any pipe. It is a hole in an embankment, and it is dug because a low corner cannot be held in a flood by anybody who is not a bigger district than this one, and because there is no contractor, no licence and no money, and because this season is nearly over.*

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*

Then, under it, in the same hand, four lines that have no precedent in the book.

*I am flooding three acres that are mine and that cannot be entered in this book as a loss to this body, and I am doing it on purpose, at the sixth hour of the morning, in daylight, in front of the people who live on this ground. The nineteen acres below the ditch head are the cooperative's and they are not being flooded. The bed in the seed house stands at three and a half degrees and is not being flooded. Those are the two things this cut is for, and I am writing them down in that order, and the three acres come last, and the three acres are mine.*

Anneke Roos read all of it back and initialled the first five lines and then said, in front of the room, that she was not initialling the last four, and why.

"I am not initialling an opinion about a man's ground," she said, "and if I initial that then a stranger in a room in six weeks can say that this cooperative's clerk and its witness stood behind a decision to flood three acres, and Mr Vale is not the cooperative and neither are his three acres, and I have been in this yard five weeks learning the difference between a thing that is recorded and a thing that is charged, and I am not going to cross that line at two o'clock in the afternoon in a flood to be kind to him."

"Enter that," said Dunmar Fitch, and entered it, and read it back.

---

The gravel took it.

That is the sentence Ilsa Kern used on the sixteenth and it is the sentence that decided the next two days, and she said it standing at the four o'clock corner of her own ditch with a bar in her hand and her coat off, looking at three acres that had been the colour of a wet road for a fortnight going brown and then going dark, in sheets, from the top down, in about three hours, with a sound like a man pouring meal out of a sack and stopping every so often to tip the crust off with the back of the hook.

"It is taking it," she said. "The gravel is taking it. It has not taken a cup of water since the spring before last and it is drinking this like a horse. Give it till four o'clock."

By four o'clock the water at the top of the field was standing in the three acres up to the top of the banks that Marek had dug by hand across three autumns, and the four hundred and ten yards of ditch were full and running, and running the right way, downhill, to the crack at the far side where a ditch of his had stopped being a ditch, and the old channel took it, and the old channel goes to Weir Row.

And the nineteen acres below the ditch head were wet at the head of the rows and dry at the bottom, and the seed house had water, and the pit had not risen a quarter of an inch.

"It is working," said Gael Senn, at about five o'clock, off the boards over the pit, to nobody in particular, and she was the only person in that yard who had a rule in her coat and a log under her arm and who was allowed to say it, and she wrote nothing for about twenty minutes because there was nothing to enter that a stranger could check.

---

The trouble came at about seven in the evening, and it came as a number.

"We have got the corner open and the field is taking it," said Marek, in the seed house, with the water coming up the yard's fourth yard for the first time since Tuesday, "and that was the design. Here is what the design did not have in it.

"That gravel took four hours. The corner is nine feet of ditch and it is taking about a third of what is coming at it, and the other two thirds is going over the top of the bank onto the field, and the field is clay in the middle and the three acres are gravel at the top end, and the clay is what is between me and the seed house.

"By tomorrow morning there will be about four inches of moving water in the middle of this holding between the corner and the bed, and the bed's main is the low main, and the bed is at three and a half degrees, and the pump is what is holding the housing, and if the water in the middle of this yard gets to the well house I have to choose between the seed house and the node, and the node is the reason the seed house has a pump in it.

"So the design was half a design. I have got the water off the bank and into the field and I have not got it off the field."

He had it on the back of a seed order, the way he does, and it was four lines, and it went into the working notes at the ninth hour with the hour on it, and Anneke Roos read it back and initialled it as an opinion, and there is no figure at the bottom of it because he does not have one yet.

"The new run is full and not draining, because the water above it is standing in it and the water below it has gone. It is the only thing that has come out of this field and put into the seed house's main in four weeks, and it is full, and it is not draining.

"I need somewhere for the water to go that is not the middle of this holding. And the only place on this place that takes water and has never refused it is Weir Row, two hundred yards up the fen road, where I put an old dead branch's pressure into the old drainage channel on the night of the tenth of last month with a crowbar, and the channel goes off to the fen, and there is a cut in it I made with my own hands in the dark in a storm.

"And between the top of that field and that channel there is nothing. There is a hedge and a fence and about forty yards of ground that nobody has ever put a ditch through, and I know exactly what I am proposing and I am going to say it in front of all of you because I have been the man who proposes things in this yard and gets refused on five correct grounds four separate times, and I am not going to be a man who does it in the dark this time.

"I am proposing thirty or forty yards of ditch, by hand, tonight, in the rain, in the dark, with a bar and a hook and five people, from the top of the outer field to the old channel at Weir Row.

"It is the bypass. It is not the bypass. It is the same length and the same place and it is the same work and I know it, and I am telling you that if I do this and call it anything else then in four years a man in a room is going to read this book and find out that this body dug a bypass at half past seven in the evening in a flood with no contractor and no licence and no money, four men waiting on a peat road to be asked, and he will be right, and I would rather he hears it from me in this room on the sixteenth of a month than work it out in six weeks."

Nobody in that room said anything for a while.

Then Jonas Byre said, "It is thirty or forty yards and I will not go down four feet, and I have said that three times and I am not going to say it a fourth, and I am not going to be the man who stops this either, so I will say what I will do."

He put his hand flat on the table.

"I will hold the top end and I will count the minutes out loud, and I have counted a load test from the boards for thirty years and the only thing that has ever been asked of me is that I do not get tired of saying numbers, and I am not going to get tired of saying numbers tonight."

And Ilsa Kern said the thing about the burn, and she said it to Marek and not to the room, and it was the only sentence either of them said about the sixth of this month and the ninth of this month and the split in this crew, and it was about the west side of a windbreak and not about anybody's character.

"I was wrong about the west side," she said. "I have been wrong about the west side since the sixth of this month and I have not said it, and I have been standing on a bank for three days watching nine hundred yards of it take a west wind with a hundred and six feet missing out of the middle, and I was arguing for burning it on the third, and the reason I was arguing was that I could not think what else to do with a problem I could not dig my way out of.

"That is all. I was wrong about the west side. Say it in the book or do not say it in the book, and I am going to bed for four hours whatever you do, because I am going to be in that ditch at the second hour and I have not been down four feet since the fifteenth day of a month and I do not intend to start."

---

They went out at the ninth hour of the evening on the sixteenth with a bar, a hook, two spades, a lantern and a rope, and Marek did not go in first, and Jonas Byre did not go in at all, and they put the top of the cut in eleven feet from the head of the new run's bank and not in the run, because Ilsa Kern had put her boot on that exact line on the fourteenth day of last month and had said *there, and not two feet nearer that*, and nobody had written it down then and nobody needed it written down now.

And while they were at it, the mill boy came up the fen road at about the eleventh hour of the night — a lad of fifteen who had walked nine miles in the dark on a road that was under at the second gate — with a slate and a piece of chalk, and he stood at the gate in the rain and would not come in out of it.

He said: the wheel read four hundred and nineteen and Mr Teale wants to know if anybody here is counting.

Marek said no, nobody was counting, and the boy wrote *no* on the slate and took it back down the road, and the figure went up the fen road before the district, as every figure on that board has gone for thirty-one years.
