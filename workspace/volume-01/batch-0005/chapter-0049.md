# Chapter 49

## Leaning Sideways

The conditional charter went up on the seed-house door on the twenty-second at about eleven in the morning with four nails and a cold chisel, and it is four clauses and a fifth sheet, and it is nailed over the top of a district inspection tag that has been on that door since the eighteenth day of last month and was never taken down, because the officer who put it there had gone inside on the eighteenth and the renewal had been refused and the charter had lapsed at midnight, and there has not been an officer in this building since.

The tag is a small oval of copper on a wire loop, and it has been under the caution, and the caution is under the charter, and nobody has taken any of them down and nobody is going to until somebody comes who has to.

Lissa Vale hung the charter at the height she could reach without a chair and stood back and looked at it, and then went into the seed house and shut the door, and the door has a glass panel in it and she was visible through the glass for about four minutes doing something with her hands that nobody in the yard could see.

She came out with the fieldbook in the oilcloth under one arm and the use log under the other, and she put both of them on the seed-house table in front of the middle of it, and she did not put them in the press, and the press stood open on the wall with the ledger on it and the March's tin under the ledger where it has been since the nineteenth day of last month, thirty-three days, at risk, on a bed at three and a half degrees.

"Three days," said Marek. "Three days since the charter was made and you have had that in your hands and you have not put it back in the press."

"No," said Lissa Vale.

"Then where does it live."

"It lives on the table," she said, "in the middle of the table, and anybody may come and look at it and anybody may ask to have the use log read to them and I will read it to them. The only thing I have not done is put it back in a press in a room where a woman keeps it, and I am not going to. If you want it explained you can ask me in a year."

She put her hand flat on the use log and left it there for a second.

"That is the fourth thing I have refused in six weeks," she said, "and I did not number it, and it is not the same as the other three, and I am not going to be asked about it today."

Marek did not ask her about it. He has not asked her about the other three either, and he has not asked her what she has in her case, and he is not going to.

---

The compost was the last thing anybody settled and it was settled on the twenty-second at about four in the afternoon at a table, and it took twenty minutes, and the number has not changed all season and did not change on the twenty-second.

Four hundred hundredweight a month, at four shillings, is eighty pounds a month. The return main of this node is specified at four hundred hundredweight a month and has received twenty-one hundredweight and a quarter on the fourteenth of this month and one hundredweight on the twenty-first day of last month. The return is a return of compost, and it was made of sheep, and the sheep were a hundred and thirty head on the tenth of October, and they are alive somewhere in this fen and they are not coming back, and nothing usable came back with them.

The new run of thirty-one yards at the head of the field was licensed on the twenty-first day of last month with a return line that said the return was three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight a month short, in a licensed engineer's own hand, under the name of the engineer of record. It was designed to be fed four hundred hundredweight a month and it is now going to be fed what sixteen households can raise in a bad month, which is four pounds five in a fortnight, twice.

"It is bought," said Tova Reed. "From here on it is bought, at four shillings, and the heap is thinner every month, and the flock that made it was not a convenience and it was not a stock, it was a machine for turning grass into nitrogen, and it went up a fen road in October with a drover's dog and something walking beside it that nobody could name at the time and everybody in this yard can name now.

"And I have written the number on the annex in my own hand for the third time this month, and the annex is a condition of the charter from the twenty-first, and a condition of a charter is not a complaint. It is a thing somebody has to do. And nobody in this room can do it, and the only sum that ever appeared was four pounds five, twice, and the rest of it was a woman's fee and a reader's day rate, and both of those were the right thing to spend it on, and I want somebody in four years to be able to find out that this holding fed a graft at about a twentieth of what the graft was designed to be fed, and that nobody in this building ever said the graft was working."

Nobody in the building said the graft was working. It is in the annex, in her hand, against the trust's interest, and it is a condition of a charter, and the charter lapses at the end of the season without notice.

---

The March's copy of the seed annex went into a public ledger on the counting house side of the road, in a room where anybody may read it and anybody may ask for a copy, and Tova Reed marked it herself with the rule she marks everything with. The row said what every row in that ledger says: the thing, the quantity, the condition, the destination, the date and the witness. The witness is Lissa Vale, the destination is Cray, and the thing is a bed at three and a half degrees and twenty-one hundredweight and a quarter of compost.

"The first of every month the trust is to be told what the bed is doing, whether or not it helps us, and I have asked for the true answer and not the one that makes the trust look well. This month twenty-one hundredweight bought with money raised from a levy went into that bed, and the bed reads three and a half degrees on the iron, and a hundredweight is worth about half a degree, so that compost is worth about a tenth of a degree, and a soil iron cannot read a tenth of a degree, and I am not going to write a degree on that ledger that I cannot stand behind. The hundredweight goes in and the degree waits. And that is not the March's line and never will be, and the March's line is still lost, and the tin is still in that press.

"And in four years the comparison this arrangement was bought for is going to be between a fen that spent four pounds five twice and a dry line that gets four hundred hundredweight a month, and those are not the same field and never were, and I want that in my own hand on the annex and I want it left there."

She wrote it down. It is the sixth line Tova Reed has entered against the trust's own interest since the ninth of the month, and Marek has read all six and said nothing about any of them, and that is on the page too.

---

The first row of next season's line went into the ground on the twenty-second of the month at about half past three in the afternoon, in the north annex, in a bed that is four feet wide and thirty yards long, by two people: Gael Senn, who asked to do it and was allowed to because the log said so, and Marek.

It is the Longneck, out of the trust's own material, eleven trays' worth, and it is the first row of a line that a woman from the Salt road has put her name under for nine thousand people who are not in this fen, and it came up in four hours.

It is leaning sideways.

The ground under the north row has been under water for a week, and the drill is going into a bed that has not been turned since the fourteenth of a month, and the row is leaning about eleven degrees off the vertical along its whole length, and nothing can be done about it until the ground dries, and the ground will take until about the first of next month.

"That is the land doing this year," said Gael Senn, and got her rule out of her coat, and measured it, and wrote it down. "Eleven degrees, leaning, off the vertical, the whole row, the first row, on the twenty-second. It goes in the book and it goes in the copy, and I have not put the word *failed* anywhere, because it has not failed, it is in the ground and it is leaning and I do not know what it will do and neither does anybody in this yard, and a woman who writes *failed* in a public book about a row of seed in a wet drill is writing about herself and not about the seed."

Marek took the drill out of the ground and stood at the end of the row with his boot on the headland and looked at eleven degrees.

"There is a thing I ought to say and I am going to say it to you and to the book and to nobody else," he said. "A month ago I cut a new run of living pipe into the head of that field with four people and a licensed engineer, and it worked, and it is working now, and it took a field that was dying and made nineteen acres of it stand, and the head where it is nailed is in good order and the pipe is green and there are four willow sets in a ditch at the top of the field that are alive and holding their lashings.

"And it is going to go on working for about seven months, and then the compost is going to run out, because the compost is four pounds five twice a fortnight from sixteen houses and it was designed to be four hundred hundredweight a month, and the thing it feeds was designed to be fed, and it is going to be starved, and it is going to starve quietly, and in the spring there is going to be a row of nineteen acres that has had the best six months it has had in three years and a root line under it that has been drawn down about as far as compost goes.

"And nobody is going to be able to tell that from the top of the field," he said. "That is what I am saying. You will not be able to see it in August. You will be able to see it in the second week of next August, and by then this will be somebody else's log."

He wrote it in the cooperative's book at the sixth hour with the hour on it, in his own hand, with the words *entered by M. Vale, engineer of record, against the interest of the application*, and it is a section-nine margin note and the sixth of the six, and the only one of the six that is not about a cut, and it is in four books in four places by the end of the week, and it is going to Cray by the March road with everything else.

---

The lamp in the seed house was still burning at about nine o'clock on the night of the twenty-second when the yard emptied, and it is a rootlight lamp and it is a living bulb, and a living bulb is fed with water and with decomposing matter, and it is fed at night by whoever is in that building, and on the twenty-second of the month that was Lissa Vale, and she did not make a ceremony of it and would not have allowed one.

There was a charter on the door outside with a copper tag under it, and a fieldbook and a use log on a table in the middle of the room with the press standing open and the March's tin in it on the thirty-third day, and a bed at three and a half degrees with a rootwood floor under it, and a row of Longneck in a wet drill eleven degrees off the vertical, and a public ledger in a counting house nine hundred miles away with a steward's rule-mark on it.

In the yard, the ruts of Nia Vale's cart were still in the gate road, where the ground had been soft on the twentieth day of a month. They had filled with three days' rain and they had not gone, and they pointed out of the gate and up the fen road and away, and nobody in that yard had put a cart back into them, and nobody was going to.

Sera Quill went down the road on the afternoon cart on the twenty-second with her case, her fee on an invoice and eight pages of notes, and she left the certificate of the twentieth day of last month on the seed-house table with the three names in the order they had always been in. It is worth one day's evidence about one wound, and it cannot be entered as a condition of the charter, and it sits in a file in Fennmere with her personal liability on it, and nobody in this fen has told her she was right, and nobody is going to.

Marek Vale kept nothing. He has no instrument, because his mother has it and it is on a table. He has no private log, because the log is a book anybody may read and the copy is in a file that cannot be recalled. He has no private advantage, because the only thing he knows that nobody else knows is what the scarroot at the top of a field felt like under his hand at the fourth hour of the morning on the seventeenth, and he is not licensed, and the only witness he has is a woman of twenty-three who did not see it, and who has written in her own book that she did not see it, and that what she heard on that bench was a licensed engineer's three lines and not his account, and that the two are not the same, and that Mr Vale's account of the fourth hour is a rumour with a witness in it, which is her phrase, used about him on purpose.

He has a certificate he cannot use without a licensed woman standing next to him, and a monthly obligation he did not want and argued against for nine minutes in a cold room in Fennmere, and a fen that knows what he did in the dark on the night of the tenth of last month and has never once mentioned it to him or to anybody else, and a row of seed leaning eleven degrees off the vertical in a drill that is still full of water.

He can feel a pressure in a piece of wood now, with his hand on it, in a way he could not in the first week of last month. It is not reliable. It cost him the ground under his feet and an hour he cannot account for and a stone at a weir and his own breakfast. It cannot be used in a room without a licensed woman standing next to him, and there is no mark on his arm, and he has looked at it twice since the seventeenth and both times it was nothing.
