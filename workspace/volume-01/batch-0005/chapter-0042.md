# Chapter 42

## What Fell Due On The Forty-Second

The rain came in at about four in the morning on the twelfth of the month and it came the way the caution said it would, without ceremony, out of the west and across the fen in a long gray sheet that you could watch arrive across nine miles of flat ground from the top of the south embankment.

Jonas Byre came out of the mill-keeper's shed at five with his coat on and looked at the race, which was the first thing anybody in that yard looked at, because the race is the one piece of water in Tarrow Fen that tells you what the sky is doing before the sky has finished.

"Two inches in the channel," he said, "and it has not stopped, and it is not going to stop today, and that is not me being dramatic, that is a channel that took a fortnight to fill in the spring and has filled in nine hours."

They went to Fennmere at ten, in two carts, with the rain going, and it took until half past three instead of two o'clock, and they were wet through by the second gate and there was no arguing about it.

---

Perrin Osse had the counter clear again, one clerk out of the room, and this time she had a fire going, which she did not mention.

"Right," she said. "The form, the statement attached, and I am going to read the top of the page before I do anything else with it, because a clerk who reads the middle of a document first is a clerk who is going to be surprised."

She read the nine words at the top of the page.

*The cooperative does not dispute the amount.*

"Right," she said again. "That is Mr Vale's hand and not mine, and I have seen this form with an amount disputed in it eleven times this year and I have never once seen a body strike the box out. I am going to say the thing that strikes out, because nobody in this room has said it and somebody is going to have to say it on the twenty-first."

"Struck out, there is nowhere on this form to say the amount has not been paid. There is a box for the amount, and a box for whether the amount is disputed, and a column for the hour the cause was entered, and there is no box in the whole of the water-supply bylaw's sixth part for a body that agrees the amount and has not got it. I have got a margin. I am going to write in the margin, and I am going to read it back, and it is going in the register and it is going to be read on the twenty-first, and I would like the cooperative to understand that they have made a clerk write in a margin on the day they did it and not the day they thought about it."

She wrote it and read it back.

*Amount not disputed. Sum not received. No facility exists in the form for non-payment and none is provided. Entered in the margin of the applicant of record at the request of the undersigned.*

"Are you going to pay it," said Dunmar Fitch.

"No," said Perrin Osse, "because you have four pounds five and the bill is sixty pounds ten and I have been your clerk for a fortnight and I am not going to ask a body with no money whether it has any."

"And what happens to it."

"Nothing happens to it, Mr Fitch, and that is the part I am obliged to tell you and would rather somebody had told you a month ago." She put the pen down. "A schedule of damage under the sixth part is a debt. A debt is a thing somebody owes. And a debt is enforceable against a body, and a body is a thing with a charter, and this body has not had a charter since the eighteenth of a month, and there is nobody in the world you can serve sixty pounds ten shillings on."

"So the sum is not owed to anybody. It is entered. And what the district can do about a sum that is entered and not owed is the only thing the district can do about anything, which is the emergency clause, on a report, at a time of its choosing, and I have written that on the margin too because somebody will otherwise tell a hearing officer that the money was paid."

---

The statement went in at the fourth hour of the afternoon, entire, eleven hundred words, with the hour on it and the three signatures in the right order, and Perrin Osse counted the pages twice and wrote a number in the register.

"Your engineer of record's own document," she said, "in which he says that the whole account this body holds of the cause of the damage in the low quarter is a rumor with a witness in it. That is still your document?"

"It is."

"Then I am going to tell you what it does in this room, because you put it in the file and I am the one who reads the file. It does not help you. It is going to be the exhibit. And in about six weeks a man is going to stand up in front of that hearing officer and hold it up and say: *the applicant says itself that it cannot prove what it knows*, and he will be right, and the exhibit will be your own, and he will not have had to do any work to get it."

"That is what it is for," said Marek.

"I know that is what it is for," said Perrin Osse. "I have been a clerk for twenty-two years and I have read that sentence in about nine documents. It is still true that it is the exhibit."

---

They came back at about six to a yard that had been standing in the rain for nine hours without them. The embankment at the low corner was running, and there was water in the bottom of the yard where there had never been water in the bottom of the yard in nine years of Marek's memory, about two inches of it, and it was going away as fast as it came in, and that was the good news.

The bad news was the ditch at the head of the field.

"Full," said Ilsa Kern. "Full the whole thirty-one yards to the bank and the four sets are under it at the toe. That is not a flood. That is a ditch that has stopped being a ditch because the water above it is standing in it and the water below it has gone, and I want somebody who knows a word for that."

Marek went out into it and stood at the ditch head at seven in the evening in the rain and watched the water come into the run at one end and not go out of it at the other, and the four willow sets were under the water at the toe and the withy lashings were under the water, and the stake at the four o'clock side with the date and the four names cut into it was an inch under and he could see the top of the cut marks.

He wrote it in the book at the eighth hour with the hour on it and read it back, and the last line of the entry is his and not the clerk's: *The new run is full and not draining, and the four willow sets are under at the toe, and I have not touched them, and nobody is going to touch them tonight, and the words in this entry that matter are the two in the middle and they are: and not draining.*

---

The burn's window shut at midnight and nobody signed anything and nobody refused anything, and Dunmar Fitch entered the fact at the ninth hour with the hour on it and did not editorialise on it, which was the single largest act of restraint of his working life and he said so himself in the working note.

*The district's hazard measure of the third was to be executed between the sixth and the twelfth day of this month on a day of the district's choosing. The window was the sixth to the twelfth. It is now the thirteenth and the measure has not been executed. The cooperative neither consented nor refused. The measure has lapsed by its own terms and no party is to be treated as having decided anything.*

Then, at nine o'clock, with the rain still going and the lamp in, Lissa Vale put the levy paper on the seed house table, because it was four weeks since the last one and it was a Saturday.

"Sixteen," she said. "Fourteen signatures last time and two who never signed and whose names are not recorded, because the paper forbade it. Nine of the fourteen put a round share in. Two put nothing at all. One put a shilling and wrote *and I am sorry*. Two signed only so as not to be the ones who had not. And that is what this body has, four weeks after it asked, and there are two things in this yard that need money before the twenty-first and there is one paper."

"What are the two things," said Marek.

"One of them is you," said Tova Reed.

She had come in out of the rain an hour ago and had not changed her coat, and she had the four crates standing open behind her in the annex at three and a half degrees, and she was holding a sheet of the trust's own paper.

"Fourteen pounds ten," she said. "Mr Vane's price. Fourteen pounds ten for a reading, a finding, and an attested copy that a hearing officer may hold, and it is not moved in four years, and he is sixty-one, and he has taken no paid work since his wife died, and he is a better reader than Ms Quill."

"And the box has four pounds five in it, and there is a rain warning on the Fennmere road and a sitting on the twenty-first, and the earliest day a reading is possible is the eighteenth, and if that money is not in a man's hand on the seventeenth then the reading is not taken, and if the reading is not taken then the one cure that this body has ever been offered by a professional goes out of the season, and I have watched you walk eleven miles to serve a notice on a man and I am not going to be the one who says the money was never found."

"Fourteen pounds ten is not compost," said Anneke Roos.

"No," said Tova Reed. "It is a reading, and a reading is the only thing any of us has ever had that a stranger can check, and I am not going to be a woman who lets a bed at three and a half degrees stop a licensed reader coming eleven miles in a flood, because that is a very small thing and it is exactly the shape of small thing that a season like this one is made of."

She put the sheet on the table flat.

"Before anybody says anything, the trust is not paying for the fen's evidence. The trust is buying a document, out of the March's line money, and it goes in the annex against the trust and not against you, and it is not a loan, because a loan is enforceable and you cannot be sued and there is no office to sue, and a thing that cannot be enforced and is called a loan is a gift with a receipt on it, and I do not give gifts with receipts."

"What the trust buys is this. The reading goes to the district first and to you second. Not the other way round. And the March gets a copy and the annex carries a sum against it, and in four years the first row of the March's own line is thinner than it would have been by fourteen pounds ten, and I am going to write that down now, in my own hand, on the annex, where nine thousand people on a road who are not in this room can find it."

It took two refusals and a vote, and the vote was five to three with the two who had spoken against it not voting at all, and both refusals were correct, and Dunmar Fitch entered all three things with the hours on them and the order they were said in.

Anneke Roos went first and went to the shape of it rather than the money, which is what she does.

"I have spent five weeks teaching this yard that a document you can check is worth more than a document you have to believe," she said, "and a document bought by a party who is named on another document about this same node is a document with a creditor in it. The trust is a party to the seed arrangements of this node. It is in a document signed on the ninth of this month. And on the twenty-first a man is going to read that annex and this reading side by side and he is going to say *the applicant bought its own second reader off a creditor*, and he will be half right, and half right is enough, and there is no power of appeal."

Marek went second and he had it written on the back of a seed order before he said it.

"It is worse than that and I have got the arithmetic," he said. "There is one line in this fen where fourteen pounds ten is not money, and it is the compost line. Four pounds five is twenty-one hundredweight and a quarter, at four shillings, and that is about a twentieth of one month's return on a node that gives eleven hundred turns a day, and the specification is four hundred hundredweight a month and we are three hundred and ninety-nine short of it in a licensed engineer's own hand. If I take fourteen pounds ten off a Salt March steward I am choosing, on a wet Saturday, in my own name, which of two shortfalls this body is going to be standing in on the first of next month. I am not going to make that choice, and I am not going to let anybody else make it in this room either."

Tova Reed read both of them to the end without moving and then answered them in the order she had been given them and not in the order that suited her, which is her own habit and which Marek has never once got used to.

"The first thing you are both right about is that a creditor in a document is a bad thing and I have spent four years making sure this trust is never a creditor of anybody who cannot pay it," she said, "and you are right that the annex names us as a party to the seed arrangements and not to the water, and I am not going to argue that word with you, because it is my word and you have read it correctly."

"The second thing is wrong, and it is wrong in a way I would like written down, because it is the exact mistake I came here to stop. You are treating fourteen pounds ten as a sum of money competing with another sum of money, and it is not, and the fact that it is not is the entire reason the March exists. A hundredweight of compost is a return into a pipe. Fourteen pounds ten is the only thing this season has ever been produced that a stranger can check without believing anybody, and there are nine thousand people on a road who are going to spend four years comparing a fen line against a dry line, and the comparison is worth nothing to them if there is one licensed reading of this node in the world."

"So it is not your shortfall against my shortfall. It is a return against a hole, and I know which of the two is worth more, and I have the arithmetic for it too, and my arithmetic says this."

"And mine says the bed is at three and a half degrees," said Marek.

"Then the bed goes on this annex too, in my hand, as a second sum, and if the March is ever found to have been wrong then there are two lines in a document in Cray with my name at the bottom of both of them and I will have earned it."

"There," she said. "And that is the last time this month anybody is going to give me money, and I have four crates of rye standing open in a cold room behind me and a soil iron in my hand, and we are going to go and find out what the rain has done to a ditch."
