# Chapter 44

## The Low Corner

The water came up the inside of the south embankment at about eleven in the morning on the fifteenth of the month and it came the way water comes into a thing that has been designed for it, which is to say that it found every weakness in ninety yards in about a quarter of an hour and then went on looking.

Ilsa Kern was on the low corner at eight and she had been on it since the twelfth.

"It is at the two-furlong mark," she said. "Not on the field. On the embankment. There is a difference and I am the only person in this fen who is going to be able to tell you what it is in four days' time. The mark is on the slope and the slope is cut, and when the water gets into the cut it is inside the embankment and not on it, and after that it is a question of how much gravel is under this corner, and I dug this corner's toe ditch in the year of the great frost and I put nine inches of chalk under it and I have never written that down anywhere because nobody ever asked me."

"Nine inches of chalk," said Marek.

"Under the toe. And above the chalk there is whatever the men who built the bank in my grandmother's time put there, and they are all dead and they are not writing to me about it."

---

He left at nine with fourteen pounds ten in a sealed envelope in his coat and a cart and one man, and the causeway below Weir Row was under, and had been since the Monday night, which meant the last two miles on foot in a wind that had come round to the west-north-west and was not a gale and was going to be a gale.

Idris Vane's door was open and the kettle was going and there was a lamp on the table that had not been lit on the last visit, and the fieldbook with the gray boards and the stiff strap was on the table where it had been on the fourth of the month, in the same place, and it was not moved and it was not mentioned and neither of them looked at it.

"Sit down," said Vane. "You are wet and you have come in a flood and you have not come to tell me about the weather."

Marek put the envelope on the table.

"Fourteen pounds ten," he said. "For a reading, a finding, and an attested copy that a hearing officer may hold. It is out of the Dryfoot Trust's annex and it is on the annex in the steward's own hand against the trust, and she has written on it that the March's first row of its own line will be thinner by that sum, and she said the number out loud at the table on the twelfth in front of the eight people who were in it, so that nobody could find out later that it had been quietened."

Vane did not touch the envelope for about four seconds.

"Sit down properly," he said. "Now the questions, and they are the same three in the same order, and I am going to ask the third one differently this time because the month has gone on and the third one has changed."

"The first: who pays."

"The Salt March Dryfoot Trust, of Cray. Out of the annex, against the trust, in the steward's own hand, and it is not a loan and it is not a gift and there is no office to sue anybody, which is what she said about it in a doorway and she was right and I have thought about it for two days."

"The second: what is the notice for."

"The charter," said Marek. "It was the charter on the fourth and it is the charter now, and on the twenty-first a hearing officer decides whether this body ever holds this node again, and I have said that sentence out loud to exactly one other person in six weeks and you are the second, and I would like it to stay in your mouth and not go into your finding, because a licensed document is not a place for a cooperative's ambition."

"My finding is a place for what I find," said Vane. "I will not put that in it. Move on."

"The third, and it has changed. On the fourth I asked you what Ms Quill read, and you told me, and I wrote it out and it went in an envelope with your mother's seal on it and it is still in her press and I have not asked about it once. I am going to ask about it now, and I am going to do the asking in a way that is not fair to you, and you are going to stand there and let me, because I put the condition in writing and the condition was mine."

"You told me what she read," said Marek.

"On the fourth of the month, at about the eleventh hour, in this kitchen, and in her words and not in a summary, and I wrote it out in front of you. That was the first of my four conditions and it is broken, and it was broken eleven days ago and I have said nothing about it since, and I am saying it now because a man who takes money to read a node and has already been handed the other reader's finding is not a second reader, he is a second signature, and I have signed enough of those in my life to know what they are worth and it is nothing."

"The envelope is not to be opened at all," said Marek. "It went in on the fourth of this month and it has not been out of that press, and my mother has not opened it and I have not opened it and nobody in that building has opened it, and the page it is on is in a book that a district clerk certified on the seventh and put in a file in Fennmere, and the clerk's five words mean that the book cannot be added to without her in the room, and that is the lock the whole of this is sitting behind, and my mother turned it herself."

"It was not to be opened before I read the node, and not at all if it was broken early. It was broken early. So it is not to be opened at all — not by me, not by your mother, not in a room in Fennmere, not by you, not by me with a witness present. It will be a piece of paper in a press with a seal on it for the rest of my life, and I will write to your mother this week and tell her the same thing, and if a man in Auberron asks me in six weeks why the second reader of this node had the first reader's finding in his ear, I will tell him that I wrote the condition, that it was broken, and that I reported it in the room where I was about to do the work."

"That is your own neck you are putting in that noose," said Marek.

"It is the only thing in this kitchen that is mine to put anywhere," said Vane. "And it does not make the reading worth more. It makes it worth what it was worth when your woman put her clip on the seam: a number, my name, my own figures unedited, to the district first and to you second, and not one word of hers anywhere in it. Now put your hand on the table and I will tell you what I am going to do on the eighteenth."

"Then I will read the node," said Idris Vane. "On the eighteenth, which is the fourteenth day, and it is a day, and I do not do it in a day, and I have not moved my rate in four years and I am not moving it now because a fen is under water and my roof is not."

"And I am going to tell you the thing I have been not telling you since the fourth, and I have been not telling it because you did not ask and because a man who has come eleven miles twice is not a man who wants a favor."

"I do not do this for a creditor. I have turned down four bodies in this district in nine years that could pay me, and I turned down all four of them for the same reason, which is that a person who buys a second opinion and then decides what it was going to say before it was said has not bought a second opinion, they have bought a signature, and I have signed enough of those in my life to know what they are worth, and I have just spent a paragraph telling you that you are one of them."

"And I am not doing this for you either, and I want that said, because you have walked into this kitchen twice and been honest with me twice, and I am not going to let a man think honesty buys him something in a room in Fennmere."

"I am doing it because a trust with nine thousand people behind it has written fourteen pounds ten on its own paper against its own line, and that is a person who has put a number in a document where it hurts them, and in thirty years I have done this work four times for four different bodies and three of them were men who wanted me to say a thing and one of them was a woman who wanted me to say a thing that would cost her something."

"She has told me her condition, which is that the reading goes to the district first and to the cooperative second, and I am going to tell you that your condition and hers do not agree: yours is *both or neither* and hers is *first and then second*, and I do not permit a condition like hers, and I have written to her and said so."

"You have written to her," said Marek.

"On the sixteenth of the month, at eight in the morning, by the cart post, and she is going to be furious and she is going to agree with me inside a day, because she is a businesswoman and I have just told her that the thing she bought cannot be held back, and that is what a person buying a document wants to hear."

Marek stood up in a wet coat in a small kitchen eleven miles from a fen.

"What happens if your reading does not agree with hers."

"Then the district has two licensed readers who do not agree, on the same node, on two dates, with both instruments preserved and both sets of figures traveling," said Idris Vane, "and that is not a worse thing for anybody than a rumor with a witness in it, and it is a great deal better than a cooperative with one licensed reader and a bill of sixty pounds ten, and I would like you to go home and remember that I said that to you before I had read anything, and not afterwards."

---

He came back in at about six in the evening by the same two miles and the same causeway and walked into a yard that had changed in the day.

The mill race was out of its banks and running brown. The new run at the head of the field was eleven inches under and the four willow sets were under at the toe. The wheel on the return race was turning and nobody was counting, and the board with the count on it was nine miles off in a miller's own yard at Fennmere where it has been for thirty-one years, and the last figure on it had come up the fen road on a slate on the ninth of the month, and there had been nothing on the board since, and the miller was not coming and the boy was not coming, because the fen road was under at the second gate.

And the low corner of the south embankment was in the yard, which is a sentence that does not mean what it sounds like, and it means that the water had come over the toe of the corner at about four in the afternoon and had stopped four yards inside the bank, and was standing, and was not moving, in a yard where nobody had ever seen water stand.

Nobody was on the corner. Ilsa Kern had come off it at three, on purpose, and had said why, and the reason was that a foreman on a corner in the dark is a man that cannot be got off it by a wife or a mother, and she was going to sleep.

Gael Senn was on the boards over the pit at the seventh hour with the lamp and the two rules, because she had decided that if that corner was going to be the thing that decided the night then the wound in the pit was going to have a figure against it, and she was the only person in that yard who was allowed to take one, and she took it.

"Half an inch and a sixteenth," she said. "The same as Monday. Pump on and continuous for fifty-five hours, and the pit is not up a quarter of an inch, and the housing is doing what the housing is for."

And at nine o'clock, with the lamp in and the rain coming on again, they put the four facts on the table in the seed house, because a thing done at a table has come to a decision and a thing done at a corner has been informed, and Ilsa Kern taught this yard that on the twelfth day of last month and it was the best thing anybody said in the year.

The four facts were these, and they are in the cooperative's own book with the hour on them and they were read aloud before they were written.

One. The low corner is in the yard, four yards inside the bank, standing and not moving, and it is on nine inches of chalk on unknown fill, and the water above it came from the fen and is the fen's water and is not going anywhere.

Two. The nineteen acres below the ditch head are the only cropping ground this body has. If the corner is held, that field is under by the morning, and a rootwood bed at three and a half degrees in the same building goes with the field, because the pump draws off the same head and the head is what the field drinks from.

Three. The three acres at the top end are the top end. They are the highest ground on this holding by about two feet, they are thirty inches of gravel over a bed of flints, and they are the only gravel on the place. If the corner is opened, they take the water first, and the gravel takes it, and the water comes out at the crack where the ditch became a crack, and finds the old channel, and goes to Weir Row, and off this holding.

Four. The seed house holds the fieldbook, the use log, the certified page of the standing log, the March's tin, the open book, and four crates of rye that belong to nine thousand people who are not in this room, and the bed that four crates and nine thousand people have in common, and the only lamp in this fen that anybody on the March road will be able to read a line of a March copy by.

"Then the sentence is not hard to find," said Tova Reed, from the end of the table, not looking at anybody. "Holding the corner is sixteen households' only field and a seed room, and the seed room is nine thousand people's. Opening it is three acres of one man's ground that are already dead and cannot be entered in this book by anybody as a loss to anybody but him."

"So the sentence is *I am flooding my three acres*, and it is six words, and it is Mr Vale's, and it is not going to be voted on, because a vote would make it the body's decision, and it is not the body's decision, it is his ground, and if this yard votes on it then I have signed a document against nine thousand people for a thing that is not nine thousand people's, and I have done that once this month on an annex and I am not doing it twice."

Nobody voted on it. Dunmar Fitch ruled the line in the cooperative's book with the hour on it and then, on his own, without being asked, entered the fact that no motion was moved and no division was taken, and read that back as well. He did not enter the sentence, and when Anneke Roos asked him why not, he said that a book cannot hold a thing before the man who owns it has said it out loud in the yard where it is going to happen, and that if he entered it now it would be his sentence in the clerk's hand, and it would not be the engineer's of record, and there would be two versions of it inside a month.

At about midnight he went out to the corner with a lamp and a bar, and it had not moved and had not gone down, and the chalk under the toe was doing what nine inches of chalk does under nine inches of chalk, which is nothing you could see. He stood on the bank above it for about a quarter of an hour and did not go down into it, because the water was up to the middle of his boot in a place that four hours before had been a corner of an embankment, and because a man who is going to break a thing in the morning does not have to be standing on it tonight.

Behind him the windbreak stood nine hundred yards long with a hundred and six feet missing out of the middle of it, and it had taken three days of west wind without losing a stem, and the district's measure that would have burnt it had lapsed on the twelfth by its own terms because it rained.

He put his boot on the bank and looked at the water for a while and went in, and the lamp in the seed house was still burning, because somebody in that building always fed it and tonight it was his mother and she was not going to be thanked for it either.
