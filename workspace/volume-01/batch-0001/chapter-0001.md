# Chapter 1

## Iron in the Water

The pump pit was four feet down and the water in it was the color of a nail.

Marek knelt on the wet boards with the test weight in one hand and two fingers on the flywheel housing, and he counted. The wheel came round. The weight rose. Above him one of the mill boys leaned into the shaft and shouted that the clerk from Fennmere had left word she would be at the well head at eleven.

He let the weight go. The wheel unwound and the counter settled on forty-one.

“Forty-one,” he said. “It wants forty-eight.”

Ilsa Kern came down the ladder behind him carrying the pump log under her arm, and she set it down on the third rung and did not come the rest of the way. “Forty-eight is what the log says.”

“The log says what Kellan wrote in it six years ago,” Marek said. “Forty-eight is what the pit could lift when the well was nine feet into good gravel. It is not four feet into a wound.”

He spread both hands on the housing and felt the shudder come up through the frame. The lift was static, collar shut, against the dead head in its own closed pipe, which was the only lift there was to make in this state. Everything in the machine was fine. That was the trouble. The pump, the linkage, the flywheel, the packing, the new gasket he had cut himself in the spring — all of it answered when it was asked. What came back up the shaft was thin. He had lifted six hundred firkins out of this pit and could feel in his teeth when a well was giving less than it was taking.

“Bring the gauge,” he said.

The boy brought the gauge. It was Kellan’s gauge, the good one, the one that lived in a wooden case in the well house and came out only for readings that mattered. Marek had watched Kellan Rusk fit it to the head tap four seasons ago, watched him set the mark with a wax pencil, watched him write the number into the log in a hand like a man signing a receipt. The needle sat where the wax said it sat. Half a division under the red line, which is as close to a fair draw as paper admits.

“Half a division under,” Marek said. “Healthy.”

“Aye,” said Ilsa, from the ladder.

Which was the whole problem, and it had been the whole problem for six years. The gauge was screwed into the head tap, above the split — above the place where the main gives off to the charter holder’s own branch and the two of them go their separate ways — so the gauge could tell anybody what the node was giving and never where it was going. A boy could read it and see health in it, and so could Kellan, and so at eleven would the clerk, and a number in a clerk’s book is a harder thing to argue with than a man in a muddy pit.

Marek went up the ladder and got his own notebook out of his coat and wrote the time and the number and the water temperature, because that is what you do first. Then he wrote, in the line under it: *gauge at head tap, reads fair. Pump test 41/48. Gauge is upstream of the split. Disagree with it.*

He looked at that for a while, and then he underlined it, which was a habit he had been trying to break since he was nineteen.

“Open the head,” he said.

Ilsa did not move. “Marek. If the clerk comes at eleven and finds the head open, she finds an unlicensed man with the housing apart.”

“She finds a head she can look down into,” Marek said. “That’s a different thing than a head she’s asked to take on trust. Get the spanners.”

They took the collar off in eleven minutes. It was not a neat job. The bolts had gone into wet oak and wanted heat, and Marek had a brazier from the pump shed and two of the mill boys and Ilsa and a cold that got into his wrists and stayed. While they worked, the yard filled up behind them in the way a yard does. By the time the last bolt came free, there were twenty people standing in the wet grass with the mill race noise behind them, and among them his mother at the seed-house door with her arms folded, not coming down and not going away. Behind all of them, where the cooperative’s ground stopped and the outer rows began, the rye had gone over sideways and lay along the rows like rope, every stalk leaning the same way, the way a crop leans when it has been asking a sky for light and getting none of it. Nobody had cut it. It was not ready. It was white at the top.

The housing was a stone footing with a timber frame on it, and inside the frame the living conduit came up out of the dark on its shoulder of wire and chopped fiber — Kellan’s cradle, built to hold the pipe where the pipe wanted to lean, and where the collar’s ring met the conduit’s ferrule there was a copper seam, dull and green at the edges, put in by a fitter nine years ago and never looked at since. The conduit itself was the color of wet ash and about as thick as a man’s forearm, and it was the ordinary work of a hundred years. Above the cradle, the collar. Below it, the water.

Marek went down the ladder on his knees and put his right arm into the housing, up past the elbow, the way he had done every autumn since he was twenty-one. The cold came up his arm and kept going. He put his palm flat against the conduit and felt for the place he had felt for last autumn, and the place was there, and it was bigger.

Then he felt the coat.

He had been expecting dead wood, the kind that a drainage ditch is full of. What he got was scarroot: living wood that had been hurt, a length of the conduit about two hands long where the grain had gone glassy and swollen, healed over on the outside and soft under the heel of his hand, and scarroot is a thing you can feel from the outside of a pipe the way you can feel a bruise through a boot. Not dead. He pressed it and it pushed back, slowly, the way a thing pushes back that has been holding a shape by itself for years.

It was warm. Warmer than the water around it and warmer than the air, and not by a hair. He had no instrument in his hand and no way to prove a difference he had found with the side of his palm, so he wrote *warmer* in the notebook and left it there, and the leaving of it was the honest part.

And over the scarwood, on the outside of the cradle, was the compound.

He worked his fingers along it. Tar and chopped fiber, laid thick, laid well, laid by somebody who knew what he was doing. Four seasons old. And then, on top of that, three more coats, thinner, each one stopping a finger short of the edge below it, the way you do when you come back in a hurry and cannot get the line straight. He got his thumbnail into the lip where the top coat had run down over the one beneath and lifted a corner, and the whole pad shifted together, all four seasons of it, as one piece, because it had never bonded to anything. It was sitting on the scarwood the way a folded coat sits on a chair.

Then he found the wet.

It was at the low side, at the six o’clock position, and it was not seepage from the pit. It came up *through* the coat. A bead of water under the black surface, and then a second, and then a slow runnel down the face of the pad and off the wire cradle and down into the water below. He put the flat of his thumb against the bead and held it there. It came up warm, and it smelled of iron, and it came up through a pad that was supposed to be holding the wound shut.

“Hold that,” he said. “Ilsa, hold that light.”

He got his notebook out with his other hand and braced it on the ladder, and wrote: *Compound pad. Base coat four seasons old, one thinner dressing laid on it every year since, the last of them a season old. Not bonded. Weight only.* And under it: *Water rising through coat at 6 o’clock, warm, iron. Wound is not closed. Coat is not sealing anything.* And under that, because it was in his own hand and he could not stop it: *coat beats, slow, one beat in about four seconds, out of step with the pump.*

He looked at that last line a long time. He did not have a word for it. He underlined it anyway.

He came up the ladder with his arm black to the elbow and stood in front of the twenty people in the wet grass.

“Kellan’s log says compound seal, dressed and renewed, every year for four seasons,” he said. “That is not a seal. That is a base coat four seasons old with a dressing laid on it every year since, none of them ever replaced, and it is coming through the middle of it. It is not holding. It is only heavy.”

The yard was quiet in the way a yard is quiet when everybody has understood the same thing at different moments.

“And the wound is not dead,” Marek said. “That is the one piece of good news and nobody wants it, but it is real. I can feel it pushing back. It is fighting to close. It has been fighting to close for four years under a coat of tar.”

A voice from the back of the crowd — old Hesk, who had worked the north row for forty years — said, “Then why are we standing here in the cold being told about it.”

“Because the pump is not the fault,” Marek said. “The pump test came in seven short and the pump is sound. There is a wound in the root line under this well and it is not closing, and the water tastes of iron because it is coming up through scarwood. If I can get that pump to run before the clerk gets here it will run on the eighteenth, and it will run on the day after that, and the well will be doing exactly what it is doing now on every one of them.”

Somebody started to clap, two or three times, and stopped.

“And I am not going to prove that in a shed,” Marek said. “I am going to prove it in front of an inspector, on a record, with a date on it. Whoever put the number in the log can answer for it in a room.”

He said it looking at the middle of the crowd, which was cowardly, and he knew it was cowardly, and he did it anyway.

Then he went back down the ladder, because a pump that will not run is an answer to nobody, and the clerk was coming in fifty minutes.

The draw was fouled. That was the whole of it, and it was not nothing. A season of iron coming up off a wound had settled on the foot valve in a skin you could take a thumbnail to, and the packing in the gland had gone to the same stuff and stopped being a seal and become a plug. A Crown mechanic would have had it out in an hour, at thirty shillings a day, come out from Fennmere, and the cooperative did not have thirty shillings for a day.

So Marek went down with a flat bar and a bucket and Ilsa Kern on the windlass, and he got the bell off the foot valve and lifted the fouling out in handfuls into the bucket, where a man could look at it and be sure of it, and he drew the packing out with a hook and packed it again with new. He did all of it in his wet coat, because there was no dry coat in the well house and nobody to bring him one.

They ran it on the brake with the collar off, which tests nothing except whether water moves. The rig came round and the pump came round with it, the pit water went down half a foot, and the standpipe filled and came up out of the delivery into the yard trough, which had been dry since the spring. Four men filled a bucket out of it and drank, one after another, standing in the wet, and said nothing about it, and the water tasted of iron.

The counter on the test weight stopped at forty-one, because the counter was always going to stop at forty-one. Forty-eight was nine feet into good gravel, and the well was four feet into a wound, and nothing done in this pit between now and Michaelmas was going to make those two numbers the same. The one thing the cooperative could have this morning was a pump that moved water.

Marek stood in the wet grass with his hands shaking and a bucket of fouling at his feet and understood, with a clarity that was worse than the fear, that he was proud of it. He had taken a thing that was not his to have and made it work with a bar and a hook and forty minutes. It was the best piece of work he had done in four years. It would run on the eighteenth, and on the day after that, and on every day after that, and the water would taste of iron on all of them, because the pump had never once been the fault and now the pump was perfect.

He put the bar back on its hook and washed his hands and looked at the pit, and the water in it was two inches lower than it had been an hour ago, which was the only true thing in the yard.

Nia Vale was standing at the head of the ladder with the season’s board in her arms, because where she was not, the board was.

“Three things,” she said. “That is what is on this board and that is what a charter can do to a holding. Two of them are about the well and I could say both of them standing here in front of these people. The third is the one nobody in this yard says out loud, and I am not going to say it either, because you have got forty minutes and because if I say it you will go and do something about it.”

“Nia.”

“I am not saying it.” She turned the board so the bottom of it faced his knees and did not let go of it. “Read it when the clerk has gone. You are the one who goes and talks to people, and the people who should have heard it from me are the people standing in this yard eating their own seed.”

The clerk arrived at four minutes past eleven.

She was perhaps forty-five, in a waxed waterproof coat, with a satchel, and the unhurried way of a person who has done the same walk four years running and expects to do it a fifth. Perrin Osse. Marek had seen her twice a year for four years and had never once been asked a question she had not already written down.

“The housing is open,” she said.

“It is.”

“Under whose standing?”

“Mine. Work cooperative standing, engineer’s call, ordinary repair, no rootwork, no graft, nothing I am not licensed to do and you can see I have done.” Marek stepped aside so she could get to the ladder. “I would rather you looked down it than took my word for it.”

She looked down it. She did not go down the ladder. She stood at the top with the wind coming off the mill race and asked him three questions — the diameter of the collar bolt circle, whether any part of the work needed the housing sealed, and what the last dated entry in the well’s log was. She wrote *wound observed, open, under compound* in a small square hand, and she did not write *unlicensed* anywhere, and Marek understood that she had made a decision about him and was not going to say it out loud.

Then she took out a second sheet, and it had a Crown seal on the corner, and it was the charter.

“Your annual inspection is on the eighteenth,” she said. “That has not changed. On the eighteenth an inspector comes, and he does not come to look at your pump. He comes to decide whether this node is maintained, in the sense the charter means, which is dated records, licensed hands, and a return path that works. If it is not maintained, three things can happen and I can write any one of them. The head gets sealed. The seed stock gets taken into district custody, which does not come back to you, because custody is not a loan. Or the families are offered relocation to a Crown town, and relocation is not refused, because there is a waiting list and the people on it are the ones who say yes.”

“Clerk,” Ilsa said. “That is three.”

“It is three,” Perrin Osse agreed. “I have had this conversation forty times.”

“Can it be deferred?” Dunmar Fitch came out from the seed-house door with his coat half on, and a charter clerk being the most careful man in the fen, he was already doing arithmetic instead of arguing. “The walk-through is a formality. If the node has a documented fault, surely the eighteen—”

“Mr Fitch.” She said it kindly. He shut up. “I can defer a walk-through. I cannot defer a date on a charter. You have seventeen days. What you do with them is the only question in front of me.”

She wrote the seventeen days in her book, and then she added, without looking up, the sentence that made the rest of the season possible to plan: “A charter condition is not a thing to be repaired. It is a thing to be *shown*. They are very different, and the people who confuse them are the people I write about in the margins.”

Then she thanked Ilsa, and thanked the crowd in the way you thank weather, and went and looked at the pad from the ladder herself with her own eyes for forty seconds before she wrote anything at all.

Marek got the compound off his hands in the yard bucket, which was cold and did not help, and started up toward the seed house to tell his mother what he had found. The wrapped kit was in his coat where it had been for eight years, since he had taken it out of his father’s toolbag: front board, living bead, and eleven pages, the back board gone and most of the survey pages gone and no explanation he would ever be given.

He was halfway across the yard with the bucket in his hand when the needle inside it moved.

Not the wheel gauge in its wooden case at the well head. The other one, the small one, in the oilcloth, against his ribs. He felt it through two layers of coat and oilcloth and did not stop walking, because he was a surveyor and it was a bent instrument and there was no reason in the world for it to do anything.

But while the head was open it would not settle. It sat over off its rest and it ticked, faintly and unevenly, and it did not come back to rest until he was a dozen steps past the housing, and even then it took a while.

He put the kit down in the shed without unwrapping it, and he did not look at it again, and he went and told his mother instead.
