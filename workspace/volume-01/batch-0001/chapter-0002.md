# Chapter 2

## The Loaned Steward

The wagon came through the gate at midday with the Salt March mud still on the wheels and one man walking beside the mule to keep it honest.

Nia Vale had the yard log under her arm and a list on her fingers, and by the time the wheels stopped she was counting crates instead of greeting anyone.

“Eleven,” she said. “Eleven and a spill of sand. He says nine days to cross the fen and it took him eleven, so that is a good man and I will not be civil to him until we have the trays inside.”

“Put them in the north annex,” Lissa said, from the seed-house door. “Not the main floor. The bed is at four degrees and I am not having the March’s rye warm up on my floorboards because a boy was slow over a bridge.”

Then the driver climbed down, and after him a woman about Marek’s age in a road coat with the hood thrown back, carrying nothing at all.

She did not look at the well head. That was the first thing Marek noticed. Everybody in this yard looked at the well head, because the well head had been open since before noon and there was a smell coming off it that any person in Tarrow Fen could name. She looked at the crates, then at the roof of the seed house, then at the wall of the mill-keeper’s shed across the race, and then at him.

“Tova Reed,” she said. “Dryfoot Trust. Your six-year filing is in my satchel and I read it on the road from Cray, so we have met.”

“I am Marek Vale. I survey here.”

“I know who you are. The filing names you as engineer of record. It also names you as having certified four of the six years of maintenance acceptance, which I would like to discuss somewhere other than a yard.”

Ilsa Kern made a sound like a door closing.

“No,” Marek said. “Later. Where do you want to start?”

“Where you start. I have four questions. I would like the first one answered in front of these people, because if it is answered in a shed I will assume it was chosen in a shed.”

There were perhaps thirty people in the yard by then, and more arriving, because a wagon from the Salt March is an event in a place where the horizon is two miles away.

Marek had spent the morning knowing he was going to be asked. He had a version ready. It had a fault diagram in it.

“All right,” he said. “Ask.”

“The filing says the node delivers eleven hundred turns a day to Lantern Reach ground.” Tova Reed did not raise her voice and did not need to; she had the flat, unhurried delivery of somebody reading out a number she had already decided was wrong. “Is that right?”

“That is what the log says.”

“Is it right?”

“No,” Marek said. “Not in the sense that the head draws roughly that. The head draws that. The column is the problem.”

“Explain the column.”

“The charter has two columns. *Drawn at head*, and *delivered to holder’s ground*. Kellan’s log puts eleven hundred in both. The charter lets him keep the difference, up to four hundred, as his own licensed draw, so long as the return is maintained. The head draws eleven hundred. Our ground takes seven hundred and twenty, and no more, and the rest goes down his private branch.”

“So the log puts a licensed take in the column that means the cooperative got it,” Tova Reed said. “And your cooperative signs an acceptance against that column every year.”

“Yes.”

“Who signs it?”

“I do. Four years. Two of the six, Dunmar did.”

“Dunmar Fitch.” She looked at him. “You signed a number you did not measure.”

“I signed a number Kellan’s crew measured, in a column headed *delivered*, in a log the charter lets us copy.”

“Then say the rest of it out loud, Mr Vale, because you have worked out that I can do arithmetic, and I would rather you did the first sum in front of them than had me do it for them.”

Marek looked at the crowd and did the sum, because she was right and because there was no version of this where a stranger did it for him.

“Seven hundred and twenty is the most the ground can take,” he said. “Ditch section is a foot and a half at the crown, and we have got eleven miles of main, and the row’s clay holds water about four hours before it ponds, and you cannot irrigate a field standing in a pond — you drown the roots and you carry the iron across on the crown. Eleven hundred less seven hundred and twenty is three hundred and eighty a day. Three hundred and eighty a day for six years is eight hundred and thirty-two thousand turns of water that this cooperative has never laid on any field it owns.”

Nobody said anything. Somewhere behind the crowd the mill wheel was turning and could be heard over the wind, which it usually could not, because the race is shallow this time of year and the sound of it is a low uneven knocking.

“That is a great deal of water,” Tova Reed said. “I have been standing in your yard for an hour. I have not eaten. I want you to understand that I did the sum on a cart with a broken axle, and I am not going to be pleasant about what it means, because you have had six years to be pleasant about it and you were not.”

“No,” Marek said. “I have had six years to check it and I did not.”

The silence went on a beat too long. Then old Hesk said, “Then somebody should have checked it,” in the tone of a man who has said a true thing and been glad to say it.

“Somebody should have,” Tova Reed agreed. “Somebody with the arithmetic and no reason to want the answer to be a particular shape. That is usually not the same person.”

He was not angry, which was the last thing he had expected to feel. What he felt instead was the particular lightness of a man who has been leaning on a door and has come back later and found it was not a door at all.

“What do you want?” he said. “The cooperative promised you a pressure report. I will have it inside a week, every reading, in a book you can carry to the March.”

“I do not want your pressure report,” she said. “Your pressure report is a drawing by a man who has an interest in what it shows. I want four things and I will say them once.”

“All right.”

“One. A measured draw, not a promise of one. I want a number that a clerk can put in a book and that I could have got myself, which means I want to be permitted to witness it and to write down what I saw and to have my account filed alongside yours whether it agrees with yours or not.”

“Yes.”

“Two. Published numbers. Your draw figures, the log, the acceptance column, and the mill tallies, open to any member of this cooperative, any stranger, and any clerk of the Well Directorate who asks. Not copied out for me. Open.”

Dunmar Fitch made a small noise. “That is a charter matter. That is not a matter a member conference can—”

“It is a matter of your shed wall, Mr Fitch, and your shed wall is on your own ground.” She did not look at him. “Will the cooperative do it, Mr Vale?”

Marek thought about the six years, and about the eleven pages in his coat, and about the fact that he had signed a column he had never measured, and he found that he did not have a single argument left.

“Yes,” he said.

“Three. My own veto on any use of the seed vault for a test. Any test. Not a sample and not a tray. If I put a condition in, you will honor it, and if you break it I will say so in writing to the trust and to the Directorate and to whoever else will hold it in.”

Marek’s mouth had gone dry. “That is — yes. Yes, if you put it in writing we will hold to it.”

“Four. A copy of everything, sent to Cray, whether or not it helps us. Your log, your readings, your refusals, the things you did in the dark. All of it. The trust does not need to be right. The trust needs to have it.”

“That is not a condition of a lender,” Tova Reed said. “That is the condition. If you cannot do all four, I would rather have none of them and I would rather have them now than on Thursday.”

“I can do all four.”

“You have not asked what I am doing here.”

“Two days ago you told me in the yard you came to argue about water.”

“I came to compare the March’s last dry-land lines against your seed house, which is a seed matter, and I have been here two hours and it has become a water matter, and I expect it will have become something else by the time I leave. If I had come to argue about water I would have brought the March’s own water and I would have argued with a spade in my hand. I have come because our northern relay failed in the wet season and four lines died with it, and this seed house is the last place in the fen with a living comparison, and I have brought you eleven crates of the only dry-land rye anybody has left who will sell to a cooperative in a bad charter year.”

“The Longneck,” Nia said, from the wagon.

“The Longneck,” Tova Reed agreed. “And by the trust’s own reckoning, which I will say once and then never again — the Longneck should not be planted this year, anywhere, including here. It is a four-inch crop and we have had a nine-inch season and it will come up thin and lock and I will be the one who has to stand in the field and explain it. I am telling you that now so that nobody can tell me later that I hid it.”

“Say it again in the yard,” old Hesk called.

“No,” she said pleasantly. “Once is once.”

Marek put his hand out, stopped, and took it back, because there was nothing here to shake and both of them knew it.

“Now the practical part,” he said. “Two days. You will want the records.”

“I will want the records,” she said. “I will start on them tomorrow morning, and I will look at them for two days, and I will not say one word about what is in them until the second day is out. Not to you, not to Mr Fitch, not to your mother, and not in the yard where people will draw the conclusion they want to draw. When I have read them I will say what I found, and then you may be angry at me, and you will have had two days to prepare for how ugly it is going to be.”

“That is a very controlled way to treat people.”

“That is how you treat a ledger,” she said. “You do not let it know you are leaning on it.”

Ilsa Kern, who had been standing with her arms folded through the entire arithmetic, now said, “You will want to see the well head.”

“I will want to see everything that has a number on it,” Tova Reed said. “I want the mill tallies, I want the acceptance column, I want your work book, and I want the seed house ledger. I want to see the head.”

“And read it?” Marek said. “Your trade — do you have any of the licensed craft? I ask because if you are going to look at my instrument I would rather know.”

She looked at him for a second longer than she needed to.

“I keep seed,” she said. “My authority is whether a line comes up and whether it comes true, and what the March can afford to plant next, and what a district office can do with a number when it wants to. I have no licensed craft, I cannot read a bead, I cannot tell a graft from a scar, and if I tell you what a root line is doing I will be repeating a man from the March who was wrong twice. What I have is a head for figures and a bad temper about seed. Do you want more than that from me, or can you use it?”

“I can use it,” Marek said. “More than that would be a waste of a good road.”

He had worked out, on the way back to the seed house, how the mill’s hand tally related to the two columns in the log, which was the thing he had not told her. The wheel was turned by the return race — the ditch that carried the cooperative’s drainage back down to the node, and the same water Kellan’s log claimed was coming back. Wheel turns and water turns were the same measure; that was the whole point of the tally board, and it had been the whole point of it for thirty years, and nobody had ever needed to explain it to a Salt March seed steward because nobody had ever asked.

What the board was not, and what nobody ever asked it for either, was a day’s draw. It was a record of what came back, and only on the days the mill ground, which was two or three in a week, and the drainage went past that wheel on all the rest of them with nobody counting. And seven hundred and twenty turns was what the whole holding was said to be able to take in a hot week, every acre of it including the outer rows, which the mill never saw the water from. Two different quantities, both honest, and a man who put them on the same line of the same page would be doing arithmetic on the wrong pair of numbers and would look clever doing it.

At the seed house door Lissa Vale had been standing the whole time without moving, and now she said, “Bring the trays in. The annex is four degrees. Watch your feet on the step, the third board is soft, and if you spill sand on my floor I will make you sweep it before you eat.”

And Tova Reed, who had walked into a cooperative in the middle of a failed season to tell it that its numbers were false, said, “Thank you,” and went in.

The afternoon went the way afternoon’s do. Crates up, sand spilled, the Longneck set in its trays on the north bed under Lissa’s eye, and the two women talking about rye for forty minutes in a language Marek could follow about two-thirds of, because it was rye, and roots, and how deep to set, and what a leaf that has already turned means.

He went out at dusk and stood at the mill-keeper’s shed across the race, where the tally board hung under a tin roof, thirty years of hand figures in five different hands, one line for each day, the seasons separated by a ruled band.

Two hundred and sixty-one was the highest figure in the whole board, and it was in the spring of the year Aldren had gone, and the nearest figure to it in the twenty-nine years on either side was two hundred and forty, which told Marek nothing at all except that the mill had a bad spring that year and had been counting honestly while it did.

She had read that board from the yard. He was sure of it, because she had looked at it and then looked at the mill and then looked at him, and the mill race was twenty feet wide and shallow and the only thing worth looking at on that side of the yard.

She had not asked about the tallies. Not once, in four hours.

That was when Marek stopped feeling braced and started feeling outmaneuvered, because a person who has the worst fact in the room and does not spend it is a person who is waiting to be asked.

He went back to the seed house and found his mother’s table clear and the Longneck trays dark under cloth and Tova Reed’s road coat folded on the bench, meaning she was not going anywhere tonight.

“Your sister says you signed an acceptance column you did not measure,” she said, without looking up from the ledger she was ruling a line in.

“I signed the column the log printed.”

“You signed it four times.”

“Yes.”

Lissa ruled her line. The pen made a sound like a small animal in a hedge.

“The kiln boy is asking for a fourth-year man and we have no wages for a fourth-year man,” she said. “I will put his name forward and I will lose the vote, and it will be your name on the resolution that the wages are not there. I want you to know that now, tonight, so that tomorrow you do not act surprised in a room full of people you have known since you were nine.”

“All right.”

“Her conditions,” Lissa said. “You took the veto on the vault. In writing.”

“Yes.”

Lissa put the pen down. “That was the right answer and it will cost you the only thing you had left to bargain with, and she knew that when she asked, and you knew it while you were saying it. Do not go and feel clever about it tonight.”

“I am not feeling anything clever.”

“You are feeling exactly one thing clever,” Lissa said, “and it is a good thing, and I am not going to be the one to say so.”

She picked the pen back up.

“Go to bed. She said two days and she will keep to two days, and you will need the eyes you have got.”

Marek went out into a yard that had gone completely dark, with the wind coming up off the fen and the first of the front that the old people were muturing about somewhere behind the weather. The well head was roped off now. The compound pad over the wound had four seasons of tar on it and nobody coming back to dress it, because the crew that dressed it had not been told to come back yet, and the crew that dressed it was Kellan’s, and Kellan’s crew was busy.

The cooperative had promised a pressure report. He had roughly a week to build one, and the only circuit on the whole holding that could be loaded and measured inside a day was the seed house’s return line, and the seed house’s return line ran under the north annex, where eleven crates of the March’s last dry-land rye were lying in wet sand in the dark.

He stood in the cold thinking about a woman who had found the worst number in the building by doing arithmetic on a cart, and had then sat in a seed house and listened to two hours of talk about rye without once mentioning it.
