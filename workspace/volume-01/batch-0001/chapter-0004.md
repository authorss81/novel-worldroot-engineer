# Chapter 4

## The Vote and the Ledger

The seed house smelled of wet sand and iron and forty people breathing in one room.

Marek came in last and sat down at the far end of the long table with his coat still on, because taking it off would have meant committing to staying. The table was made out of two doors laid end to end and it had never once been polished. Nia had put a lamp at each end and a third in the middle where the plan was, and the plan was the same plan it always was: the whole holding in one flat piece of grey seed paper with the ditches in ink and the embankment in a heavier line, and the well head marked with a ring of salt.

Somebody had put a chair under the north door to keep the draught off the annex, and every so often somebody got up and moved it two inches and everybody watched them do it.

Lissa Vale sat on the seed-steward’s side of the table. Tova Reed sat opposite her, with the seed house book in front of her, closed. Dunmar Fitch had the charter and the log and a fresh sheet, and he had a new quill, and he was holding it the way a man holds something he is hoping will not be needed.

There was no chair for Kellan Rusk because Kellan Rusk was not there. There was a chair for a man Marek had never seen, who was standing by the door with his hood down and his hands behind his back.

Before anybody moved a motion, Tova Reed stood up.

“I am going to say three things and then I am going to sit down,” she said, “and then you are going to do whatever you are going to do without me watching, because I do not want to be a reason for anything.”

She put her hand flat on the book.

“One. I withdraw the condition I entered yesterday at the seed house book. Not the conditions. The one about the vault. Marek Vale agreed to it, you witnessed it, Mr Fitch entered it, and he broke it at two o’clock this afternoon, and I want the record to show that I withdrew nothing that was in writing and that the writing still stands as a thing that was agreed and then broken. That is the whole of what I am withdrawing. My veto on the vault still stands. Any further test, any sample, any tray, I still say no without a reason.”

A murmur. Dunmar’s pen moved.

“Two. On the three trays. They are spoiled. I will say that once more so that no one in this room goes home believing there is a chance of recovering them, and then I am not going to say it again this season unless somebody asks me directly. The March will not charge Lantern Reach for them. That is not a kindness and it is not forgiveness and it is a decision I have made about the trust’s money, and I would like the room to hear the reason, which is that if the trust starts sending invoices to cooperatives that break their own conditions, then within a year every cooperative in the March will be doing it on purpose.”

“Who pays, then,” said a man near the lamp. He was a tenant, one of the north row, and he had a bad cough that he had had since before the first White Harvest. “We do. We eat it.”

“Yes,” Tova Reed said. “You do.”

“Three.” She did not look at Marek. “I have read the cooperative’s work book, its acceptance column, its seed ledger, its return specifications, and thirty years of hand tallies off a board in a shed. I am not going to say what is in them tonight. I am not going to say it because it is not mine to say first, and because a stranger who opens a cooperative’s books in its own seed house and then tells it what is wrong with it will be believed, and it should not be. Whoever in this room has the standing to say it should say it, and I will write down what they say, in my own hand, in my own book, and send it to Cray whether it helps you or not. And the March’s copy goes out on Thursday, whether it helps you or not, and it goes out with the two days of my own work in it, so that nobody in Cray can later say that the trust only ever heard one side.”

She sat down.

Lissa Vale moved one grain of sand on the table with a finger and looked at nothing.

“Right,” said Dunmar Fitch. “First motion. The disposition of the three trays and the return of the trust’s material. Mr Vale, the chair will hear the cooperative’s position first.”

“I spoiled them,” Marek said.

“Mr Vale—”

“I put the load through a line the trust’s steward had refused in writing and I put it through anyway, and the water came up and it was iron, and three trays are dead, and I will pay for them out of my wages and my mother’s wages and whatever is left after that, and I would like it minuted that I said that in the first thirty seconds and not after somebody had asked me.”

Marek felt the room decide whether to be angry at him or merely disappointed in him, and it went with disappointment, which was worse, because disappointment is what you get from people who expected better and have stopped being surprised.

“Second,” said Dunmar. “The claim in the log.”

And there the shape of the night turned, because that was the motion that mattered and everybody knew it.

“The figure in the acceptance column for six years is wrong,” Dunmar Fitch said, in the voice he used when he was about to be difficult. “I have a loading test, an arrow diagram, and a tape measurement by a person with no reason to be kind to us. I believe it. I am not able to enter it. I am not able to enter *delivered to holder’s ground, six years, overstated by a factor of one and a half* into the cooperative’s book and copy it to the sub-office, because when a hearing officer at Fennmere asks me what cause I have, and I will be asked in the first five minutes, what I am obliged to say is that our own work book says the return branch is forty-one rods and our own tape says it is twenty-two. I would then be presenting the cooperative with a cause that no hearing officer will accept from an unlicensed surveyor, and a charter that is already fifteen days from expiry. I will not put a true number in a book and get us sealed for it.”

“You could enter the cause,” Marek said. “You could enter: *return branch as built falls short of the specification in this cooperative’s own work book; cause of the shortfall not established.* That is a fact. You can stand behind a fact.”

“I can stand behind a fact with a licensed hand behind it,” Dunmar said. “There is not one in this cooperative. There is one in Fennmere who charges money, and two in the capital who do not come out, and that is the whole of the Crown’s answer to a cooperative with no contractor and a date on its charter.”

“Mr Vale.” The man by the door spoke for the first time. He was perhaps sixty, gray, with a good coat and the manner of a person who has delivered other people’s bad news in other people’s parlors. “Bram Oskell. I keep the papers at Kellan Rusk’s pump house in Fennmere. He has sent me up this evening so that you hear it from a person and not from a bird.”

“Mr Oskell,” said Lissa Vale.

“Mrs Vale.” He put a folded paper on the table and did not unfold it. “It is not a demand. It is an offer, and it is a good offer, and Mr Rusk has put it in writing so that nobody can afterward say that it was not offered.”

He unfolded it.

“A new gasket for the well pump, cut and fitted, this week. A proper seal on the wound over the compound, dug out to the scarwood and dressed in new, not dressed over. Both at no charge to the cooperative, at no charge for the next season, and no charge for labour. In exchange, the maintenance contract runs exclusive for two years at the present rate, and the renewal of the charter carries the same term, and the cooperative’s acceptance column is confirmed for the six years already logged.”

The room made a noise that was not a word.

“There is a second page,” Oskell said. “The second page is the schedule of the work, with the dates, so that you can see it is not a promise. Six days. He can have a man in your pit on the fourth day.”

Nobody spoke. Marek watched the second page land on the table and watched two of the north row understand it before the rest of the room did, and understood it, from the way their shoulders came down, as the end of a thing they had been frightened of for six years.

Marek looked at Tova Reed.

She had not touched the paper. She was looking at it the way you look at a lever on a machine you have just been shown the drawing of.

“Mr Oskell,” she said. “Say the second part of the second page.”

“There is no second part of the second page.”

“There is. You have written *subject to acceptance of the recorded maintenance, 6 seasons* at the bottom, and you have written it in a smaller hand than the rest, and you read the whole of a document aloud when you deliver it, and you have just stopped.”

Oskell looked at her for a moment with an expression that might have been respect.

“Subject to acceptance of the recorded maintenance for six seasons,” he said. “The cooperative confirms that the maintenance carried out in those six seasons was as logged, and that the log entries are correct.”

“He is offering you a proper seal,” Tova Reed said to the room, “and he is not offering it for the wound. He is offering it for the log. If you accept, then on Thursday morning the cooperative’s own charter clerk will sign a statement that the six years of figures were correct, and every one of them was not, and the signature will be made by a charter clerk who is an officer of this cooperative and not a bystander, and the statement will be filed in a book that a Crown man can copy, and it will be true in the way that a document is true.”

“Because he will do the work for nothing,” said the man with the cough. “Gasket. Seal. Six days.”

“Because he will do the work for nothing *in exchange for a signature*,” Tova Reed said, “and I have been in this room for one day and I have already learned that the cooperative has no money, and I am telling you that the shape of this offer is the shape of every offer you will be given for the next twenty days, and it is the shape that finishes you. He will fix a wound that is one thing, and buy a piece of paper that is another, and the second is worth more to him than the first is to you, because the second is about the six years in front of me and about everything before them, and I have read a report on the shape of these offers that is nineteen years old and is about the Salt March, and I will not say more about that in this room.”

“Nineteen years?” Marek said.

“I am not a lawyer and I am not accusing him of anything,” Tova Reed said. “I am a seed keeper and I have watched a great many people accept a free thing.”

Lissa Vale spoke for the first time in nine minutes.

“He is right,” she said, to Marek, flatly, without looking at him. “And you have known it since the yard, and you have been standing in this seed house for an hour letting a man from another county say it in front of these people. That is not modesty. That is letting somebody else do your work in a room where you are the one who signed the column four times.”

“Mother—”

“I am not going to be crossed with.” She turned back to the table. “Mr Oskell. Tell Mr Rusk the answer. The cooperative declines the two-year exclusive extension. The cooperative declines to confirm the recorded maintenance for six seasons. The cooperative declines the gasket and the seal, and it declines them free, and it declines them today, and I will put the refusal in this evening’s minute in my own hand and Mr Fitch will enter it, and it will go down the road with you tonight.”

“Mrs Vale,” Oskell said, respectfully, and then: “May I ask, for a man who keeps papers, what is the cooperative going to seal the wound with?”

“Nothing that anyone has offered us,” Lissa said.

Oskell put the papers away, unhurried, and looked at the twenty people for a while with the expression of a man doing arithmetic of his own.

“There is one more thing and then I am going down the road in the dark, which I do not advise.” He said it to the middle of the table, not to Marek. “Mr Rusk asked me to mention it, and I am not sure why, and I have thought about it on the road and I have decided to mention it anyway. If the cooperative wants a licensed hand, there is one within a week’s ride. Quill. Sera Quill. She has a piece of work at the salt cistern past the water-meadow and she will be on the fen road on the sixth day going out, and she will be on it coming back on the seventh unless the cistern goes badly. She is not cheap. She is not expensive either. She is exact.”

“Exact how?” Marek said.

“She will not come without a written scope and she will not sign anything she has not read twice,” Oskell said, “and she has ruined two pumping houses in this district for signing something she had not read twice, and neither of them was hers, which is the part people remember.”

The door was cold when he went out.

The room broke up into the argument it had been holding since he left, which was about whether the fourth-year man had a wage, and which Marek now understood had nothing to do with the fourth-year man. Lissa went to check the trays. Tova Reed stayed at the table with the seed house book open, writing.

Marek put his hand on the back of his own chair.

“Mr Fitch,” he said. “Before anybody goes to bed. One more thing, and it is not a motion.”

“I have a book full of things that are not motions, Mr Vale.”

“Open the figures. The draw figures, the log, the acceptance column, the work book page with the return branch on it, and my loading test. All of it. Open to any member of this cooperative, any stranger, and any clerk of the sub-office who asks. Not copied out. Open. The board on the mill-keeper’s shed stays where it is and anybody can walk out and read it.”

Dunmar Fitch put the quill down.

“That is not a motion either, because a motion has to say who is affected and how long it lasts and what happens when somebody comes with a Crown badge and asks for a page.”

“Then I will say it as a standing act of this meeting and let the clerk find the words.” Marek put his hand flat on the table next to the log. “And I will put my own page in with it. Mine is the loading test from this afternoon. It says I loaded a line I was refused the use of, four strokes past the refusal, and it says the stop was made by hand and not by the pump. It is my hand, it is my error, and it goes in the same book as Kellan’s six years, on the same page, in the same open book, and if somebody comes with a badge they can read both of mine and both of his in the same afternoon.”

The room went quiet in the particular way that a room goes quiet when everybody has understood that the man offering this is going to be the one who suffers for it.

“Mr Vale,” said the man with the cough, from the north row. “You are opening your own figures.”

“Yes.”

“Alongside his.”

“Alongside his.”

The man with the cough looked at the lamp for a while, and then said, “Then somebody ought to be thanked for that,” which was not quite a thing anybody had said, and which nobody took up, because it would have been a kindness and nobody in the room could afford one tonight.

Dunmar Fitch wrote for a long time.

“Open book,” he said at last, “on the seed house table, from the sixth day, until the charter is settled either way. Mr Vale’s loading test is entered as part of it. Entered as what, Mr Vale? I have to write a word.”

“Enter it as what it is,” Marek said. “A loading test I ran without the consent the trust’s steward had refused in writing, on a circuit she had a veto over, on the third day of her audit, which stopped the water and spoiled three trays of the March’s last dry-land line.”

The quill stopped.

“That is longer than a minute would take,” Dunmar said.

“It is a true minute,” Marek said. “Write it.”

He watched Dunmar write it, all of it, in the flat unhurried hand of a man who is going to be cross-examined on the contents one day in a cold room in Fennmere, and then he went out into the yard and shut the seed house door behind him.

The coupling was still in pieces on the floor of the pump shed where it had fallen, and he picked the pieces up and put them on the bench, and there was nothing he could do with them. The pad over the wound was four seasons old and had been dressed for the last time an hour before he spoiled three trays of somebody else’s seed, and the crew that dressed it was on its way down the road, and there was no contractor, and no extension, and no money, and a charter with a date on it, and a licensed root engineer on the fen road in three days’ time who would not come without a written scope and who had ruined two pumping houses for signing something she had not read twice.
