# Chapter 5

## The Woman With the Old Book

The open book had been on the seed house table since dawn and by the time the light came properly nobody had come to read it.

Marek had spent the fourth and fifth days in the seed house in a way that would have been funny to anyone who liked him, reading other people's ledgers, and on the fifth night he had sat on the step with the wrapped kit across his knees and worked out, on the back of a requisition, the exact sequence in which he was going to fail.

On the sixth morning he walked out to the fen road at first light with the requisition in his coat, because Oskell had said she would be on it going out, and because the road to the salt cistern was the one piece of ground in Tarrow Fen where Sera Quill could not avoid walking.

He met her at the third gate, half a mile east of the cooperative, in the flat gray light with the reed beds on both sides and the fen coming up behind him in a long low breath of cold. She was walking on the left-hand verge the way people do who have spent their life in a trade with a right of way, and she was carrying a stick and a flat leather case and a canvas roll, and she had stopped when she saw him coming, and she had not gone back to being surprised.

"Vale," she said.

Marek had not expected her to know his name, and it took him a second to decide what to do with that. There were four Vale's in the fen and she had said it the way you say a thing you have thought about.

"Sera Quill."

"You are the surveyor at the cooperative with the well that does not balance." She did not make it a question. "I have a piece of work at the salt cistern past the water-meadow, and I shall be there until dark today and I do not stop for anything on the way, and I am telling you that so that you can spend the walk preparing whatever it is instead of trying to be impressive in the first hundred yards."

"I have a scope in writing," Marek said, and put his hand on his coat pocket and then took it off again. "I wrote it out. I want you to read it and change it and tell me what it should say, and then I will pay whatever you say, and I want to be paid before you do anything, and I want you to know before you look at anything that I am not licensed for any of the work we would be talking about."

Sera Quill stopped walking.

That was how it happened. She had been prepared to be asked for a favor, or money, or a ride, or a story about the fen, and he had handed her a list of his own deficiencies instead, and it had taken her off her stride for the length of one step.

"Say the rest of it," she said.

"You trained my father."

"Yes."

"Eight years ago he went into a deep relay under this holding and the shaft came in on him. You trained him before that. You have not been in this fen since, or I would have heard."

"I have not been in this fen since the week after he died," Sera Quill said. "I have papers. I will not show them to you and I will not tell you where they came from, and if you try to buy them I will be unpleasant. Write that on your scope so we do not both waste an afternoon."

"That is fair."

"The other thing I will say now, because it is relevant and because I would have said it inside two hours anyway, and I would rather you had it in the cold with nobody watching." She shifted the canvas roll off her other shoulder. "There was a branch cut on this fen on Crown money eight years ago. Not on a charter holder's money. On the district's. While your father was in the ground. I have spent six years failing to find out by whom, and I have failed it in three separate offices, and I have stopped saying the name in a room with two people in it."

"Where was the branch cut?"

"I do not know."

"Which branch was cut?"

"I do not know that either."

"Then what do you know?"

"That it was cut," Sera Quill said, "and that it was done by somebody with the authority to schedule it, and that your father was standing in the middle of it when it happened, and that the finding against him said a cave-in."

Marek found that he had turned to look at the fen and had forgotten the fen.

"Why tell me this on a road?"

"Because you have a well that does not balance and a load test in a ledger, and because in about two days I am going to open your well head, and because I would rather you were not surprised by what I have got in my case when I put it on the table." She held his eye. "I am not offering you a mystery, Mr Vale. I am telling you not to let a man in a bad coat tell you that your father's death was weather."

She started walking, and after a dozen paces she slowed until he was beside her, which was consent of a kind.

"Read me your scope," she said.

Marek did not have the scope. He had a requisition and a list and the back of four days of not sleeping. He stood in the cold on the fen road and assembled it out loud as he went, which was the worst version of doing it and the only one available.

"A well head at the Lantern Reach cooperative, running on a charter that expires on the eighteenth. The head draws eleven hundred turns a day and the charter requires a maintained node, which means dated records, licensed hands and a return path that works. I want the condition certified, in writing, as maintained or not maintained. I have found the return branch as built falls short of the cooperative's own specification, and I cannot certify a return that falls short, and I cannot prove the shortfall with a licensed hand, because I do not have one. I have a wounded conduit under a compound pad that is not a seal. I have a six-year log with a false column in it and no licensed reader. And the fee — say the fee before I tell you I can pay it, because I have not added it up in four years and I would rather not be wrong in front of you."

"Honest," Sera Quill said. "Go on. The fee is fixed by the schedule. Two days at the head, two nights, a written finding, a signature, and my travel from Fennmere, which the schedule does not cover because the schedule assumes the Crown sends somebody. Fourteen pounds. It does not include the second visit, and if I have to come back it is another fourteen, and men always assume the second visit is free."

"I can pay fourteen pounds. I can pay it today. It is money I have not spent in four years and it is money I earned by leaving this fen when I should not have left it, so it will be easy."

Sera Quill laughed. It was not a kind sound. "That is the worst arithmetic I have heard this year, including yours at the seed house. You have just told a stranger you have saved money by going somewhere you were not wanted, and I do not know yet whether you did it for money or for something else, and neither, from the look of you, do you. Fourteen pounds, Mr Vale. Pay it to me and not to the cooperative, because if the cooperative pays there is a document and a document has an opinion in it. And I am not paid in seed, and I am not paid in a favor, and I am not paid in being told about my own past at a table. Money I can be angry at. Do you have a scope?"

"I will have a scope in four days."

"No, you will have it in two days and you will read it to me now, and I will write my objections on it, and you will sign where I tell you to sign. And here are my terms, so you can put them in it before we arrive."

"The first: your raw notes come to me. Not copies. Yours, the page you wrote at two o'clock on the third day, with the four strokes past the refusal in it. I do not want the version you will make later when you have slept. I want the ugly one and I want it in my hand within a week."

"The second: I supervise the logging myself, and you do not touch the instrument without me in the room. Whatever is in that kit of yours, you do not put a hand on it while I am not looking. If you put a hand on something and it does a thing and you cannot account for the thing, we have no record, and I will not sign a page that is only true because you believed it."

"The third: I sign what I have seen. Not what you have. Not what the cooperative needs. And when I sign, my license is behind it and my name is on it, and if I am wrong about a well I have ruined two pumping houses in this district and I will do it again in front of a district officer, because that is the entire difference between a licensed trade and a man with a gauge and an opinion."

Marek stopped walking. "That is not a fee. That is a risk."

"It is a fee," Sera Quill said. "It is the only part of a fee that matters. If your well is sound, this costs you fourteen pounds and you have a document. If your well is not sound, this costs you fourteen pounds and the district has a licensed engineer who looked at it and will say so, and you cannot un-see that either. Every honest man is a threat to somebody. Stop looking shocked and write it down."

So Marek Vale stood on the third gate of the fen road in the gray morning and wrote down the terms of Sera Quill on the back of a requisition, in his own hand, and then read them back to her, and she made him read them back twice, and then she signed the page as witness to the terms and not as witness to the work, and gave it back.

"There is one more thing," she said, when it was folded into his coat, "and I am going to say it now instead of at the gate tomorrow morning, because you are about to spend two nights deciding whether to bring it, and a man who has spent two nights on it should have an answer by the time he asks."

"You have a book in that kit and you have mentioned it twice in a man's voice, which is how men talk about things they expect to be asked for. I am not going to ask you for it. I want you to know that I know about it, and that I have not asked, and that when I do ask — and I will ask, Mr Vale, because a well head that does not balance and a dead engineer under it and a log with a false column are three things and not two, and I do not have the third one yet — I will ask it in front of witnesses and I will write down what it does. Bring it or do not bring it. Do not bring it half. A thing that has been wrapped up for eight years is not evidence of anything except that its owner has been frightened of it, and a frightened man is not a witness, whatever is in the wrap."

Marek walked back the way he had come, with the fen behind him and the reed beds going white along the top, and the requisition folded into his coat in two places because he had read it four times on the way out.

"Two days of logging," he said. "Why two? The well head will tell me what it is going to tell me in a morning."

"No it will not," Sera Quill said, and then, after a moment, in a different voice, as if the professional one had said more than she had authorized: "Because it does not do it on a schedule. I have seen wells like yours do one thing at noon and another at midnight, and a man who logs for a morning writes down a morning. Two days is the shortest span in which a pattern can be distinguished from a coincidence, and I do not care what the pattern is, I only care that when I sign my name under a number it is a number and not an impression."

She turned up the cistern road, which branches off the fen road a quarter mile up, and they stood at the branch a while longer because neither of them had said goodbye and it seemed worse to walk away than to stand.

"Have it open before I get there," she called back. "The collar, the housing, the whole of it. And have somebody who can be trusted in the room for the whole of both days, because you are not going to be the only witness in that pit and I am not one of your cooperative."

The walk back to the cooperative took an hour and a half, and Marek did most of it without thinking about the fee.

The two days of logging needed two days of pressure, and he had one afternoon's loading test with his own error written into it, and the coupling was in pieces on the pump shed bench where the coupling could not be replaced before the eighteenth, and the compound pad over the wound had been dressed for the last time three days ago by a crew that was on its way out of the fen and would not come back. There was no contractor, no extension, and no money in the cooperative account but a great deal of money in his own, which was the wrong way round.

He was going to have to bring the kit.

He got to the seed house at noon and told his mother what he had signed, and Lissa Vale put down the shears she was using and looked at him for a while.

"Fourteen pounds," she said.

"Fourteen pounds. Personally. The cooperative can reimburse what it can after the charter settles, and if it does not settle, then it was fourteen pounds I was going to lose anyway and I would rather it went on a licensed signature than on a new gasket."

"You would rather it went on a licensed signature than on a new gasket," Lissa said, "and the new gasket is what keeps your well from failing under the cooperative's feet in February. Do not dress that up. You have spent the winter's wages on an opinion. I will not pay it back and I will not pretend you did not."

"No," Marek said.

"All right." She picked the shears up. "Two days. I will find you two people who can be in that pit and be trusted and not talk, and I will not be one of them, because if I stand in that pit for two days I will say something at the end of it that thirty years of keeping seed does not entitle me to say, and then it will be in somebody's book forever."

She snipped a dry leaf off a tray and threw it away.

"Gael will do. She will keep her mouth shut because she wants this to be real more than she wants to be important, which is a great deal rarer than it sounds."
