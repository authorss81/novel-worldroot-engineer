# Chapter 6

## Measured Water

Sera Quill arrived at the well head at seven on the seventh day with a sheet of paper, a leather case, and a boy carrying two sacks that smelled from a mile off.

"Compost," she said, before Marek could offer a greeting. "Sheep, six weeks turned, from a farm at the water-meadow who has never met me and took cash. Two hundred hundredweight. It goes into the return main tonight, and it is logged, and the log is dated, and if the cooperative's book is going to be open then the open book starts with the first thing anybody spent money on."

"Who is paying for that?" Dunmar Fitch said.

"You are. No — listen to me before you say no, Mr Fitch, because you will say no and then say it again in a letter. This well head has a wounded conduit about two hands long under four seasons of tar. I am going to log that conduit for two days. In two days, a wounded conduit that is not being given anything back will be further along than when I started, and when I sign my name under its condition I will have signed it about a wound I made worse in order to measure it. Nobody can be asked to take that from me. So the return goes in."

"There is no money for two hundred hundredweight of compost," Dunmar said, "and there is no money for the fee either, and both of those facts are going into the same book."

"The compost is mine," Sera Quill said. "Two hundred hundredweight at four shillings is forty pounds, which is three fees, and I am not being generous, I am being consistent. You will pay me fourteen pounds at the gate and the cooperative's open book will open with a line that says the compost came off my own money and the return went in on the seventh day, and that line is worth more to you than the four pounds it would have cost you to buy it yourselves, because a document with a document inside it is a document with an opinion inside it. And if the cooperative reimburses the fee, then the cooperative may have opinions about what the fee bought. I have thought about that for a whole morning and I am telling you that I would rather be argued with than wrong." She looked at Marek. "And I have thought about whether to make the argument for you. Mr Vale, listen. Three things, or you do nothing at all. Something alive to hold on to. A way through that will carry it. And something given back. A man can do the second one with wire and the third one with money, and if he only has the second and the third he has plumbing, and plumbing on a living thing is a way of making a corpse out of it. I have watched it happen twice. If you cannot put three of them on this well head, I will leave today and you will keep the fourteen pounds and I will have wasted a day, and you would have been better off."

"There is an anchor," Marek said. "The conduit in the housing. It is alive. It pushed back when I had my arm in."

"Then the anchor is free and it is already yours. The path is a problem, and the return is a problem, and the problems are mine, and you are not to help me with them, because if I hand you the path I will be supervising something I built, and I do not certify my own work."

She worked for two hours.

The kit came out of the oilcloth in front of the whole crew on the first morning, because Sera Quill would not have it in a bag in a pit with a man in it. The strap had gone stiff after eight years and Marek had to work it loose with a thumbnail while Gael Senn held the lamp, and the front board came open on the boards of the pump shed, and the bead in its copper collar sat in the middle of it about the size of a hazel nut, dull and dark, and nobody put a hand near it.

The path was a copper contact clip — a small, ugly, hand-made thing of two turned collars on a bent bar, the kind of tool a man makes for himself and does not sell. She fitted one collar to the copper seam at the housing, where the collar's ring met the conduit's ferrule, and she made Marek hold the other and told him where to put it and made him put it there himself, and then she tested the contact with a plain wire gauge and got a reading and wrote the reading down, and it was the first entry in the whole two days that was not Marek's. "There," she said. "Now say all three of them back to me, so that they are written down twice. Anchor: that bead, and the conduit under it, and both of them alive, and if either of them stops being alive then this stops being an instrument and becomes a piece of wood with a memory in it. Path: that clip and that seam, and nothing else, and I fitted it, and I am the one who will have to answer for it. Return: two hundred hundredweight of compost into the return main, logged, dated and paid for, every bucket of it going back into something that is still running. Three. No two."

"For the return, you are going to be careful with your own hands and you are going to be worse with them than usual," she said. "I have found that reading costs people. Not reading. The day after. Two of my students have told me a thing that could not have been in the book they had opened, and one of them told me a date that had not happened yet, and both of them were young men of about twenty-five who would sooner have cut their own hand off. Log long, log dull, and if anything comes through that instrument that is not a pressure, you put the instrument down and walk away from it and let me write it up. That is not fear, Mr Vale. That is procedure, and I will not sign anything that was obtained by a man who went on after he had gone wrong."

Gael Senn was the witness Lissa Vale had found. She came down to the pit at seven with a notebook of her own and a bad coat and the settled expression of a young woman who has decided that being frightened of something is a poor reason to be absent from it.

"For the lamp," Marek said, "I need somebody to feed the rootlight bulbs. Not you, you are watching. I need somebody who knows what the lamps are."

"I know what the lamps are," Gael said.

"Then feed them. Every one. Write down the time and how much water you gave them and how much compost. If they go dim, write that down too and do not fix it without telling me."

Two days, and the pump running and stopping on a schedule Sera wrote out herself, and the collar open, and the pit roped, and Marek on a board over the water with a brass gauge on his knee and a notebook and a pencil that had to be sharpened every hour.

He had expected the work to be boring. The first day was the most boring two days of his working life and it was exactly what she had ordered. Pressure at rest, quarter-hourly. Pressure under a light draw, quarter-hourly. Pressure under a full draw. Temperature at the head, at the collar, and in the sump. Water at the main, at the return, and in the sump. The sound of the pump. The sound of the wind in the willow windbreak. The state of the compound pad at four fixed points, drawn to scale in a grid he had made with chalk on the cradle, so that in a year somebody could come back and compare.

And through all of it, the two needles.

The brass gauge said what brass gauges say, and it did not lie; it was an honest instrument on an honest tap and the water above the tap was doing what the water above the tap does. But the small needle in the fieldbook, lying open on a board at Marek's knee with the oilcloth folded back and the copper clip's bar laid through it, moved differently. Not wildly. Not enough to point at anything. It sat perhaps half a division to the left of the brass at noon on the first day, and by the end of the second day it sat a full division to the right of it, and neither instrument had been moved, cleaned, or knocked, and Marek checked both of them against each other so often on the second day that Gael Senn began to read the times aloud to him so that he would not have to look away from the dial to write.

"Do not reconcile them," Sera Quill said, at the second hour of the second afternoon, without looking up from her own page. "You will not get a reading in this pit that reconciles two instruments, because you do not know that they are reading the same thing. Write down what each one says at the same minute. Let the difference be a thing in the book. If you make them agree you will have done arithmetic on the only two witnesses in this pit and one of them will no longer be a witness."

"Two o'clock," Gael said.

Marek wrote: 14:00. Brass, 3/8 under the red. Small, 1/2 under. Difference, one eighth and growing.

At four o'clock on the second day the difference was nearly a division, and at the same moment the pump noise changed.

It did not fail. It did something Marek had never heard it do: the whole four-foot frame of the well house hummed, and the hum went up and down through four or five of those slow beats in a space of about half a minute, and then stopped, and the whole structure of the housing went tight and quiet, and the pit water lay still.

Sera Quill looked up from her page.

"That is not the pump," she said.

"It is off the pump."

"It is the housing. Listen." She went absolutely still. "That is a pressure change coming up the pipe and going through the frame, and it is not a pump, and it has a beat, and it is coming up from underneath the water in the pit. Mr Vale, that is the fourth one since noon and I have got them all. Go and get the pressure off the sump tap, off the bottom, quickly, and I want the small one on it too, and I want you to take both readings at once, at this exact minute, and I do not care what your two instruments say about each other."

She sent Gael out first — to the sump tap at the bottom of the pit with the second dial, to take a reading at the same minute as the one at the head, because a pulse that arrives from below can only be found by two people at once — and then went out through the seed house door herself, because the second dial was in the well house and she had said she would fetch it herself.

Gael Senn came up the ladder with the dial in her hand and went down the outside of the pit on the step, because there was only one ladder and Marek was on it, and the pump started while she was on the step.

The pump noise came back and covered the door and covered the yard and covered everything.

And Marek Vale, alone on a board over four feet of iron-colored water with a clip he was not supposed to touch and a well he had been logging for two days, put his hand on the clip, and thought: *hold it long enough to be worth writing down and then get off it.*

He got them.

He did not see anything. Nothing looked back. There was no room and no face and nobody in the thing saying anything to him or to anybody. There was a set of hands, and they were his hands, in a body that was his body and was not his body, and they did four things in order and he was each pair of hands as it did them. It was work. He understood that inside the first two seconds and understood it better by the fourth, because he had done every one of these four things with his own hands in a pit, and a thing done in a pit by a man who knows the job does not need a face to be legible.

The left hand went onto a valve wheel and turned it a quarter and held it, and the arm knew the wheel's size without being told, and the wheel was cold and the cold of it was in the shoulder. The right hand went flat onto a rail and stayed there for a length of time he could not count. Then the left hand went back to the wheel and turned it three quarters and stopped, and the right hand went up to something — a lamp, a hook, a chain — and came down again. Then the left hand shut the wheel all the way, and the valve closed, and everything went quiet and slightly rang.

Underneath that, much larger and much older and not his at all, there was a rhythm in the water. It had been there the whole two days and he had written it down in a column he had not understood. It was a pulse, one beat in about four seconds, slow as a man breathing asleep, coming up under the pit the way the sun comes up under a door.

And inside it, between the second thing and the third, as though the shaft had been there the whole while and he had been working down it: a place. Cold, narrow, the walls sweating. A lamp on a chain throwing one moving edge of light. A wheel on a stand, and hands on it — his hands, older, with a torn cuff, turning something through an arc and stopping it, and stopping it, the same four things in the same order, over and over, patient, over a time that had no day in it at all and was not waiting for him.

Then it stopped and Marek was on a board over iron water in a fen in the darkening afternoon with blood running out of his left nostril onto the back of his hand.

The needle in the fieldbook was not where the brass needle was. He was not sure it was where it had been.

> The head is sound.  
> The cut is upstream, on ground you do not keep.  
> Write it down and sign it.

He put the clip on the board. He wiped his nose on his sleeve. He did not call for anybody, because the pump noise was still up and because he had a professional decision to make and he made it in about two seconds.

He picked up the pencil and he wrote on the page of the open notebook, in the middle of the two days' columns, in his own hand:

*15:52. Entry not from an instrument. Stopped session.*

And then, underneath: *Session terminated by me. Reason: witness withdrawn to the sump, and operator unfit to continue. Three findings follow. I am writing them now with nobody in the pit at all, which is the worst condition there is and the reason I am writing them in this order before she gets back.*

The pump noise dropped. In the doorway Sera Quill stood with a second dial in one hand and a folded paper in the other, looking at him over the pit, and Marek understood that she had been standing in that doorway through the whole of it.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat down on the edge of the pit.

She came down the ladder past him, set the dial on the board, and looked at the notebook open on his knee. Then she took out a white-handled brush from her case and dipped it in clean water and gave him his face, one wipe at a time, and did not ask him a single question about what had happened for the space of about a minute, and Marek was grateful for it in a way he had not expected to be grateful for anything that week.

"Now," she said. "You have blood on your cuff and a page with three findings written on it in a hand I have not read, and you stopped the session. Say the reason first."

"Three," Marek said. His mouth was dry and his head felt packed. "The operator was unfit to continue. I have had a nosebleed, I have lost about a minute I cannot account for, and I do not trust what is on that page. I will write it down anyway. That is the point. That is what you told me to do."

"You did exactly what I told you to do," Sera Quill said, "and I have trained four hundred men and two of them did it, and one of them died of something else afterward, and one of them is a seedwright forty miles north of here who is going to be extremely inconvenient to somebody in the capital. You are not going to be able to do it again. That is not me being frightened. That is what happens to the instrument and the man both."

"I know," Marek said.

"What is it called?"

"It has a name and you will not want it, and I am going to say it anyway, because a man who is told the name can look for it later and a man who is not told it cannot. It is called a memory bleed. It is a name, not a diagnosis, and it is older than either of us, and it is the reason the Assembly makes me log every single thing that happens in a pit in front of a witness and then makes me read it back. Get some sleep."

"Write the findings." She took the pencil out of his hand, looked at the point, gave it back. "In the form I am going to read and sign. A maintenance pattern, a date, a method, and a witness. Not a voice, not a message, not a man speaking to you from anywhere. If you write one word of that in here I will not sign it and I will tell you why for four hours."

Marek wrote.

*Finding 1. The well head, the pump, the collar, the housing and the cradle are sound. Pump achieved 48/48 on a loaded test on 14:40 of the second day, head pressure holding at 3/8 under the mark. Consequence: the fault is not in the well head and is not in the machine.*

*Finding 2. The node carries a recurring pressure pulse of about one beat in four seconds, arriving from below the sump and entering the frame. It came singly all day on the first day. On the second afternoon it came in four events, of two, three, four and five beats, at the times noted, and the first event was not felt at all at the head and was only in the sump reading. Not accounted for by weather, by draw, by the pump, or by the compost return. Not identified in the course of this session, and no inference is to be drawn from it.*

*Finding 3. Under the conditions above, and with the fieldbook's living bead in contact with the well's living conduit through a copper clip at the housing seam, the operator experienced four seconds of structured sensation consistent with a working sequence — valve, hand, wait, valve, lamp, valve — laid over the well's own older pressure rhythm. He cannot separate his own hands from another pair of hands. He cannot state whether the sequence was the well's, the book's, or his. It is logged as UNVERIFIED. It is not evidence of anything until a second licensed reader has read the same node through a separate anchor and two independent records agree.*

He read it out. He had written it in the flatest hand he could manage and it took him longer than any page he had ever written in his life.

Sera Quill read it twice.

"You have already changed my word," she said. "Finding two says *not identified in the course of this session*, and I did not tell you to change it. I had meant to say it myself, at the bottom of the page, with my hand over the top of yours, because a licensed engineer who puts a phrase in an operator's mouth owns it. Instead you corrected me toward the more careful phrase on your own, at a moment when you were the least fit to be careful about anything." She turned the page around and read it. "That is the only good thing that has happened in this pit today and I am going to have to lean on it for the rest of this season. I am not going to have to be disappointed about it once. Not even once."

"Now the date." She did not reach for it. "I am not opening that book in a pit. I told you on a road that when I ask, I ask in front of witnesses, and there is one in this yard and she has a step to come up. You will read it out loud to me from where you are sitting, and I will write down that I took it from the operator and not from the instrument, and if anybody ever asks me what that book contains they can have this page and not my hands."

Marek turned the book on his knee so she could see the page, and did not turn it toward her, and in the bottom margin of the fieldbook's remaining eleven sheets, in a hand he had known since he was six years old, there was a line of figures.

*Seventeen. Set in the low. Eleven. Watch the fourth beat in the small hours.*

And beneath it, in the same hand, a column head and a number: *confirmed — set — 4.*

And a year. A year eight months after his father had gone into the ground and not come up.

"It is my father's hand," Marek said.

"Yes. And it is dated after he died, which means one of three things and I do not know which, and I have thought about it and here they are. It is a copying error. It is a date for a different season written in the wrong column. Or somebody wrote in his hand who wanted a record that looked like his, and there is a fourth thing which is that it is his hand and the record is about work he did after he was dead, and I am not going to be the one who says that out loud in a fen in the eighth day of a bad season, and neither are you."

"Then it is a copying error," Marek said. "Write it down as a copying error. Treat it as one. Put it in the finding. *The date in the last column is unverified and is probably a copying error; it is to be treated as one and no inference is to be drawn from it.*"

Sera Quill looked at him.

"You want me to write that."

"Yes. I want it in the finding because then it cannot be used by anybody, including me, and it cannot be used by me especially, because in about four days I am going to want to believe that column very badly."

She wrote it. She signed underneath it and made him witness her signature, and then she turned to the notebook.

"Your raw notes."

"Here." He had them ready. He had them ready because he had spent the whole four seconds thinking about them. Nine pages, two days, quarter-hourly, in his hand, including the page with the four strokes past the refusal on the third day, and including the page where he had written, in the middle of a column of numbers, *small needle vs brass, difference growing, do not reconcile, per witness.*

Sera Quill put them inside her case and buttoned it, and Marek felt something go out of him that he had not realized he was holding up.

"From this minute they are mine as well as yours," she said. "If you change a page now I will know, and if you want something back you come and ask me for it in front of a witness, and if I say no you do not take it. Yes?"

"Yes."

"Now look at your own arm."

Marek pushed his sleeve back. There was nothing on it. He had expected — he did not know what he had expected, some mark, some line, something that would mean the four seconds had been a thing that had happened to him rather than a thing that had happened near him.

"There is nothing," he said.

"There is nothing," Sera Quill agreed, "and I want you to look at it twice a week for a year and tell me the first time it is not nothing, and I want it in your own book, not in mine, because I will be dead or retired and you will not. And Mr Vale — it will not stay that way. Four hundred men, and there is not one of them who touched that bead twice and had nothing to show for it. That is not me frightening you. That is a trade fact, and I would write it on the wall of my own shed if I still had one."

"No," Marek said. "It will not."

"I have watched four hundred men handle those books and I have buried two of them." She got herself back up the ladder with the case under her arm. "Now. Log the last hour. Write the pressure at the sump, the pressure at the head, the temperature, and the hour. And then we will go up and you will tell me the entire sequence out loud, in order, from the first thing your left hand did to the last thing it did, and if you leave anything out I will ask you about the gap, and I will keep asking you about the gap until you have either found it or admitted it is not there."

Marek logged the last hour. It took forty minutes and he wrote every number twice.

Then they went up out of the cold, and Sera Quill sent Gael Senn to bring the compost in and log it, and Marek sat on the well house step with the oilcloth folded back beside him and the fieldbook open on his knees to a page that was not his father's, and tried very hard to work out what a man is supposed to do with an instrument that has just told him something true that he cannot prove and that he cannot repeat.

Because it had told him the head was sound. He had known that from his own arm a week ago and now two days of dated numbers said it as well, in a form that a clerk could copy and an inspector could check and a hearing officer could not wave away.

And it had told him the fault was upstream. Somewhere above Lantern Reach, above the fen, above ground that was not in the cooperative's book and not in the charter and not in any map that had ever been nailed to the seed house wall, somebody had cut a branch.

Marek had grown up on this holding. He had walked its ditches as a boy. He could not have told you, if you had put a lamp in front of him, where a branch was supposed to be.
