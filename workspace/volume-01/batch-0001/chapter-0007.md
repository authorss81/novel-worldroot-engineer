# Chapter 7

## Who Cuts a Branch

The charter’s fourth schedule put Weir Row and the old branch above it under a maintenance order, and the cooperative had never once exercised it, because the members who could remember their grandfathers all said the same thing: that branch was abandoned before I was born.

Marek read the schedule in the seed house on the seventh night with a lamp and a cold cup of tea and went over it three times.

Under a maintenance order meant: the ground was the charter holder’s, and the cooperative had no standing on it at all. Going over the weir was not trespass against a stranger. It was trespass against their own charter, in writing, in a schedule any inspector could produce, and it was exactly the kind of unregistered work that a certain clause existed for.

He sat with that. Then he wrote in the back of the notebook, in the page where a man keeps the things he is not yet prepared to do: *weir line, day 9, two of us, daylight, take nothing off, cut nothing, photograph nothing because we have no such thing, mark where we step, back off at dusk.*

That was all. It was not a plan. It was a man writing down the reason he was about to do something.

Ilsa Kern walked him out to the weir in the morning because the weir was an embankment and the embankments were hers, and she carried a bar and a spade and did not go past the second post.

“The stop logs have been down since before I came here,” she said. “The plank is sound. If the north post is loose do not lean on it, it will put you in the race.”

“I am not going to lean on anything.”

“You are going to lean on something,” Ilsa said. “You always do. It is only ever the rail.”

“Ilsa.”

“I am going to milk at ten and I am coming back at two.” She shouldered the spade. “Do not be clever. Tell me what you are standing on.”

The day was long and gray and smelled of coming rain. The fen road ran out at the weir and did not resume on the other side; beyond it was a cart track along the top of the old embankment with water grass on the crown and a drop on the fen side that would put a man in up to his chest in two places.

Gael Senn walked half a pace behind him all the way with her notebook under her arm and, at the first gate, said: “You said you wanted me to come and learn.”

“I did.”

“You said it in a voice you use when you are about to do something you have already decided about.”

“That is also true,” Marek said. “Both of those can be the case. I want you to come and learn, and I am not going to tell you what I think I am going to find, because you are twenty-three and you have known me since you were nine and I would like one day to find out whether you would have followed me up here if I had told you the whole thing.”

Gael thought about that for about a minute.

“That was nasty,” she said. “And fair. All right.”

They left the cart and the wheel ruts. The wheel ruts were about eight feet back from the crown.

That was the first fact, and Marek wrote it down before he had gone twenty yards, because a rut that should be on the crown and is eight feet back on the berm is a rut made by somebody who was keeping well away from something. The berm was soft going. The crown was hard. The crown was what a loaded cart wanted. Somebody had been driving along the berm, close to the drop, eight feet out, for long enough to wear two ruts a cart wide.

Nobody drives a cart on the berm unless the crown is not available.

Marek stopped and looked along the top of the embankment at the fifth post, which was leaning a little because the berm had gone soft under it, and where a Crown sign was nailed at about the height of a man’s shoulder.

It was old. The tar had gone gray and had the fine crazing that old tar gets when it has been in weather for thirty years, and it had been nailed over twice, because there were four nail holes in a rectangle and only two nails in the timber. Marek came close and read it.

**BRANCH MAINTENANCE — DISTRICT SCHEDULE.**  
**NO LOCAL HOLDER.**  
**REPORT CUTS TO THE SUB-OFFICE, FENNMERE.**

No local holder. A district schedule. Marek had read the charter’s schedules for six years and had never once looked at a sign, because a sign is not a document and a document is what a hearing takes.

He read it four times and then copied it out word for word into the notebook, with the date, and then stood in the wet grass looking up the fen at nothing at all, because the answer to the whole season had just been nailed to a post eight feet behind where it ought to have been, in letters three inches high, by somebody who had come to this embankment on purpose and had stood on the berm so they would not have to walk past the sign.

Nobody in this cooperative maintained the branch. The sign said so. It also said that somebody, somewhere, did.

“Come and look at this,” Marek said.

Gael came and looked at it and copied it out too, in her own notebook, in her own hand, and signed it with the time.

“What does it say, in a sentence?” she said.

“It says the branch belongs to a schedule and not to us, and that whoever cuts it reports it to Fennmere.” Marek was already walking. “Which means the two things I have been told since I was nine years old are both true. The branch was abandoned. And somebody has been maintaining the abandonment, on a schedule, for thirty years, and reporting it.”

The cut was two hundred yards along the crown, where a living branch came up out of the fen side of the embankment and lay along the top of it in the open air, rootwood the color of a wet branch, a foot thick where it lay and running off down into the water for forty feet on the far end.

It had been cut.

The cut was not a saw cut and it was not a chopping cut. It had been made with something that closed, a shear or a clamp, because the face was flat and clean and had been closed to within a sixteenth. It had been made by somebody standing on the crown, which meant somebody knew it was there, and it had been made at the exact point where the branch crossed the crown, because somebody knew where the load was.

And the wound had healed.

That was the thing that stopped Marek cold. Scarwood does that. A living cut face laid open to the air will call in a callus, dry at the lip, and then the lip will close over, and in four or five years you will have a healed seam with a faint line in it and a bulb of new wood on the old face. He had seen it a hundred times in ditches and ditches do not have somebody’s die on them.

Gael said, very quietly, “Is that a scar?”

“It is a cut that healed. Two cuts, I think. Look.”

The second cut was above the first, a foot and a half along the branch, and it was fresher: the face still pale, still wet-looking, the callus a soft white ridge around the edge, no lichen, no bloom, no mineral crust. Two generations. And on the older healed face, struck into the dead wood with a steel die in letters a quarter of an inch high, so that it had gone into the grain and would still be legible when the rest of it was gone:

**N-2214/K**

Above it, struck smaller and shallower, from a different die and a worn one, so that one arm of the letter was thin:

**N-3318/K**

Marek knelt down for a long time.

He had expected — he had not known what he had expected. A wound that had gone bad. A root that had torn in a flood. Something a man had done in a hurry once and never come back for. He had expected neglect, because the cooperative’s whole story was thirty years of neglect, and because a neglected thing is a thing you can repair and put in a book and feel better about.

This was not neglect. This was a job. Somebody had been out here with a die and a shear at a time of their choosing, on a cycle, on a branch they were not responsible for under a schedule they were not named in, and they had stamped their own work into the wood so that the next man could find it, which was either conscience or inventory, and Marek could not decide which and did not much care, because both meant the same thing, which was that this was happening on a plan.

He got his tape and his plumb line and his scale rule and he did it properly, because that was the only thing he had. He measured the diameter at four points, the height of the branch above the crown, the distance from the cut to the north post and to the fifth post where the sign was, the length of the healed face, the thickness of the callus, the width of the second cut, the depth of the water at the root and the state of the drop. He chalked his initials on the crown at the point he had measured from and he wrote the number on the post. He did not cut anything and he did not take anything and he stepped where he had marked, all day.

The dating took the longest and he did it in the worst possible way, by looking at it and thinking.

The lichen around the older cut came up to the edge of the callus and had gone over it in places, which lichen does not do, and does not do in four years. The mineral bloom in the grain had a depth he had only seen on the seaward side of the mill-keeper’s shed, which was facing weather for forty years. The second cut had a pale face and no bloom at all.

“Six years, at least,” he said, writing it as a range and not a number, because a number he could not defend and a range he could. “Six, seven, eight. The old one. The new one is this season or last, and I will not go closer than that with a rule and a cold hand.”

“You can do better than that,” Gael said. “You told me on the road that a scar dates by what has grown back into the cut and not by what has grown on it. Is that not the rule you gave me for the lamp bulbs?”

Marek looked at her.

“That is the rule I gave you for lamp bulbs,” he said. “The lamp bulbs are rootwood. This is scarroot.”

“It is wood that was once living and got hurt.”

“It is.”

Gael wrote something in her notebook, and then stopped, and asked him a question that made him sit back on his heels.

“Then which one are you dating? The date the branch was cut, or the date the branch was last cut?”

Marek sat in the wet grass on the top of an embankment in the middle of a fen with a plumb line in his hand and found that he did not have an answer, and that the answer mattered enormously, and that he had been walking up here for two days assuming he already had one.

The difference between the two dates was the whole of the season.

If the branch had been cut once, six years ago, in a year when nobody was checking anything, then it was an old wound and the cooperative had let a thing rot and there was nobody above them at all.

If the branch had been cut six years ago and cut again since, and would be cut again, then there was a schedule, and a schedule has an author, and an author has an office, and an office is somewhere a long way from a fen.

He wrote down both dates. He wrote them as two lines, side by side, in his own hand, and he wrote the second one and then put a rule under the whole entry and wrote: *Both. Ask which.*

“That is a strange thing to write on a page.”

“It is a strange thing to find in a fen,” Marek said, “and now the finding is in a book with a person’s name on it, and in four months somebody is going to have to say which of those two lines is the one they believe, and the man who wrote both will be the man who has to be asked. So the man who writes it writes both.”

He drew it. He drew it to scale on the facing page, from the fifth post and the sign, with the crown line and the berm and the two ruts eight feet back, and the branch in elevation, and the two cuts with their measurements, and the two die-struck serials copied out in block capitals letter by letter, because a copy is a copy and a transcription can be argued with but a transcription is at least checkable. He put a scale bar on it and wrote the date twice, and the names of the two of them, and the weather.

Then he took the notebook round and put it on his knee and gave it to Gael Senn.

“Read it. All of it. And then put your name at the bottom and the date and what you have actually seen, because I have written three things on that page and only two of them are measurements.”

Gael read it. It took a while. She was slow and careful and she went back twice, and at the end she wrote:

*Witnessed by Gael Senn, lamp-keeper, Lantern Reach Work Cooperative. On the ninth day, at the weir line, in daylight. I have seen the two cuts, the two stamped numbers, the ruts on the berm, and the sign on the fifth post. I have seen Mr Vale measure them. I have not seen the cause and I am not asked to.*

“I would have written the last line anyway,” Marek said. “You saved me about four seconds of embarrassment.”

“You did not need saving from it,” Gael Senn said. “You needed somebody to say it out loud, and I am the only person out here.”

It was on the way back up the crown, ninety yards from the cut, on the fen side where the ground drops away to the old water meadow, that he found the marker.

It was a flat slab of fen sandstone set on end in the ground, chest high, weathered to the color of the moss around it, with a shallow dish worn into the top of it by about two hundred years of people putting cups down on it. There was no writing that he could read from the path. He went over the fence and walked around it and read it from the other side, and then he stood with his back to Gael, so that she would not be able to read his face.

*HANSA KERN. WENT WITH THE WATER. 1802.*

And under that, cut much later and much shallower, in the vernacular script of a fen family:

*she took the short road to the weir and did not come back by the long one — E.K.*

Marek stood there and let the whole shape of it come in on him. The short road to the weir. There had been a path — you could still see where it had been, a green line of trodden grass coming out of the meadow and away along the old bank, four feet from the marker, going up toward the fifth post and the sign and the place where the branch lay out on the crown in the open air where a loaded cart could not pass it and would have to go round on the berm in the soft.

Somebody’s grandmother had walked this way to the water for two hundred years, and about thirty years ago somebody had put a Crown sign on the fifth post saying there was no local holder, and every few years since then somebody had come with a die and sheared the branch on a schedule, and the path was still there, and the cup-mark on the slab was still there, and nobody had ever thought to say the two things out loud in the same sentence.

He did not tell Gael. He walked back over the fence and said, “The chalk mark on the north post has washed. I will do it again before we go,” and she said, “All right,” and he redrew the mark with the chalk twice over while she wrote the times in her own book, and there was nothing whatever in her book about the slab.

At two o’clock, Ilsa Kern came up the cart track from the bottom meadow with the bar over her shoulder and a milk pail in each hand, and Marek went out to the middle of the crown to meet her and asked her to put the pails down, and told her about the marker.

Gael Senn, thirty feet away with her back politely turned, learned every word of it.

Ilsa went and stood by the marker and put her hand on the top of it, on the dish, the way you touch a thing you have touched before, and she read the letters with her finger going along them, and she did not read the shallow last line twice because she did not need to.

“Yes,” she said. “The E. is my family’s hand. The whole slab is. That is my grandmother. She is in the fen book at Fennmere. She went out on the sixth of a wet month in a year nobody wrote down and came back on the ninth, and that slab is where they put her clothes. The short road went along the bank from the meadow up to the weir steps and it was there when I was a girl.”

“The branch is over the top of it.”

“Yes.”

“And a Crown sign eighty feet away says no local holder.”

“Yes,” Ilsa Kern said, and took her hand off the slab. “And now say the rest of what you are thinking, Marek, because I can see it in you and it is not a good one.”

“I was going to say that I am sorry we put our feet on your grandmother’s road.”

“You were going to say it very carefully, in a way that lets you be sad about my grandmother and still have come out here anyway,” Ilsa said. “Do not do that. I have watched you do that to yourself for twenty years and I am not a widow I am a woman with a job, and I would rather be useful and told the whole thing than comfortable and told half. The road is a road. You were on it because somebody scheduled a cut over it and stamped the cut and put a sign on the post, and if I am angry I am going to be angry with the office that did it, and I would like you to notice that the office has not sent anybody here to be angry with.”

“I did not put it in the book.”

“Of course you did not. You have been out here all day on a trespass and the first thing you think about is the paper.” She picked up the bar. “Put it in.”

“It is not a measurement. It is not evidence of a cut.”

“It is evidence that the cut is over a place people used, which is the only reason the thing matters to me, and it will not matter one single atom to an inspector unless somebody makes him care, and you cannot make him care with a scale bar.” Ilsa Kern looked at the barrel of the branch lying out on the crown in the open air, ninety yards away, and her voice went very flat. “Put my grandmother’s road in the margin, in your own hand, and put the date on it, and put your name under it. And then tomorrow, when the inspector is standing in that yard with a clause in his hand, you will say my name out loud. Not because it helps you. I want to be clear about that with you, because you will do it because you think it helps you, and I will not have it done for that reason. You will say it because it is true, and because if you leave me out of it then this stops being a cooperative and becomes a man with a grievance, and men with grievances are what put a die into a branch every three years.”

Gael Senn had turned around by then and was standing very still with her notebook shut.

“Write it in the margin,” Ilsa told her. “Not in the finding. In the margin, with the date, and put that it is the witness’s own ground and not a measurement.”

Gael wrote it in the margin, in her own hand, with the date, and signed it.

“All right,” Ilsa said, and shouldered the bar, and went down the track with the pails swinging. At the bottom of the slope she stopped and shouted back up the embankment, in a voice that carried the way it had carried across a fen for a hundred years.

“And Marek. Do not be careful. Careful is the word we use when we got away with it.”

They came back along the crown in the gray afternoon, and about a mile short of the gate the road forked, and a man was standing at the fork with a good coat and a canvas bag and the patient expression of somebody who has been waiting two hours and does not intend to say so.

He was not Bram Oskell. He was younger, and he had the pump-rig of a man who works on water for a living, and he had come down the fen road on his own.

“Mr Vale,” he said. “Mr Rusk sent me with the wagon.”

“Mr Rusk has sent three people to this cooperative in four days.”

“He has sent me.” The man shifted the bag. “I am to tell you the parts kit is at the Fennmere yard and it is yours for the season. Gaskets, packing, fiber, fortyweight of compound, a new pit ladder. No charge. Whatever you like to do with it. And he wants the page out of your book.”

Marek stopped walking.

Gael Senn stopped walking.

“The page,” Marek said.

“The drawing. The measurements. The numbers on the cut faces. He is not asking for the book, he has said so himself to Mr Oskell, and he does not want the fieldbook and he does not want your father’s name on anything. He wants the one page. He will pay for the kit in kind, which is worth about nine pounds to a cooperative, and the page is a sheet of paper.”

Marek felt the cold arrive from a long way off, the way cold had arrived on the fen road when he had worked out what a man in a bad coat could not do for him.

Nine pounds. He had paid fourteen for two days of a woman with a stick, and a kit worth nine would buy a new coupling, a new seal, and half of what a proper seal needed, and it would be in the pit by morning, and it would keep the well’s wound dressed for a season.

“No,” Marek said.

The man nodded as if he had expected that and had been paid to hear it. “He said you would say that. He said you would say it in front of her, too.”

Marek turned his head.

Gael Senn was standing in the road with her notebook against her chest and her hood down and an expression that Marek had not seen on a member of this cooperative before and did not see again for a long time.

“He said you would want to be asked in front of a witness,” Marek said. “All right. In front of a witness.”

He took the notebook out of his coat, held it up with the drawing open, and put his thumb on the page.

“I have a cut on a branch with a Crown die number on both faces and a Crown schedule sign nailed to a post that says nobody here maintains it, and I am the surveyor of a cooperative whose charter expires on the eighteenth, and I want it on paper. I want it on paper because if it is in a book then in nine days a man has to stand in a room in Fennmere and admit that he saw a number and said nothing, and if it is in a bag of gaskets on a cart then in nine days there is nothing at all, and the only thing left is a well that runs and a cooperative that has been told to be grateful.”

He put the notebook back in his coat.

“No,” he said. “Tell Mr Rusk no, and tell him I said it out loud, and tell him I said it in front of a witness named on the page.”

The man looked at Gael, and at Marek, and shifted the bag again.

“Nine pounds,” he said.

“Go home, Renn,” Marek said.

Renn Tallow stood in the road for a moment longer, and then he said, “The pad is dressed,” which was the last useful thing anybody said to Marek that day, and he went up the fen road with the empty bag over his shoulder and did not look back.

The gate was in sight when they heard hooves behind them, and it was a cooperative boy on a fen pony with his legs wet to the thigh, coming up the track at a dead gallop with a lantern he had not needed because it was still light.

“Vale,” he said, pulling up. “The sub-office. A rider came in at dusk with a schedule and Mr Fitch sent me.”

Marek got his breath.

“Tell me.”

“The annual,” the boy said. “It is not the eighteenth. They have brought it forward. They have brought it to tomorrow, and they have brought it to *this* tomorrow, and Mr Fitch has the paper and he is holding it in his hand and he has not sat down.”
