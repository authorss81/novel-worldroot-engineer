# Chapter 9

## The Thing at the Weir

The wind came up out of the west at nine, and by ten it had stopped being weather and started being a thing with weight to it, and all three lamps on the fen road came off their hooks before Marek had the seed house door barred.

“Leave them,” Gael Senn said, from under the bench. “They are wired. They go when the wire goes.”

“The north one is not wired. The north one is on a hook because the hook is all it has ever had, and I put it there, and it is a mile up the fen road and there is going to be a post down by morning.”

“There is not going to be a bulb in it by morning either. Give me the satchel.”

Marek gave her the satchel. She went out into it with her hood down, because a hood fills with water and gets in the way of a man’s hands, and she had a hooked pole and a jar of lamp oil and a tin of the compost a living bulb eats, and she took the north road at a run while the rest of the crew carried trays up off the low floor onto the long bench by feel.

“A mile,” Marek said.

“Mile and a bit. Quarter of an hour if I do not have to fight the last gate.”

“I will come with you as far as the third gate.”

“You will not.” Gael Senn pulled the strap over her head. “You have been in a pit for two days and a seed house for one night and you are running on four hours and cold bread. I have done this round since I was nineteen. Go and stand near your mother.”

She went out into it and was gone in nine seconds.

Ilsa Kern had the last of the March’s trays up onto the bench by ten and had a row of lamps going along the top shelf of the north annex to keep the seed above freezing, and Marek came through and put his back against the door and looked at the room.

“This is the first night in six years the season has turned against us the right way round,” Lissa Vale said, looking at the rain on the inside of the shutter. “We should be delighted.”

“It is the first night the seed does not have to be carried anywhere,” Marek said.

“That is not a sentence anybody in this room is glad to hear,” his mother said, “and it is true, and I am not going to say the rest of it out loud, so somebody else can.”

Ilsa put the last tray down and came over.

“Gael is up the fen road,” she said.

“Gael is up the fen road with a hooked pole and a mile of lamp oil in a wind like this.”

“I know where Gael is,” Ilsa Kern said. “I have sent three men out after her and I could not get a man past the yard gate, because the yard is under four inches off the embankment and it is coming up the middle. I sent the two strongest and the mill boy, and the mill boy is nine. That is what I have done, so stop, because you are about to do it again.”

“Where are your two strongest?”

“In the well house. Sandbagging the pit.”

“That’s right,” Marek said. “Sandbag the pit.”

The word came out of him the way it does when a thing is arranged and you are only finding out the arrangement now.

“There is a lamp out,” he said.

“There is a lamp out.”

“It is a living bulb. It eats compost and water, and it does not eat wind. If the wind takes that hook off the wire tonight the bulb starves in nine days, and there is a seed house on this holding that has never once had a bulb in it starve, and Lissa Vale will have to explain why there is a dark corner in here. It will not be me explaining it, because I will be in a pit for a fortnight.”

Lissa Vale, who had not raised her voice once in three days, said, “I am going to be a woman who lost a lamp and I would rather be a woman who lost a lamp than a woman who lost a boy. Sit down, Marek.”

He sat down.

“Did she get it up there,” he said.

Ilsa Kern had been at the door and now came the length of the room, and she said it the way she said everything, in order, with the numbers first.

“She got the satchel to the third gate and then a mile and a bit into the wind, and she ran it. The north post is gone over. Not broken — gone over, the staple out of the wood, the post in the ditch with the bulb still in it, and a bulb in the water is a dead bulb. She knew that before she got there, which is why she is the one we sent. She wrenched the staple out of the second post, got the lamp onto the wire and lit it off the satchel, and that is the lamp you are seeing from here. It cost her a coat and most of a can of lamp oil, and she will be the last person in this yard to complain about it.”

“And the way back?”

“The way back is shut,” Ilsa Kern said. “The drain ditch came up while she was at the north post and the causeway under the weir road went with it, so she is on the far side of the race with two feet of water where the road was, and there is nothing on that side of it at this hour except the weir steps and the maintenance plank. The plank is four feet wide and it is the only thing over that race a person can lie on without falling off. The two men I sent got as far as the third gate. That is the last quarter of an hour, and I have told you all of it because you will go up there otherwise.”

What he heard was not a dog.

It came up out of the west along the fen road, low, and it was not one voice: it was a voice and under it another, and under that the sound of four hundred feet going one way through standing water in the dark. Then a sheep, and then a great many sheep, and then the sound the fen made when a great many hooves went one way through standing water in the dark, which Marek had heard every autumn of his life and had never once been able to name.

He was on his feet with the door open.

“Marek—” Lissa Vale got a hand on the back of his coat and missed most of it. “Marek, sit down, it is the fen, it is stock moving, it is the pasture flock, they will—”

“The pasture flock is on the far side of the weir line.”

Ilsa Kern was already running for the yard door.

They got four feet into the yard. The water was shin-deep and running hard in one direction, and beyond it, up the fen road past the third gate, the whole dark shape of the flock was coming down the embankment crown in an unbroken front a hundred yards wide, and the noise a hundred and thirty head of them made was a river breaking, and the drover’s dog, a long gray thing, was in the middle of it going the wrong way, doubling back and getting carried, and above and behind and along the line of them all there was something walking on the crown.

It went along the crown the way a man walks on a road.

It was longer than a man and lower than a man and it did not run. It walked, and it kept the flock moving, and it made a sound with its jaw the whole time that was not a bark and not a howl and was closer to a man counting in his throat. Its coat was a wet color that was not a color. Where the light off the seed house lamp got along its back, the wet parts were the exact color of a compound pad four seasons old and the dry parts were the color of the scarwood under one.

Its head was flat and low and there was nowhere in it to find an eye.

It was between the flock and the road, and the flock was coming down the crown toward the weir steps, and the weir steps went down into the race, and the race on that night was carrying a great deal of the fen.

“Pathhound,” said Lissa Vale.

Marek turned round. His mother had not moved off the shutter, and she was not looking at the animal. She was looking past the yard, out over the weir line, at nothing that was in it.

“Mother—”

“Pathhound,” she said again. “That is what the old fen called it, and it is not a word that means much. It has been in the water long enough for the water to make a thing of it, and it puts living things off a flow that is not doing what it was cut to do. It is not a nest, it is not mad, and it is not a dog, and it is not hunting — it is herding. Go at it with a stick and you will have the whole bank of the race after you and you will lose eleven ewes for it.”

“Then what is done with it?”

“Nothing is done with it,” Lissa Vale said. “You cannot call it off and you cannot tie it up, and you cannot kill the thing it is standing on and be rid of it, because I have known two men try that and both of them were still herding in the spring. What you do is change where the water goes, and then it goes and looks for the water, and if you are lucky it does not come back.” She turned her head, and the lamp showed nothing at all in her face. “And I have not said that word in this yard in thirty years, so notice that I have said it.”

“The plank,” Ilsa said, in a voice Marek had not heard from her since she was thirty-one. “Gael is on the plank.”

He could not see her. He could see the lamp she had hooked on the far side of the weir, four feet above the deck, and the light going round with the water, and the maintenance plank lying across the race at an angle, and the flock coming down the last thirty yards of the embankment toward it, and the drover’s dog working back and forth in front of them, and the thing on the crown walking beside the front of the flock with its jaw making that counting sound.

“I have to get to the plank,” Marek said.

“There is no way to the plank. The yard gate is under water, and the weir road goes under at the third ditch, four feet deep, running out of the fen, and it will hold you and drown you. I am not losing the engineer of record to a race tonight, not with the inspector coming back on the eighteenth, not for anything.”

“The fen road is dry,” Marek said. “The embankment crown is the only high ground for a mile. If I go along the crown I am in the sheep.”

“In the sheep,” Ilsa Kern agreed, “and then in the race, and then I will have carried the last of the seed into a dry room and watched both of you go down together, and I am not doing that on a night when I have lost the north lamp already.”

“Then somebody else goes.”

They got two men as far as the third gate before the road came off the crown, and there they had to stop, because the gate was under water from the ditch back and the only other way up the fen road was the one thing in Tarrow Fen that neither of them could swim. Renn Tallow was on it with a handcart, single, loaded: the pit ladder lashed on one side, four sacks of fiber, and fortyweight of compound in a tarred pot under a hide. It was eleven o’clock and the wind had him leaning and the cart was nearly sideways in the wet.

Marek got to the gate with his hand on the post.

“Renn.”

Renn Tallow did not stop. He came the last three feet at a jog and his boots went in the ditch and he brought the cart up out of it with his shoulder.

“Renn. There is a girl on the weir plank. There is a lamp on the weir steps and ninety ewes coming down the crown behind her and a — there is something on the crown with them. Help me get her off. I have not got a rope and you have got a handcart and there is room on it.”

Renn Tallow looked at him.

He looked at him for a length of time that Marek will remember for the rest of his life, and there was nothing in the face at all, no malice and no fright and nothing that could be called anything, and Renn Tallow said, “I have got the load.”

And he went on up the fen road with the cart, and his lantern went off up the crown ahead of the flock and out of sight, and Marek stood at the gate with his hands on a gate post in a storm, with a locked road between him and a lamp-keeper.

He went back for the pole.

The gate was under four inches and rising. He got the two men behind the embankment with a board and a shovel, and stood at the top of the yard steps and looked up the crown at two hundred feet of it between him and the weir, and there was nothing to be done with the yard that was not the yard, and no rope, and no way across, and the flock coming on.

He was a surveyor. He could not swim, and there was no line, and there was a knot in him about his sister and about a shaft and about every single time he had ever gone to a place where he should not have gone and been right about being there.

He went up the crown on foot.

It was not courage and it was not stupidity and he could not afterwards decide which. A girl of twenty-three was lying on four feet of plank over a race in the dark, the road had closed, and the alternative was standing in a yard being right about the water. He went along the shoulder of the embankment, on the berm, on the eight feet of soft going where the cart ruts were, with his hands up in the air and his mouth shut, and the sheep came past him on both sides in the dark and the mud went to his chest and then his waist, and he did not have to touch one of them.

The thing on the crown walked past him.

It was going with the flock on the far side, and it came level with him, and it did not stop and did not hurry, and he could feel it there the way you can feel a person in a room in the dark. It made no sound at him and gave him no attention. It was not interested in him because he was not carrying the pattern it was moving, and it had four hundred feet of work to do.

The far end of the crown, where the branch came up out of the fen side and lay out in the open air, was where the flock was being driven. And there, in the lee of the branch, twenty feet short of the weir steps, was the second cut.

The weir steps were eight feet below the crown. The plank lay across the race at an angle and went down into the water at the far end, and the lamp was on the far step four feet up, and the water under the plank was black and moving fast and full of fen weed.

Gael Senn was lying on the plank with her arms round the lamp post and her boots over the edge.

“Marek,” she said. “Do not come on. It will not hold two.”

“I am not coming on. Listen to me. Can you get to the far bank?”

“The far bank is thirty feet of water and I cannot swim either, you told me that in April.”

“Then stay down and put your face in the crook of your arm and do not let go of the post whatever happens, and if a sheep comes on the plank let it go over. Do not fight anything. Do you understand me? Let it go over.”

“I understand you. Go and do whatever you are going to do and stop telling me things.”

The flock came onto the crown behind the cut and the front of it reached the branch, and the dog went in among them barking, and the thing on the crown lifted its head.

It made a different sound. Not the counting. A short low sound with a rising edge to it, like a man saying *no*, and the front rank of the flock stopped, and then the whole flock stopped, a hundred and thirty head of it packed shoulder to shoulder on a twelve-foot crown, and stopped moving.

That was the moment Marek understood what he was looking at. Not a thing that was driving them. A thing that was holding them off a line on the ground, and doing it the way his mother had said, with no interest in any individual animal in it. It had put four hundred animals on a twelve-foot embankment and stopped them dead and it was not eating any of them and it was not looking at any of them; it was standing at the branch with its flat head down and its jaw going, and the flock would not step over the branch.

It was rootwoken, his father’s word for a thing the water had been at for long enough to change the terms of. It had been keeping them off that line since before he was born.

He got his crowbar out of his belt — cold iron, five feet, chisel end, the only heavy thing on him and the right tool for a healed callus — and went along the shoulder on his hands and knees to the branch.

The second cut was a foot and a half above the first and its face was pale and still hard. The old healed seam was below it, two hands across, the callus a rolled white lip with a line in it like a scar on a person.

He set his left hand on the scarwood of the cut face.

He should not have. He knew the terms, he had written them down himself two days earlier, and he did it anyway, because the flock was four hundred feet of animal on a twelve-foot road and there was a girl on a plank and he was the only person within a mile who had ever touched a living branch on purpose.

He got about four seconds.

He got cold, and iron, and a direction.

The direction was wrong.

That was the whole of it, and it was the only thing he ever got out of damaged wood in his life. No voice, no shaft, no father’s hands, no sequence of four things. A pressure, and the pressure had a direction in it, and the direction said the flow was going away up the branch and out into the meadow, and Marek got that clear and certain and it was backwards. It was coming up out of the meadow and along the branch and pressing at the cut, and it had been pressing there for six years, and everything the animal was doing on this embankment was about that pressure and nothing else.

Damaged wood lies. He got his right hand up and put the chisel end of the crowbar into the healed seam, on the line, at the third hand, and leaned his whole weight on it.

He knew the name of it now, and that was worse than the cold and the iron and the wrong direction. On the eighth day he had come out of four seconds of a stranger’s hands with no idea at all what had been done to him, and now he had a word for it, and the word was *memory bleed*, and it meant that the place in him where his own hands were could be written on. It was the same four seconds as the first one and it cost him more, which is the thing nobody tells you: the length is not the price. It cost him a handprint on a stone he had no memory of touching, and it cost him the hour between the gate and the crown. There is not a licensed reader in the whole of the fen who has seen either of them, and he is going to have to write the whole of this down tonight in a book in a seed house and be believed or not believed.

The callus did not move.

He got his left forearm under the bar, put his hip against the branch and pulled with his back, and the bar went in about a quarter of an inch with a fine dry crackle like a boot going into frozen mud, and the seam opened a quarter of an inch along three feet of its length and stopped.

He got the graft shears out from his belt — his own, not Kellan’s, and a good pair, and the only thing in his kit that was made for living wood and cost more than he had paid for anything else he owned.

He put the blades into the quarter-inch gap and closed them and used the shear handles as a lever, and the joint at the elbow where the two shears were riveted went past its design and made a bright silver noise, and the seam opened another half inch.

And behind the seam, in the space that had been closed for six years, the water was there.

It did not come out like a pump. It came out like a held breath. It came out sideways along the channel that runs under the crown between the berm and the fen side, the old drainage channel, a ditch that has been dry and choked with reed for thirty years, and it went into that channel with a noise like a man turning a bucket over, and the channel took it and ran and went away down toward the meadow, and the pressure in the branch came off the cut all at once, the way a hand comes off a shoulder.

The thing on the crown dropped flat.

Not slowly. Its legs went out from under it and it went down onto the mud on its side and lay there making no sound at all. The hundred and thirty head that had not moved for four minutes moved. Not away — the pressure was off the line, and the line was not a line any more, and the flock turned, all of it together, in a space the size of a threshing floor and went back up the crown away from the branch at a walk, and the drover’s dog came out from among them and went round the far side and started to work.

Marek got the crowbar out of the seam and stood up on a twelve-foot embankment in a storm with ninety ewes walking past him in the dark.

The seam was open the length of three feet and it was not going to close. Scarwood laid open to the air with the pressure behind it puts out a callus in weeks and a knot in a season and never a scar, and what the water does after that is go on going, all year, in every year, out of a cut a man made with a crowbar and a pair of shears on the night of the tenth day of the season because a girl was on a plank.

The animal got up.

It took a long time. It got its front legs under it and stood, on the crown, with its head down, and it did not look at Marek and did not look at the branch. Then it turned and went off along the drainage channel, down the side of the embankment into the reed, on the line of the water that had just gone that way, at a trot. It did not hurry and it did not look back, and Marek watched it go down into the dark and did not see it again.

He had not done anything to it.

He had changed where the water went, and the animal had gone to look at the water, and that was all that had happened. If a man in Fennmere had asked him what he had done to it he would have had to say that he had opened a cut in a dead branch with a bar and a pair of shears, and that the animal had left because the thing it was standing on was no longer there, and that he had not asked it to do anything at all.

Gael Senn got herself up the far steps out of the weir race with the lamp held over her head in both hands and came across the crown in the rain.

Marek was on his hands and knees on the stones.

“Marek.”

“I am all right.”

“You are on the ground, and I can hear you being sick, and if you are sick on this crown it goes in the ditch and it is your mother’s problem in the morning.” She got the lamp down onto the stones away from him and sat on her heels beside him out of the wind. “Gael Senn, lamp-keeper. I was on the plank. I am on the crown. I am whole. Do you want to say a thing now or in a minute.”

“I changed the flow.”

“I do not know what that means and I do not care, and I have watched a man explain something to me once already this week and he was wrong. How long were you on the crown.”

“Ten minutes.”

“You told me for the fourth time this week that you were somewhere for four seconds and it was ten minutes, and I would like to know what you are counting,” Gael Senn said. “Hand me the bar. You are holding it with both hands and you do not know it.”

He gave her the crowbar.

He braced his right hand on the crown stones to get himself up, and there was mud on it, and there was a print in the mud, the print of a right hand. He looked at it, and thought: that is not mine, or that is mine and I do not remember putting it there, and he could not decide, and neither could the print.

The flock went up the road in the dark behind them with the dog at the tail of it, and every animal on the fen heard it go and did not move, and by three in the morning there were ninety ewes and thirty-six lambs and four rams somewhere between the fen pasture and the peat roads with a dog, alive, in a bad night, moving off in the dark toward water they had never been to.

None of them came back.

The storm went over the fen between one and four and dropped nine-tenths of an inch on the whole of the Tarrow, and the top of the embankment at Weir Row came down in two places and put a foot of water in the cooperative’s yard and down the well pit.

Marek was not there. He was in a doorway with his back on a wall and a blanket round him, no blood in his nose and no memory of the last hour of the walk from the third gate, and he was sick three times and his hands were shaking in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

At dawn Ilsa Kern went down the well pit with a lamp.

The pad had gone under. Four inches of water came up through the compound and stood on it, the compound had let go of the cradle on the low side, and the whole thing had sunk half an inch into the wound it had never once been sealing. The scarwood under it was open to the water and the water was iron. The base coat and the four dressings laid on it since had all let go at once, the tar had gone into the water and made a slick that went out across the pit, and there was no compound left in the cooperative of Lantern Reach to put over anything at all.

“Four sacks of fiber and fortyweight of compound went up that road at eleven o’clock last night,” Ilsa Kern said, to nobody, down in the cold. “And I let it go up. I stood in the yard and I let it go up, because I was sandbagging a pit and because the girl was in the water. And that is the arithmetic, and we are all going to live inside it.”

She got up out of the pit and stood on the rim with the lamp in her hand, looking north up the fen road at nothing.

“And the flock,” she said, to the same nobody. “That flock has been the seed house’s return for eleven years. Sheep manure off the pasture into the bed behind the north annex, all winter, every year, and it is the reason that bed has held four degrees through two bad seasons, and now it is the only thing holding it there, because there is nothing else to put in it. It went up that road at eleven o’clock along with the compound. I sent two men after it. And we are all going to go on pretending that I did not stand in this yard and work out what that meant while the water was coming up.”

Nobody asked her what it meant. Marek, standing in the pump shed doorway with his blanket round him, knew exactly what it meant, and could not have said it aloud without sounding like a man who spent the night destroying something and is now describing his own grief at it, which is what he was.

Eight days to the eighteenth.
