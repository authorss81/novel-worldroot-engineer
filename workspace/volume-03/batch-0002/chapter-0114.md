# Chapter 114

## What Is In The Coat

The first of next month came up on a Friday and the yard was full by the ninth hour, and four of the things that fell due on a first of a month fell due in it, and one of them had been falling due on most mornings for two years and had never fallen due at all.

The leak was measured at the seventh hour, twice, with two rules, the second of them the one that reads smaller on that corner, pump on, water into the housing and not into the pit, and the pit was not up.

**Five-eighths. The second rule smaller. The pit is not up.**

And the reading was not done in a kitchen. It was done in the open at the hard standing with about fourteen people standing round it, because the fen had decided on the twenty-second of last month that it is to be read out, and because the clerk read the last one out on the second of last month in an empty yard and the man of about fifty had said at the time that a number nobody has heard is a number nobody is responsible for, and because somebody had gone and looked for the figure and the book had not kept it.

Jonas Byre was appointed the reader of it, and the appointment was made standing up and out loud before the pump was touched, and his reason for taking it was given and entered and was that he read the dial from above the pit out of thirty-one years of counting a mill wheel in the voice of a man reciting a habit, and that if there was going to be a reading of this figure in a yard with fourteen people in it then he wanted the man who counts out loud to be the one doing it.

> Leak at the pump housing, return main, read at the seventh hour of the first of next month, in a full yard, by the examiner, twice, with two rules, the second of them the one that reads smaller on that corner, pump on, water into the housing and not into the pit, the pit not up: **five-eighths of an inch.**
>
> **This is the forty-third day since the last entry in this body's book concerning this leak, which is the seventh hour of the eighteenth of a month two months back.** It is the same figure as that entry. It is the same figure as the reading of the twenty-first of this month, at the seventh hour, with the same two rules, the same corner, and the pit not up.
>
> **The number of mornings between that entry and this one is forty-two.** The number of those mornings on which the figure was taken cannot be given, and the fen entered that it cannot be given, and the reason it cannot be given is the same reason as on the twenty-second of last month and it is written here again in the same words: **a practice that was never written down leaves no account of itself, and the absence of the account is not evidence of any morning in particular.**
>
> The figure is not moved. The count of mornings is not moved. The pit is not up and there is no entry for the pit being up.

The reader read the figure and the count and the forty-third day back into a yard of about fourteen people, and then the examiner read the whole of it back including the sentence about the mornings, and neither of them was thanked and neither of them can be.

And then the thing the coat is named for, which is a number and a room. Nobody in that yard said it out loud, and the reason it was not said is in the book, and the reason is that the argument is not this body's. It belongs to a woman at Lantern Reach who has been making it since the flood with a chisel in her hand, to an empty yard, and she is four hundred miles off and she is not in this yard, and a body does not carry a person's argument nine days' walk away without asking her. So it stands in the book unattributed, and it is true, and it is that **the number is the number and the room is different, and a figure that does not move is not an answer and is not a resolution and is not a result.**

The door was read out whole at the tenth hour and the count of the readings of the seventh column went to twenty-four, and the fen entered that the count on the sixteenth of this month had been made in an empty yard by two men four hundred miles away and this one was made in a full yard by four, and that both are the same number, and that the last time the two ends of this body stood in front of the same count in the same hour was never.

---

The examination was kept at the eleventh hour with no officer and no form, by choice, and Gael Senn kept it and wrote nothing in advance, and there is nothing in this holding now that requires it, and she kept it anyway, and the standing reason was entered by her own hand and is four words and has never been explained to anybody.

> Kept at the eleventh hour of the first of next month, with no officer and no form, by the person who keeps it, by choice. **Nothing found.** Licensed person present, none. The column for a cause is left as it is left and no cause is entered, and it is not to be read as a finding that there is no cause.

**Nothing found, and not *all well*,** and the difference between those two phrases is the standing of the whole of this arrangement and it is in the book and it has been in the book since a chapter a long way back now.

And then the compost line, at about the eleventh hour, in the ordinary words, in the seed house book, at the north end of the bench, with the bay doors open behind her and about eleven people standing in the room, and **Gael Senn wrote it and Lissa Vale did not.**

> Return main, this month: nothing put in. None available. Specification four hundred hundredweight. **Shortfall four hundred and fifty-two hundredweight, ninety pounds eight at four shillings.** Carried forward and not discharged. **Sixth month.**
>
> The figure is not three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight and it is not going to be three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight for as long as this holding is in this field, and the two of the three things that made it larger are entered under it in the ordinary words and are not a fault. Thirteen weeks of horse at a hundred and four shillings is twenty-six hundredweight at four. The coming down the plateau road on the twenty-seventh at a hundred and four shillings is twenty-six hundredweight at four. And nothing came back in this month at all, which is the first month in eleven years that nothing came back in, and that is a fact about the ground and not about a man.
>
> **The shortfall's own run is six months now and not five.** It has not been discharged in six months. The specification has not been met in six months. It is entered for the sixth month in the sixth month and it is not to be softened in the writing, and if it is ever softened in the writing then the softening is the only thing in this book that a person in nine miles can use against it.

Anneke Roos read it out in the yard afterwards and the reader was the man of about fifty, and she said the reason out loud before she said the number and she said it in the steward's way, which is that she asked one question about who loses.

"Three hundred and ninety-nine was a well and nothing else and I told you so in the second month and I was not wrong, and it is now four hundred and fifty-two and the extra fifty-three is thirteen weeks of horse and four days of horse and a month where nothing came back, and **a body that puts a road in a compost line and calls it a fault has learned nothing at all and I would like that written in my own hand and not softened by the man who read it out.**

"Going to look at a thing is not a fault. It is a cost. I said that on the sixth of last month in this yard and I have said it twice since in books and I am saying it a fourth time and it is the last time this fortnight, because it is my standing and I am spending it on a number and not on a person. The fault in this line was put there in a month five months back by a well and it is still there and we added to it with a road, and somebody in a year is going to write the two together and see which of them a person could have done without."

And then the man of about fifty asked the question, and it was in the ordinary way and it was not a large question and it took the room by the back of the neck anyway.

"Is the steward going to be told what another person wrote in her book."

"Yes," said Anneke Roos, "and it is four months old and it is a question I asked her, and here is the whole of it. **On the seventeenth of a month three months back I asked Gael Senn whether she would enter the monthly return in Lissa Vale's book in her own hand, because the steward's hand is on every page of that book for eleven years and there is no rule in this holding that says a return may be written by anybody else, and she asked me why, and I told her, and she said yes.** It is the only page in eleven years that is not in the steward's hand. The steward has known about it for four months. She is standing in this room."

Lissa Vale read the whole of it back after the clerk, including the reason, and then she said one sentence about it, and the sentence is in the book, and it is not a complaint, and it is the only sentence she said about the page all morning.

"Then from the first of next month it is entered in whatever hand it is in, and the reader for it is the man of about fifty and not me, **and I am the hand it is not, and a book that has one page in another hand in it is a book anybody can check, and a book that has every page in one hand in it is a book that can only be checked by one person.**"

---

The second half of the first of next month is at a counter nine miles off, and it is a clerk doing the ordinary thing, and the thing he did was correct.

The copy that went down the fen road to the sub-office on the eighteenth of last month went in an envelope with a covering line naming the office at the head of the form on the third page, and it asked nothing and told nothing, and it lay on a counter in Fennmere for a month, and on the second of next month a clerk at that counter took it out of the tray with the returns in it and read it, because a clerk reads what comes across his counter.

He read it twice. He read the seventh column, which had been copied as a line and not as a count. He read the four lines of the direction off the page. He read nothing that was not on the page in front of him, and there is nobody in this fen with standing to say he should not have, which is not the same as there not being a thing to be sorry about.

Perrin Osse is forty-five and is the clerk of that counter and has been for twenty-two years and has taken the charter-condition walk-through four years running and will take a fifth, and she is the institutional memory of a district that keeps its registers in a small square hand because a small square hand can be read at a hearing. She was not in the room when the copy was read. She was told about it at about the fourth hour of the afternoon by the man who had read it, and he told her without being asked, in the ordinary way, standing at the counter, and she wrote it into the register and read it back before she initialled it.

> Entered at the fourth hour of the second of this month, at this counter, on the statement of the clerk who read it: the copy of a reading sent from Lantern Reach on the eighteenth of last month was read at this counter by a clerk of this office on the second of this month. He read the whole of it twice. He is not the holder of it, it was not addressed to this office, and it was not in a tray of returns and was not part of the business of this office. **He had no reason to know it was not his to read and no rule here that told him.** Nothing has been taken out of it. Nothing has been copied out of it. It is to be replaced in the tray and the tray is to be closed over it, and the envelope is to go back on the ordinary post to the sender with a line saying that it was not addressed to this office, and the sender may or may not send again and that is not this office's business.
>
> **A register records what happened and is not a judgment about whether it should have.** I am the clerk of this counter and I enter what happened with the hour and I read it back, and I will not have anybody in this building go into that tray again on a curiosity.

And then she said the two things she has said twice in four years, and she said them without being asked and to nobody, and the man of about seventy in a yard four hundred miles off is right about all three of the sentences that came out of the next ten minutes and nobody in Fennmere knows that.

"**The record of an instrument is not the instrument.** We now hold a copy of a reading. A reading is a piece of paper somebody made. **The thing that made it is a book in a locked room four hundred miles off on a shelf, and this office cannot get at it, and the reason this office cannot get at it is a clause on a sheet of fen paper that names nobody and requires no signature and cannot be taxed, waived or defeated, and it is four years old and it cost them a price in public and in a yard to write it down.**

"And I want the other half of that said, because I am the clerk of this counter and I say the other half. **That clause does not stop me. Nothing in this district stops me from writing to a woman at Cauldron Reach and asking her for the book.** I have no power and no part and no business and there is nobody nine miles off who would find out, and I have known that for four years, and the only reason I have not done it is that I have spent twenty-two years in this chair and I have never once in twenty-two years put a question into a return. That is not because the clause holds me. It is because of me. **A thing cannot be enforced is the only kind of thing that can be refused, and a refusal nobody can enforce is a refusal somebody has to keep by hand every day of their life, and that is the whole of what is on that counter and there is nothing in this building that will stop me except me.**"

And the fen did not know any of it. That is entered, and it is entered as a fact and not as a mercy.

---

And then Coll Renner came to the counter at about the fifth hour with his own book under his arm, because a licensed man's book is not a private book and it goes in, and he stood there and heard the second of it read out, and the man of about fifty was not there to hear it and would not have understood most of it.

He said one sentence about the coat, standing at a counter in a district office, with his hand flat on his own book, and it is the most uncomfortable thing anybody says in this fortnight, and he said it flatly, and he was not being clever, and he went home after it.

"**The copy in my coat is the safe one, because it is mine, and a licensed man's copy is a licensed man's, and no office can call it a record of a holder. And that is exactly why it is the one that cannot be relied on. I can lose the coat. I can lose the book. I can be run over on the fen road in a month, and this fen would then hold three copies and two of them are in its own name and can be declared part three, and I would not be here to say that I had one.** And I have said that out loud in an office because I would like it written down somewhere that it is not a thing anybody is comfortable about, and I would like it written down by a clerk who has never met me and is not going to be thanked for it."
