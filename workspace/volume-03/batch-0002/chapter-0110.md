# Chapter 110

## What Is In The Stack

Three people asked Merrid Ollvar to let somebody else open the stack, and she said no three times, and the third reason is the one that is going to be true in thirty years, and it is not an engineering argument at all, and it is the reason the stack is open.

The first was Marek Vale, on the twenty-second, at about the tenth hour, in the yard, standing about four feet off the north side with the wind on his back.

"Let me do it. I have a knife that has not been on anybody's tree and I have a rule and a lens and I have hands that did nine of these faces on the ninth in an afternoon and did not tire until the fourth hour, and this cold will take the skin off the back of your hands inside a week and you have thirty-one years of this to do the rest of it with."

"Thank you," said Merrid Ollvar, "and no, and here is the reason and I am giving you the real one and not the polite one. **You would do it well and that is the trouble.** If a man from nine miles down the road opens my father's stack then the thing that comes out of it is a thing that was found, and there is a difference between a thing that was found and a thing that was opened, and I have been on this shelf thirty-one years and I have never once had somebody else's hands in my father's wood, and I am not starting at fifty-four because it is a Monday and the wind is off the ice."

The second was Coll Renner, in the evening, in the barn, and he gave it in the terms of a licensed man and could not have given it in any other terms if he had tried.

"I am licensed and I have read four hundred records that a person made with their hands, and I am telling you that what is going to come out of that stack is going to be read with a rule and a lens by somebody in about ten years whether you open it or not, and it will be read wrongly, because a stack that has been fused for a long time is read from the outside in and a man reads what the outside tells him. **If I open it, it is read from the middle out, and there is a difference in a record between a thing that has been dug into and a thing that has been taken out.** I have a legal reason and it is a good one and I am not going to dress it up as anything else, and it is that a licensed man who opens it puts his license near it, and then it is a record that somebody did something to and not a record of what a family did."

"And if I open it," said Merrid Ollvar, "then it is a record of what a family did. **I know. That is why I am opening it.** I have thought about this for four days and I have got the same answer four times and it is not a good answer, and I would like somebody else to say it is a bad one before I do it with my own hands."

The third was the man at the second house, on the morning of the twenty-third, before the light, and he was not being generous and he said so in the first nine words.

"I am not offering."

"I know."

"I am telling you that it is my father's ground and my father's spade and my father's name is not on the wall of the third house but it is on the holding paper somewhere and it is on nothing else in this country, and if there is anything in that stack then the two of us are going to have to be able to say we did not go into it, and I would rather we were both standing here when it came open." He shifted the spade. "And if you want the other reason as well, here is the other reason, and I have never said it out loud to anybody and I am saying it because you have been here five days and have not once asked me anything I did not want to answer. **If a man from nine miles down the road opens it, then in thirty years there will be a story in this valley about the day the valley let a stranger open the stack.** And there will be a story, and the story will be the only account anybody has, and it will not be true, and my grandchildren will not be able to tell that it is not true because they will have the story and not the wood."

She said no to that one by saying yes, and the man at the second house put the spade down against the wall where it had been standing for about an hour, and did not touch it again that day, and nobody thanked him for the offer because nobody thanks anybody.

---

She opened it herself, on the twenty-third, at about the eighth hour, with a knife, and it took most of the day.

It is pressed rootwood in bales, going round two sides of that yard, higher than a man and longer than the barn, and it has been against the north wall for longer than anybody at Cauldron Reach can date, and the outside of it is not wood any more. It is a gray pulp, wet underneath, and the bales have set into one another where the weather has got at the edges of them and dried them back together, and a bale does not come off in one piece. It comes off in lumps, and each lump has to be cut through, and the cutting has to be done with the grain and not against it, and a woman of fifty-four with a knife and no hurry cut through the first four feet of it between the eighth hour and the eleventh and stopped because her hands had gone.

Marek Vale was not allowed to touch it. He was asked to look and he looked, and he was asked twice, because nobody trusted the first asking, and he did not touch it and he said at the end of the day that he had wanted to touch it more than he had wanted to touch anything on that shelf.

Nobody in that yard made a sound of any kind while she was doing it, and that is a fact about the yard and not about the woman, and the man at the second house sat on the wall for the whole of it and did not speak, and there were about eleven people there by the fourth hour because word goes four hundred yards on that shelf faster than a letter goes nine miles down a road.

And what was in it, and it is a working description and there is nothing in this paragraph that is not a fact about a piece of wood.

The outside of every bale is pulp to the depth of a hand or a little more, and the pulp goes inward in a wedge, because that is where the water ran. Inside the wedge the wood is still wood. It is pale, and it is short, and it is cut in lengths of about a foot and a half, and every length in the stack is within an inch of every other length, and the ends of them are cut, and the cut on the end of every one of them has a bevel on it.

A single slope, on one side only, about an inch and a half across, and the same side on all of them, and not a saw and not a cleaver and not an axe.

Marek Vale put a rule to the nearest of them and read it from about a foot away and did not touch the wood with the rule either, and said the one sentence he was allowed to say on that day, and he said it to the yard and not to her, because she is not of this fen and this fen's findings are read back by its own reader.

"**The cutting is done and the pressing is done by two different people, or in two different trades.** The cutting is a knife with a shoulder on it and a hand that knew what it was doing. The pressing is a mill, and a mill that will make a bale you cannot separate with a knife in an afternoon is not a mill a family builds. **A press that good is not a family press.** So the lengths were cut by one man, or one household, and they were pressed by somebody who had pressing to do and who took other people's rootwood as well as their own, and one of those two is not this family and we cannot tell which and I am not going to guess in a yard."

And the fen counted the bales, because the fen counts things, and the count is in the ledger in Lissa Vale's hand with the hour on it, and it took about forty minutes and it is the only work of the twenty-third that anybody enjoyed.

There are sixty-one bales on that side of the wall and the other side of the corner, and the man at the second house did the counting and would not be helped, and he counted aloud at the end of each row and got the same figure twice, which is not usual and which he mentioned twice. There is no timber in them. That is the second fact and it is the reason this took a day and not an afternoon, and it is in the book: **the pressing went on hot and it went on green, and everything in this stack was put in wet, and half of it was never wood at all until it was pressed and it will never be wood again.** A stack of pressed rootwood that has been against a north wall for thirty years and has never been burned is not firewood and has never been firewood, and the family has never burned any of it and would not say why not and did not have to, and nobody asked him again.

And the lumps that came off were carried to the middle of the yard and piled in a heap about the size of a small cart, and the pile was still standing there on the twenty-fourth and was still standing there when the fen left, because there is nowhere on that shelf to put a heap of wet pulp, and that is a cost of one day and it is entered as a heap in a yard and not as a figure, and that is the only figure this body has produced that anybody in that yard can check by walking round it.

---

And then Lissa Vale was stood in a yard and had to hear about it, and the fen entered the whole of it, and nobody in that yard softened any of it, and she did not ask for any of it to be softened.

She is the steward of the fen and she is the woman who would not open it, and she said why on the eighth of this month in a yard four hundred miles from here, and it went in the seed-route ledger in her own hand with the hour on it, and it is the best piece of reasoning anybody in her own building has produced this season, and it has been carried four hundred miles and obeyed by a family she has never met.

*A stack is a record. A body that opens a record it is frightened of is a body that will say so later and not sooner.*

"On the twenty-third of this month," said Lissa Vale, from about four feet off, in a yard in the high country, to about eleven people and one of her own fen, "a woman opened her own family's stack with her own knife in about five hours, and it took her until the fourth hour before her hands would hold the blade, and she did it alone because she would not let three other people do it, and she was right about that and I would like the fen to hear that I was right about it too, and I would like nobody to say thank you to either of us, because the two of us did the same job in different yards and only one of us was frightened.

"**I would not open it and somebody else did, and I was right not to open it, and the cost of being right is that I am standing in this yard hearing about a thing I could have read.** That is the whole of what I get out of it and I would like it entered in the same words, and I would like the reader to be the man of about fifty, and I would like him to read it twice, once for the part where I was right and once for the part where it cost me something to be."

And the man of about fifty read it twice, in that order, and the clerk was not in the room and Lissa Vale wrote the entry in her own hand and signed her own name and not the clerk's, which is the second time she has done that on that shelf, and the fen entered afterwards that a body with a clerk does that once in a season if it is lucky.

---

And the last of the twenty-third was the thing that turned it, and it was not turned by the finding and it was turned by a person.

Merrid Ollvar asked, in the ordinary way, at about the sixth hour, standing in the light off the west wall with her hands behind her back, what the fen intended to do with it, and the answer she got was a sentence from Lissa Vale that nobody had asked for and that was the whole of the resistance of that day.

"It is not evidence," said Lissa Vale, "until this body says it is. And the moment this body says it is, then a family that has had pressed rootwood against a north wall for longer than anybody can date has thirty-one years of not knowing what was in it, and that is going into a book in four hundred miles and into an office in nine, and there is no way of getting it back out of either. So I would like the fen to say out loud, today, in this yard, that it is not treating this as evidence, and that it is treating it as wood, and that if the fen ever does treat it as evidence then the date it did it is in the book and so is the reason."

And the man of about fifty said that was the fen's standing and had been for a year, and the man of about seventy asked for his initial to be put against nothing, and the fen entered that a family's thirty-one years had become a thing that was not yet a thing in a ledger, and that the person who had done that was a woman of about fifty-four with a cut hand and a stack of waste open in her own yard.
