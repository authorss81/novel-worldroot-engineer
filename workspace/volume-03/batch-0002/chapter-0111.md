# Chapter 111

## The Maintenance Sequence

The sound wood was dated on the twenty-fifth and the twenty-sixth, in the lee of the north wall, with the wind off the ice and the lamp going, and it came out where nobody expected it, and the place it came out was inside Merrid Ollvar's own thirty-one years.

Marek Vale said the method out loud before he used it, which is his standing, and Lissa Vale entered it in the seed-route ledger in her own hand with the hour on it, and it is the same method and it is the whole of the science there is to offer.

"A rootwood seam is not a tree," he said. "It does not make a ring in a year the way a trunk does. It makes a layer while it is alive, and a layer is laid down in a season, and while a seam is standing out of the ground and working it lays one every year, and if you cut its face clean you can count the layers with a lens and a lamp and a good light, and you can count them from the outside in, because they are in order and there is no way to get them out of order.

"And the size of a layer on this shelf, which I measured on a stump in the lee of the stone wall yesterday and measured twice, **is about a twentieth of an inch in a year, which is an eighth of what a pear limb puts on here**, and I do not know why and I am not going to pretend to.

"So I count the layers and not the thickness. One layer, one year. And if a man has cut a length of rootwood and stood it up in the weather for thirty years and it has laid down thirty layers, that is thirty years, and there is no room in that for a story about a man a long way off."

The layers on the nine lengths he read were, in the order he read them, six, six, seven, five, six, seven, six, five, and six.

Which is not twenty-six years, and not eleven, and not anything at all anybody on that shelf has been alive for as an adult, and it took the rest of the twenty-fifth to find out why, and the answer was under the bales and not in them.

**The bales had been pressed hot and the press had cooked the outside and the outside had gone on laying down nothing at all**, and a pressed length lying against a bale that is pressing against it gets its own layers from the bale and not from the ground. The top of the pile had six. The bottom of the pile had six. The middle of the pile, at the wall's foot, where the press did not reach, had twenty-eight, going back without a break.

And the middle of the pile was dated separately with the same method, and it took a man most of a day, and it came out as twenty-eight layers and therefore twenty-eight years, **and at four places in those twenty-eight there is a band where the layer is thicker and the wood is different from the wood above and below it**, and he read the four, and the gaps between them are seven years, seven years, and six years, and he is not going to round them for a woman in her own yard.

And the number was stated in the open, in a yard, with a woman standing in it.

"Twenty-eight," said Marek Vale. "Twenty-eight years, and four times in them, and the last of the four about six years back, and the gap is seven years, seven years, and six years, and I have given you the gaps because a man who comes back on a rotation of seven years is a different thing from a man who comes back when he can, and I have not got the least idea which of those he was and I am not going to stand in your yard and pretend that the difference is nothing.

"**Twenty-eight years is inside your father's time and possibly inside your own. This was not cut by somebody from nine miles down the road. It was cut by somebody in this yard or within walking distance of it, and he was alive, and this shelf is his ground.** And I said at the gate on the first of this month that I would not guess in a yard, and I am not guessing, I am telling you what the layers say, and the layers are the only witness I have and I would like you to understand that I have gone past every other thing I could have said."

---

And the sequence, and it is a hand's method and not a vision, and no person in that yard saw anything at all.

The lengths came off the wood in an order, and the order is not an order you would keep by accident, because the wood is in the ground and the wood in the ground does not tell you what is next.

What a man does when he takes rootwood out of a seam is take what is loose and what is easy, and what is easy is the outside, and a man who has pressed rootwood for a living does the opposite: he takes the outside first, because the outside dries and it will not press if it is left, and he takes it down to the wet, and then he waits for the wet to go off the rest, and then he takes the next, and he does not take the core until the core has had its own season and has stopped, because a core cut live will bleed on the press and the bale will go at the seam.

**A man pressing rootwood for a living does not take what he wants. He takes what he can, in the order he can take it, and the order is the same every year, and the order is written into the length of every piece he cuts because the piece is a witness to when it was cut.**

And all four of the bands are in that order, and the bands are the same work done four times in twenty-eight years with six or seven bare years between them, and a bare year is a year in which nothing came off that seam at all, and I did not find a reason for the bare years and I have not looked for one.

"The pressing was not the point," said Marek Vale, at about the fifth hour on the twenty-sixth, with the lamp in the lee's mouth and his hands dead from the cold. "The pressing is the thing you do to a thing that has been cut. **The point is that the lengths came off in a particular order because the wood had to come off in that order, and a man does not arrange that by accident, and a man does not go on doing it for four years by accident either.** This is a maintenance order. It has steps in it. And the steps are written in the wood the way the eleven cuts are written in the row, and I have got the same tool in two places in this valley now and one of them is eleven trees and one of it is a heap of waste against a wall, and I am not going to stand in a yard and tell a woman that her family's waste is a list."

And then the object, and it was found by the woman, not by the man.

---

Merrid Ollvar found it at about the fourth hour of the twenty-sixth, on her own knees in the pit at the wall's foot where the last of the lumps had come out, with a short piece in her hand about the length of her forearm, and she called out to the yard and did not come out with it because she has been on that shelf thirty-one years and she does not take a thing out of a wall without somebody seeing it go.

"Get Lissa Vale," she said. "Get the woman who wrote the book about it. And get the licensed man and bring him to me, and do not touch anything else, and I am going to stay exactly where I am until you get here because I have read one book in my life about a thing like this and I have not read it since I was nineteen."

There were three of them, in three bales, at the core, where the press did not reach, and there were no more than three in the whole of the sixty-one, and the three of them came out of the fourth band, that is, about six years back, and the fourth band is in the twenty-eight-year-old wood, and a person on that shelf has been at it within living memory.

They are grafts. Cut, fitted, and bound, by hand, with a strip of wet sacking and a length of bast that has gone to powder on the outside of one of them and not on the other two. A scion of a different kind of rootwood into a rootwood, cut to a face on both sides, and the face of the stock and the face of the scion fit, and where they meet there is a collar of new wood that did not exist before the two were put together, and it has grown over a joint and it is holding.

**There is no nursery within a hundred miles of that shelf.** There is a seed house four hundred miles off a road nine days long. There is a man at the second house with a spade and there are four houses and eleven acres and a buyer on the seventh of next month, and a family that has never signed a piece of paper in its life, and nobody in any of it knows what a graft is, and there are three living grafts in the waste at the foot of a wall.

Coll Renner went down into the pit on his knees and was in it a long time and did not say anything at all, which is not his usual way, and then he came up and sat on the wall with his hands on his knees and said the sentence that is the whole of what that yard found.

"Yes, it is a graft," he said. "I will say it in the plainest words I have got and then I will say the other thing and the other thing is the one I would like entered first.

"**Somebody made this on purpose, they made it to keep, and they put it in with the waste.** And that is not how a man keeps a thing he means to use. A man who means to use a graft takes it out of the waste and sets it in the ground, because a graft in a press is a graft in the dark and it grows at a fortieth of what it wants and the join at the heel takes water from nobody and it is dead in four years. It is a graft that was never finished and it was not lost by accident, because a thing that is lost by accident does not get bound in bast. **That is how a man hides it.**"

Nobody in that yard said the word after him. About two hundred people have said that word about that stack in the two days since, and none of them said it in this yard on the twenty-sixth, and one of them said it and was not answered, and the entry says so.

"Then somebody was hiding something in my yard," said Merrid Ollvar, at about the sixth hour, standing up out of the pit with her hands black to the wrist. "And I am not asking which of us and I am not asking when, and I am going to say the rest of it and then I am going to stop talking, because I have been talking since the eighth hour of the twenty-third and I have not yet asked the one question that matters. **If he was hiding it then he thought he was coming back for it. A man does not hide a thing from a man who is standing in the same yard.**"

And nobody answered her, and the reason nobody answered her was in the ledger, and it is the reason nobody is going to answer her now: the fen is not going to be the body that stands in a stranger's yard and says to a woman of fifty-four that the answer is a person in her own family, and a person in her own family is not the fen's business, and the fen entered that it declined to ask and that the question was available to anybody in that yard and was taken by one person and refused by nobody, and that the refusing is the finding.

And Coll Renner said the thing that keeps the door open, and he said it because he is a licensed man and a man who has been licensed for twenty-two years is the only person in that yard who is paid to be careful, and it is the sentence of the day and it is not a speech.

"I will tell you the one thing I can defend and the one thing I cannot, and both of them go in the book in my own hand," he said. "I can defend this: **a graft in a press is nobody's hobby.** A man who grafts for pleasure grafts a fruit tree and grafts it over a fence and shows it, because that is the whole of the pleasure and there is no other half of it. This is a rootwood graft, fitted by hand, at a place with no nursery in it, and it went into the waste and not into the ground.

"And I cannot defend this: **I cannot read intent in wood.** I have been licensed twenty-two years and I cannot tell you whether a man who puts a graft in with the waste is hiding it or keeping it, because the two are the same action and only the man knows and he is not here. So I am not going to say he was hiding it, and I am not going to say he was keeping it, and I would like the book to hold both of those as unread, and the man of about fifty to read them out, and the fen to go on being a body that does not know."

Underneath the day's work, dated the twenty-sixth, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the twenty-eighth note under section nine of the charter schedule, and there is no charter, and the note says so on its face.

*Sound rootwood from the opened stack dated by counting the layers with a rule, a lens and a lamp, a layer measured off the ground at a twentieth of an inch and said out loud before it was used. Six layers at the outside of the press and twenty-eight at the core, and the press explained. Four bands in twenty-eight years, at intervals of seven years, seven years and six years, the last of them six years back, which is inside this household's own time and was not expected to be. The lengths cut in a maintenance order, because a man who presses rootwood for a living takes what he can take in the order he can take it, and the order is the same every time, and the order is in the wood. A tool with a shoulder on it, a single bevel on one side, the same side, in a second place in this valley. Three grafts in three bales at the core of the pile where the press did not reach, fitted by hand and bound in bast, in rootwood stacked as waste, on a shelf with no nursery in it, by somebody who was alive. A licensed man's finding that it is a graft and that a graft in a press is nobody's hobby, and his refusal to read intent in wood, and the fen declining to ask the question in that yard and the declining entered.*

*This is a record and not an argument, and the sentence is off this note for that reason, and the reason is written here so that the chain cannot be seen to have dropped one.*
