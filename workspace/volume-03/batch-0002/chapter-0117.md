# Chapter 117

## The Buyer Comes

Marek Vale wrote the answer on the seventh of next month, at about the fifth hour of the afternoon, in the seed house, at the north end of the bench, and nobody had asked him for it, and he gave it to a man hired at the change of horses for the purpose, and he did not tell the man what it was.

He wrote it in about four minutes and it is nine lines and it is in the seed-route ledger in his own hand with the hour on it, and Lissa Vale read it back, and the reader for it was the man of about fifty, and the man of about fifty read it back twice and did not like it and read it back anyway.

> The thirty-first of the row fruited on the sixteenth of a month three months back and it fruited eleven weeks out of the season of that row, and the row had not fruited since the same month the year before. The fruit was sound, thirty-one of it, laid on boards in a stone room, none spoiled, none bruised, one cut open in a yard at Lantern Reach on the third of this month with the flesh and the core sound.
>
> A graft does not do that. A tree that has been grafted goes on being a tree and the wood says what the scion does, in the scion's order. So whatever put that set in is not in the fruit and is not in the row's memory. **Something is going along an order in that row, and the order did not begin with the season, and the order is the thing and not the fruit.**
>
> That is the whole of what I can defend. I have not read the row this season and I am not going to guess at the four older cuts on the next row. **I am not telling this household what to do with a hundred and forty-one trees and I am not going to, and if this sheet is read as advice then it has been read as something it is not, and I would like the reader to read it again and say that out loud.**

And the man of about fifty said that out loud.

"That sheet is not advice," he said. "**It is a fact about a row and a date, and it went up the road nine days behind a buyer who is at this moment in a yard with a paper on a bench, and everybody in this yard knows that and the fen has entered that it knows it.** Read it again and say it again, because the man of about fifty has just said it twice and he is not going to be thanked for saying it and neither am I."

And Anneke Roos put the hire in the compost line at four shillings a day and entered that this holding has now paid two men to carry paper to a shelf four hundred miles off inside three days, **that the first of the two was asked for and the second of the two was asked for by nobody, and that the fourth hour of a day is the cheapest thing this body has ever bought and it is not going to be on the list of things it is proud of.** And the hired man left at about the sixth hour with the sheet, and the sheet would be on a shelf four hundred miles off in about nine days, and the fen entered both facts on the same line and did not put them in the order anybody wanted them in.

The buyer was in Merrid Ollvar's yard on the seventh of next month from the seventh hour in the morning, and the household had decided by the time the sheet would arrive.

---

The buyer is the buyer, and he is a man with a paper, and the paper is four lines and a figure and a day, and it is on the bench in the yard at Cauldron Reach at about the seventh hour on the seventh of next month with about fourteen people standing round it, and nobody says his name twice.

> Offer, the seventh of next month, four seasons the same. **Nine shillings the hundredweight**, at the barn door, weighed in the yard, on the day, and not on a day after it.
>
> The fruit is taken **as it stands at picking** and the buyer does not warrant it against frost, hail, scab, wind-burn, or **any fault of the ground.**
>
> This offer is for the season and does not return. The buyer is not asking what is in the ground and has not asked in four seasons.
>
> Weight at the barn door, two men, a scale, and the count said out loud.

Nine shillings the hundredweight, the same four seasons, and he has not warranted the ground. He has warranted himself against the weather, and this is the same clause Merrid Ollvar read out in a barn on the tenth of last month with her finger on it, and it has not moved, and the buyer is not a man who has been hiding a trick.

He is about fifty, he has a coat for the wind, and he does the thing that makes him not a villain, which is that he gives the figure before he is asked for it, in the open, in a yard, with a clerk four hundred miles off holding a pencil he is not going to be able to use.

"Nine shillings," he said. "The same as four seasons back, and the same as the season before that, and I have never once put it up in a year when a thing was wrong, and I have never once put it down in a year when a thing was good, and my factor at the counting house has asked me twice why I do not price a season, and I have told him twice that I do not price a season because I have never in four seasons had anybody tell me what the season was."

And then somebody told him, and it was not the fen and it was not the man with the spade and it was not asked for.

Merrid Ollvar told him at about the eighth hour, standing in the yard, with fourteen people in it and about a hundred and nine hundredweight of fruit in a stone room behind her, and she told him in about nine sentences and she told him the way she had said everything for three weeks, which is that she says the whole of a thing in one go and then waits.

"The fruit is not ordinary," she said. "A row of my pears fruited on the sixteenth of a month three months back and it fruited eleven weeks out of the season of that row, and the row had not fruited since the same month the year before, and there are four men in this district who can tell you what that is and three of them are in this country and the fourth of them is four hundred miles off and has not been able to tell me. **I do not know whether it is a harvest or a fault. Nobody knows. That is the truth about it and I have had eleven weeks of it and four days of strangers in my yard and I am telling you the whole of it before you have said a figure to anybody, because you have four seasons of a price on a paper and I have three weeks of not knowing, and you have a right to both of them before you weigh anything.**"

"And I am not asking you to change your price and I am not asking you to promise to come back. I am telling you, and you may do as you like with it, and if you take it at nine shillings then that is the whole of the transaction and I will not say another word about it, and if you do not then I will not say a word about that either."

And the buyer stood in the yard and thought about it for about as long as it takes to look at a sky.

He said the sentence, and it is the sentence, and it is the whole reason anybody is standing in that yard.

"**In four seasons nobody has ever given me a reason,** and I have learned not to ask for one. It costs me nothing not to ask and it saves me a great deal of standing in a yard like this one. So I am not going to say thank you for it, because it does not change what I came for, and I am not going to say I do not believe you, because I have not got a way of knowing and I am not going to invent one at a barn door."

---

The weighing took until the second hour and it was done in the yard and the count was said out loud and it was **one hundred and nine hundredweight**, and two men had a scale and Merrid Ollvar stood over it and did not let anybody round the figure.

And the sum is the largest figure said out loud in a yard in three years, and it was done four hundred miles off, on a bench at Lantern Reach, on the eighth of next month, and it was read out of the seed-route ledger by the reader of this body in a barn on a shelf to about twenty people, and here it is, entire, and the reader read it twice.

> *From the entry of the twenty-ninth of this month, in the ledger, and entered in the ledger, in my own hand, and read out on the eighth of this month on a shelf, and I am four hundred miles off and I am not guessing anything.*
>
> *One hundred and nine hundredweight at nine shillings the hundredweight is **forty-nine pounds and one shilling.***
>
> *Here is the comparison, and I am going to say it once and I am not going to be asked to defend it twice. **Forty-nine pounds and one shilling is more than this holding has taken in since the charter was made.** Not more than the repair cost. More than everything. The repair is standing. Sixty pounds ten is unpaid and is a different figure and is not mine to spend and is not in this sum. And the four pounds five in the tin is not in this sum either.*
>
> *And it is the largest figure that has been said out loud in a yard in this district in three years, and I would like somebody in this yard to sit with that for a minute, because a figure that size is a life and a figure that size is also a very ordinary day's money to a man with a factor at a counting house, and **both of those are in it and I am not going to take either out.***
>
> *And the last thing. **I have not got that price in this book.** I have got that a figure was read out in a yard on a shelf on the seventh of this month by a buyer and that the price was in it. I have not got the price, because a price in a book that this body did not hear said by the person who owes it is a price this body has guessed at, and I do not guess at prices, and I have asked for the price to be sent to me on paper, and I would like the reader to read that back and I would like the clerk to enter that I asked.*

And the man of about fifty read the last two lines back and then read the first three back and the yard went quiet for about a minute, and the man at the second house said it into the quiet.

"So the biggest number anybody has said out loud in this fen for three years is a number this fen cannot check," he said. "**That is the first honest sentence anybody has said about money since the flood.**"

---

And the household decided at about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the seventh of next month, in daylight, in a yard, with the buyer standing in it and about twenty people in it, and the fen was four hundred miles off and was not asked and was not consulted, and the not-asking is entered with the hour and it is correct.

They sold all of it. Nine shillings the hundredweight, at the barn door, weighed in the yard, and they set aside a fifth for the four houses before anything was carried out of the stone room, and the reason they set aside a fifth took about two minutes and was not a virtue.

The reading came first. The man at the second house read it, because he can read, and the figure and the count were said out loud, and the four lines about the fault of the ground were read out loud, and then the paper went back in the buyer's coat.

And the reason for the fifth was this, and it is the only reason and it was said by Merrid Ollvar and it is in her own hand on the back of the reading because she wrote it down before the money was counted, which is her way and has been her way for thirty-one years.

"Because a household of four that sells everything has nothing in the stone room on the eleventh of this month, and because we are four households and one of us is sixty-one, and because I have had the money in my head since the eighth hour and I would like it out of my head and in the drawer with somebody else's name on the account, and I am not going to stand in a yard and pretend to anybody that I did not say that first."

And nobody said a word about the row of eleven cuts, and nobody said a word about the older four, and the buyer's paper says he has not asked what is in the ground and did not ask it, and nobody in that yard told him about the eleven cuts, and that silence is entered and it is the second silence this month that has been entered, and the fen entered that it is going to have to learn to count them, because a body that enters some silences and not others has a habit and not a rule.

And the buyer put his hand on the last of the crates and said the other half of his sentence, which he had not said before, and he said it flatly and to nobody in particular.

"I will not come back after this season," he said. "**That is not a threat and it is not an opening. I have four hundred acres of my own on ground that is flat and watered and never once late, and a shelf four hundred miles from a road is not a place a man builds a trade on, and the only reason I am here is that this fruit is early and I can put it in a market in a fortnight when nobody else has anything.** If this fruit goes on coming eleven weeks out then somebody will find that out in a year and I would rather not be the one who is asked about it in a court. So: one season, one price, no promise, and I am not sorry about it and I am not going to pretend to be."

And nobody in that yard believed that and nobody in that yard disbelieved it, and the fen entered it in the ordinary words, and did not enter an opinion about it, and the buyer counted his money out on a trestle in the barn and went down the plateau road with two loaded carts and about a hundred and nine hundredweight less a fifth of fruit off a shelf where a woman had kept pressed rootwood against a north wall for thirty-one years without knowing what was in it.

The sum of forty-nine pounds and one shilling was not sent to the fen. The price was sent on the eighth, on a sheet with a firm's name at the head, and it was the same nine shillings, and the fen entered it, and the reading entered it, and the crate entered it, and the row of eleven cuts entered it, and none of those five went anywhere, and all five are the same week, and the fifth clause of the second of last month says that the record of this route is one record and no part of it is anybody's own, and the reader read the fifth clause out at the gate of that yard on the eighth of this month to a buyer who was on his way down four days of road and would be gone by the second hour of the ninth, and the buyer did not stop, and the fen entered that he was not asked to stop, and that not stopping is not refusing to hear.
