# Chapter 109

## The Seventh Hour

The leak had never had a rule.

That is the whole of it, and it took two volumes to be able to put the two halves of it in that order, and on the twenty-first of this month, which was a Tuesday, at about the seventh hour, in the fen, with three people in the yard, a woman of about twenty-four measured a leak of five-eighths of an inch twice with two rules and said the figure out loud to two men who had never heard it.

Gael Senn is the examiner of this holding and she keeps the books while four people are off a plateau, and there is no appointment in writing and there has never been one, and the pump is at the end of a hard standing about forty paces outside the north gate and it runs off the head and it makes the sound it makes, which is a sound four people in this yard could find in the dark.

She did it the way she has done it for two volumes and nobody asked her how, because nobody has ever asked her how.

The pump on. A finger on the joint at the housing, which is where the water goes and not the pit, and the pit was not up and had not been up since the spring. First rule, the plain one, and five-eighths. Second rule, the one that reads smaller on that corner, and five-eighths, and the second rule does read a shade smaller than the first on that corner and did so in the spring, and the figure does not depend on which rule a person reaches for. Then the pump off, and the housing drained, and the finger wiped, and both figures written down with the hour on them and read back before they were initialled.

"There," said Dunmar Fitch, who is fifty-eight and is the clerk of this body and whose standing is that he may enter an observation unasked, and who was standing at the gate with the book open on the top of the wall. "Five-eighths. Say it again in front of the other one so that the two of them have it and not one of them."

"It is five-eighths of an inch," said Gael Senn, "and the second rule reads smaller on that corner, and the pit is not up, and it is the twenty-first of this month at the seventh hour."

And the man of about fifty, who had been standing at the gate with his hand flat on the post the whole time and had not said anything, said one word.

"Say what?"

"That is the first time in two years I have heard that number said out loud in a yard," he said. "It is in the book. I have read it in the book. I have never once heard a person say it."

---

And the entry the clerk made is the finding, and it is in his own hand, and it was read back, and the hour went on it.

> Leak at the pump housing, return main, read at the seventh hour of the twenty-first of this month, twice, with two rules, the second of them the one that reads smaller on that corner, pump on, water into the housing and not into the pit, the pit not up: **five-eighths of an inch.**
>
> The last entry in this body's book concerning this leak is the seventh hour of the eighteenth of last month, which is **thirty-three days** back. The figure then entered was five-eighths of an inch. The figure now entered is five-eighths of an inch.
>
> The figure in the book was therefore thirty-three days old and has been carried forward in this body's ordinary use of it as though it were a current figure. **Nobody in this yard decided to carry it forward.** The carrying forward is a thing a book does and not a thing anybody did.
>
> The party left Lantern Reach for the plateau on the seventh of this month, which is **fourteen days** back. That is a different interval and it is not the reason. **Eighteen days of the gap are days on which the whole of this body was standing in this yard**, and the party was not on the road for any of them, and the measurement does not need four people in it and never did.
>
> **This body cannot now say on which mornings the figure was taken and on which it was not.** There is no rule, no day, no hour and no owner for it in any book, and it has been kept for two volumes by one person as a habit, and a habit that is never written down leaves no account of itself, and the absence of the account is not evidence of any day in particular.

That was read back by the clerk and read back after him by the man of about fifty, and the man of about fifty asked for his initial to be put against nothing, which is his standing and which has never cost this holding anything.

And the fen entered the third thing, which is the one the book does with.

"The figure is five-eighths and it has not moved and it is the same figure as on the eighteenth of last month," said Gael Senn. "I want it entered that a figure which does not move is not an answer and is not a relief. It has never been mended and nobody has looked for a cause. I am not going to write in a book that five-eighths is where it has always been, because five-eighths on the eighteenth of last month and five-eighths on the twenty-first of this month is two mornings and not a record."

---

The man of about seventy was fetched at about the ninth hour, and he was fetched because two people went and got him, and that is in the book too.

They brought him out to the wall he sits against with his stick and a folded blanket over his knees, and it is the fourth time in a season he has been brought out to a thing, and he is the only person in this holding who has never once been asked what he thinks of anything and who answers when he is asked.

He was told the figure and he was told the two rules and he was told the pit, and he asked one question, and the question was not about the leak.

"Who owns it?"

"There is no owner," said Dunmar Fitch. "There is no rule, no day, no hour and no appointment. It is hers, in the sense that she is the one who does it, and it is nobody's, in the sense that nobody has ever asked her to and nobody has ever said what it was for."

"Whose is the word *it*," said the man of about seventy. "Not whose is the leak. I know whose the leak is. Whose is the doing of it, at the seventh hour, on most mornings, for two years, in a body that has kept seven practices by choice since a charter lapsed at midnight."

Nobody answered him for a while, and the pump came on by itself because the pressure in the head comes on and goes off on its own, which it has done since before the charter was made, and about nine people came into the yard over the course of the morning because a pump going and a woman with a rule is not a thing anybody walks past.

"It is mine because I do it," said Gael Senn. "It has been mine since before the charter and it is not a decision anybody made. I would like it said that I did not choose it either. I was nineteen and I was the only one here at the seventh hour and I have a hand for a joint and I used it."

"And you have never once written it down."

"I have written the figure down. I have written it down thirty-nine times, I would guess, and I have put the hour on it, and I have never once written down that it is to be done, or why, or who wanted it, because nobody ever asked me for any of those three and I did not think to ask myself for them."

---

And then the sentence that is the hinge of it, and the man of about seventy said it from the wall with his hands on the blanket, and it is the sentence this whole batch turns on, and the fen entered it whole and nobody thanked anybody for it.

"A rule that has to be remembered by the person it was made for is not a rule," he said. "It is a habit with a number in it. And I am the only person in this yard old enough to have watched one of the other six get started, so I will tell you what happened to that one, and then you will decide about this.

"The return main was kept open every winter for three winters, and it was kept open by Jonas Byre and by me and by Ilsa Kern, and the three of us did it because we were the three who were there. And then Ilsa Kern went to her sister's for a month in the bad year and it was not done, and nobody in this yard knew it had not been done until the third week of the following spring, and by then there was ice in the low end of it and about nine hundredweight of a return that had to be dug out by hand in the wet. Jonas Byre said in this yard afterward, standing exactly there, that the rule had not failed. The rule had never existed. There had been three men who remembered it.

"So put it in the book," he said. "Put it in with a name against it and a day against it and let the man of about fifty read it out. I am not against it. I want it to say that it took two volumes and a gap that cannot be dated, and that nobody proposed it, and that it was proposed by the man of about seventy at a hard standing by a pump at the ninth hour of the twenty-first of this month and not by the steward and not by the engineer and not by the woman who has been doing it."

Jonas Byre was in the yard by then, and he is the man the story is about in the second half of it, and he did not want it softened and he said so before anybody asked him to.

"He has told it right," he said. "I was one of the three. It was not Ilsa Kern's month away that stopped it. It was the month before that, in the same year, when I was in the low quarter with my own father's leg and could not get across the ditch, and the man who is standing here now as the man of about seventy did it himself for three weeks and did not say, and by the time anybody noticed there was ice in the low end.

"And I am not going to have it put in that book that I forgot it, because I did not forget it. **I was the one who remembered it, and remembering it was the whole of the work, and the day I was not able to remember it the work was not done and nobody knew for three weeks.** And I am forty-eight and I do not get up at the fifth hour any more and I do not go down four feet on a ladder, and I would like those two facts in the same book as the figure, with my name against them, because the man of about seventy is talking about men of about fifty and I am one and I am telling him what it is like to be one at this hour and not at the seventh."

And Dunmar Fitch entered all of that with the hour on it, and read it back, and the man of about fifty read it back after him, and asked for his initial to be put against nothing, which is his standing, and the fen entered that the man of about fifty has now asked for his initial against nothing on six occasions this season and that nobody in the yard has ever suggested he stop doing it.

---

And the answer was not to drop it, and the answer was worse than dropping it, and it took the rest of the morning.

The fen entered the practice, in the ordinary words, with a name against it, and the name is the only one that can go against it, and the practice is not the leak and is not a repair and is not a matter of money.

> Reading of the joint at the pump housing, return main, at the seventh hour, with two rules, pump on, water into the housing and not into the pit, the pit not up. **Gael Senn.** The figure is entered with the hour and is read back before it is initialled. It is not a monthly figure. It has no day, because it has never had a due date and this body has decided not to invent one, and a due date on a thing that has been done on most mornings since before the charter would be a way of saying it was not being done.

And then the man of about fifty asked the question that is the other half of the chapter, and he asked it standing at the gate with his hand on the post, and he asked it about the figure and not about the woman, and he said so first.

"I want to ask it about the number and not about her," he said. "I have voted against this body three times and I am not going to make it four by going at a person who has been getting up at the seventh hour since she was nineteen.

"**A number nobody has heard is a number nobody is responsible for.** And that is not an argument for mending it, because it is not a fault anybody has got to be responsible for. It is an argument for the pump, and the rule that is now in the book says the figure goes in with an hour and a name, and that is a figure somebody is answerable for, and it is answerable to this yard on any morning anybody chooses to ask her for it, which is a different thing from the seventh hour.

"And I will say the other half, because I have said that all season and I am not going to stop now. The half is that the thing the road took out of this yard was not the yard and it was not the habit. Four people went up a road on the seventh of this month and three people are in this yard today and this figure is five-eighths, exactly as it was on the eighteenth of last month, and there is not one hour of the thirty-three in which a person standing in this yard could have said that number out loud to anybody. **What the road took out was the number of people a figure could be said to in front of, and that is a smaller thing and it is the one that is gone.**"

Underneath the day's work, dated the twenty-second, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the twenty-seventh note under section nine of the charter schedule, and there is no charter, and the note says so on its face.

*A leak of five-eighths of an inch measured twice with two rules at the seventh hour by a person who has been measuring it since before the charter, in a yard with three people in it, and the figure read out loud for the first time in two years. The last entry in the book concerning it the seventh hour of the eighteenth of last month, thirty-three days back, and the figure now and the figure then the same, and the figure in the book carried forward for thirty-three days as though it were current by nobody's decision. The road is not the reason and the yard was not empty, and eighteen days of the gap are days the whole of this body stood in that yard. A practice that was never written down, found to have stopped, and no day in it that anybody can name. A man of about seventy at the ninth hour asking who owns a habit, and the answer being that nobody knew it needed an owner until somebody went looking for a figure and the book had not kept it, and a rule that has to be remembered by the person it was made for not being a rule. And a figure in the book with a name against it and no due date, proposed by a man of about seventy at a hard standing and not by the steward, and the man of about fifty saying that what the road took out of this yard was not the yard.*

*An argument. This is worth nothing against a cut.*
