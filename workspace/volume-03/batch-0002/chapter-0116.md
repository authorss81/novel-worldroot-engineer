# Chapter 116

## The Household On The Shelf

The answer came back on the fifth of next month, seven days after the return went on the post at the change of horses, and it is a line in a book and the line is correct, and nothing in it is anybody's fault.

> District office, records. Fourth part, return of inventories. Reference as given.
>
> Received: the 28th. Entered against six items.
>
> **Held.** One.
> **Held.** Two.
> **Held.** Three.
> **Held.** Four.
> **Held.** Five.
> **Held.** Six.
>
> **No item on this return has been classified. No classification has been requested. Nothing is required of the holder further than that the return has been made.**

And then there was a second line on the same sheet, in the same hand, and it is the second line and it is the whole of what the office did, and it took seven days to come four hundred miles, and nobody in this yard can do anything about it.

> **The word "held" is a fact about a holder and not about a record. An item entered as held is not thereby in any part. The parts of this schedule are entered by this office and not by a holder, and a holder who states a part has stated a fact about the office's classification and not about the record.** A holder who has stated a part wrongly may be asked to state it again. A holder who has not stated a part has not said anything about the record at all.

It was read out in the yard at the eleventh hour on the fifth by Lissa Vale, and the clerk entered it with the hour, and the man of about fifty read it back and asked for his initial to be put against nothing, and then he said the thing that is the whole of it and that is the last thing anybody in this fen will learn about a form in this season.

"They have spent a fortnight of a records officer's time to tell us, in writing, on their own letterhead, that they have not touched it," he said. "Nobody asked them to say that. **A line saying *held* is a fact about a holder and not about a record, and a body cannot cite what is in part three and cannot say that part three is empty, and this office has just told us in a second line that what we did on the twenty-eighth was not a classification at all.** And the answer to line five is that the licensed man is not this holder's record and the office has written *held* against it, which means the office has now entered a fact about a holder about a thing that is not a holder's, and I would like somebody in nine miles to explain that to me in a year."

Nobody in that yard could explain it to him in a year, and the fen entered that, and entered that the second line is the reason this fen's return cost nothing and that the fen would have sent it anyway.

---

And the sixth was a morning in the seed house with the bay doors open and the well house door read at the tenth hour and the count of the readings of the seventh column standing at twenty-four, and the man of about fifty asked for the book, and he was refused, and the fen entered that as the whole of it and entered that nobody in that room was going to try again.

He asked it standing, in the ordinary way, with the reason attached, the way he does everything, and he said the reason first because he has learned that this yard cannot be trusted with a bare request.

"I am going to ask for the book and I am going to say why first, and I am not going to be the last one in this holding to want it, and I want that said now so that nobody can say afterwards that a man of about fifty walked in here and opened a thing on his own. **Their line on that sheet says they have not touched it. Their line says a holder has not said anything about the record. And the one thing this fen cannot do is answer a question about what is in that book, and it has been able to answer every question anybody has put to it for two volumes, and we are now in the position of a body that has hidden something in its own building and does not know what.**

"And I am not saying anybody has done anything wrong. I am saying that the man who filled in the fourth line of that return on the twenty-eighth described a reading and did not describe a book, and I know why she did it, and I put my own reason for wanting it in front of that, and it is a bad one. **I want to know what is on page three.**"

Lissa Vale said no.

She said it in one sentence, standing, and she did not give a reason in the first breath, and the fen has entered that there was a silence of about four seconds and that the silence is in the book and that a silence is not a reason.

"No," she said.

And the man of about fifty stood there and did not ask a second time, and did not ask at the door, and did not ask that night, and the man of about seventy said the whole of what happened next and it is two sentences and neither of them is a judgement.

"He will not ask again," said the man of about seventy. "That is not a virtue and it is not a decision. He will not ask again because there are eight other people in this yard and the answer was given in front of all of them in one word, and a man who asked twice in front of eight people would be doing it to make a point, and he does not make points, he stands at gates."

And Lissa Vale gave the reasons afterwards, in the order she has them, and they are the two reasons of the eighteenth of last month and she gave them because they have not gone anywhere and because a refusal with no reason in it is a thing a body has to be defended against and she would rather defend it.

"It may not be opened again in this season. Not by me, not by a licensed man, not by an officer of any district, and not by a person who comes up a road and says they are from an office. The first reason is that a book open twice in one season is a book somebody can say was altered between the openings, and I have not got a copy that would answer that, because the one I have is a copy of a reading and not a copy of a book.

"And the second reason I wrote down four weeks ago and it has not changed and it is the true one. It is my husband's book and I had it in a press under a ledger for nine years and did not open it once in nine years because I was afraid of it. I have opened it once, on a plateau, in a barn, in front of eleven people, and I am not opening it again, and that is a record-keeping decision and I am the only person who is going to write it down.

"And I will say the other thing, because the man of about fifty put a bad reason on the table in front of eight people and he is entitled to a true one. **I have read page three. I know what is on it. It is a sheet of district paper with an office's name at the head and one word on it in a dead man's hand, and it says *necessary*, and it gives no reason, and it tells a clerk to enter it against a man instead of against a node.** And I am not going to be asked in a seed house in front of nine people to say that word out loud, and anybody in this yard who wants to know what my husband wrote can come up the road on their own two feet and ask me in a barn with nobody in it, and I would like that entered as an offer and not as an invitation, because an invitation can be declined politely and an offer cannot."

Nobody in that room thanked her and nobody in that room was thanked, and the man of about fifty asked for his initial to be put against nothing and it went in.

---

And then the thing that came down off the shelf on the sixth, which was a hired man from the change of horses with four lines in Merrid Ollvar's hand and his name against three of them, and the hire of him went into the compost line at four shillings a day for a man going up and four for coming down, and Anneke Roos entered it in the ordinary words and entered that the line for this month is the sixth month's and the man went up on the fourth, before the line was even written, and that the fen does not know who put the four shillings there, and that somebody in that yard paid for it, and that the fen has decided not to ask.

> The figures were read out in your yard and I was not in it and I heard about it from the man at the second house, who is a better reader of a yard than I am, and he has told me what you did with the doors and the boards and I have been told that the man who put a well in the ground is four hundred miles off and cannot come and is not coming.
>
> Three ways and we have taken the one that costs all four of us, because the other three each cost one of us and one of us is not a person we are willing to spend. Four houses, one drawing each in turn, on a day letter, and the letter is on the stone at the well and the first house is a Tuesday and it is the worst of the days because there are four of us in it and the old woman is sixty-one and the pail is heavy in the heat.
>
> **We will do that for a month and look at it on the first of the month after, in daylight, at the well, with whoever is standing there.** We are not going to decide anything else before then. There is nothing about the fen in this and you were not asked and were not going to be.
>
> And the man at the second house says this, and I am putting his name to it because I will not put a man's words in a letter without his name on them. **You spent a bucket.** He says it flatly and he says it about the third and a half feet and not about the weather and not about my ground. He says he is not angry yet and he would like it entered that he said that in those words. And he says he will be in about nine days, and he says the reason he is not angry yet is what a woman of yours said to him in a barn on the fourteenth of last month, which was that a man who gave a thing away in writing before he did it cannot be helped today and there is nothing in him for you to have yet, and he says **that there is now about as much in him as there is going to be, and that he is giving himself until the ninth day to be angry with it, and that he would like the ninth day put in your book so that a man in nine miles cannot find out afterwards that it was never said.**

And the ninth day is the fifteenth of next month, and it is in the book, and the fen entered it as the day a man said he would come, and did not enter a reason, and did not enter an opinion, and did not thank anybody.

And Lissa Vale asked the question she asks, in the yard, of the four of them, on the sixth of next month, and it was about the four houses and not about the message.

"Who loses," she said. "Not the fen. Who loses. Four houses have lost three and a half feet of a bucket since the thirteenth of last month because a man from the fen read a root, and on the second of next month the same four houses have not had a household to ask about it, because the man who would have answered was four hundred miles off reading out figures in a seed house, and on the fourth of next month the man with the spade was asked a question about a day letter by a woman he has never argued with, and he said yes."

She stood there and then she said the other two things, and the fen entered both of the silences and the two silences are the entry.

"**Nobody in that yard has said that a man from the fen should not have come up that road, and nobody in that yard has said that he should have told them first.** Both of those were available at every hour of every day of nine days and twenty days of this month and neither was taken, and the fen has entered that neither was taken, and the fen has entered that it is not going to say which way it would have gone, and it has entered that it knows which way it would have gone, and it has entered that knowing is not the same as doing, and the reader for this entry is the man of about fifty and the clerk is to read it back including the last clause."

Underneath the day's work, dated the sixth of next month, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the thirtieth note under section nine of the charter schedule, and there is no charter, and the note says so on its face.

*A return of inventories sent on the twenty-eighth of last month and answered on the fifth of this month in seven days: six items entered as **held**, none classified, and a second line on the office's own sheet saying that the word *held* is a fact about a holder and not about a record, and that a holder has not said anything about a record. Read out in a yard, entered with the hour, read back by the reader after the clerk. A request for the fieldbook, made standing with its reason in front of eight people, and one word of refusal, and four seconds of silence entered, and a man of about fifty who did not ask again and the fen's own reason entered that he will not, which is not a virtue and is not a decision. The steward's two reasons and a third, which is an offer and not an invitation: any person who wants to know what her husband wrote may come up the road on their own two feet and ask her in a barn with nobody in it. A message of four lines down off the shelf in the occupier's hand with the man at the second house's name against three of them, a day letter for four houses, a month, and a day to look at it on. Three and a half feet of a bucket since the thirteenth of last month entered as a cost in four households' water and not as a fault of the ground. A man saying he is not angry yet and giving himself until the fifteenth of next month, and the ninth day put in the book so that a man in nine miles cannot find out afterwards that it was never said. And two silences entered whole.*

*An argument. This is worth nothing against a cut.*
