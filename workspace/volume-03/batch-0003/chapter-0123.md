# Chapter 123

## The Red Line

She gave it to him on the nineteenth of this month at about the ninth hour in the seed house, in front of about eleven people, and she gave him nothing else, and she said so in the first breath and then she did not say anything else for about two minutes.

"Marek Vale. Take it. It is a piece of a thing and it is not a document and it is not paper and you are not going to be able to copy it, and I am not giving it to you because I trust you. I have known you since you were nine and your father's hands are in that and there is no trust in this at all. **I am giving it to you because it is the only piece of the machine in the world and it is going to be in a building where people read things out loud every day of their lives.**"

And she put it into his right hand, which is the one that holds a rule, and it is a collar section about the length of a hand and two fingers wide, and it is steel and brass and it is not clean, and there is a cut in it.

Not a wrench mark and not a shears mark. A cut, made from the edge, on one side, and the metal at the edge of it is pushed away from the cut and not toward it, which is a thing about how a person moves a blade and not a thing about what was wrong with the metal. And a wire is not in it. There is a socket at one end with the terminals standing proud of it and nothing in the socket, and the wire that was in it is somewhere else in the world or is not anywhere, and she did not say which.

"It is out of the western salt corridor," she said. "**The rest of that relay is four hundred miles away in a district's storage, under a schedule of stored plant that I have never seen and am not going to describe to you, and I am not going to tell you the number of the schedule, and if I did it would be a number that a person could ask about.** Take that piece out of my hand and do what you like with it. I am not going to see it again and I would like that entered."

Coll Renner did the reading, and he did it with his own clip and his own book and about eleven people standing round a bench, and he said the thing he said at the sixth hour on a plateau in a barn three months ago, and he said it before he started, standing up, with the clip in his hand.

"Before I touch it. There is a bead set in that collar, about the size of a pea, and it is a live element and it has been out of a circuit for nineteen years. **I am not going to touch the bead and I am saying so in a room so that it is in the book that I said it.** Everything else on it I will handle. Everything else on it is a piece of a machine and a machine can be handled by anybody who is not a fool about a bead, and I am licensed and I have a clip, and I am not going to be careful in a way that makes a better show than the handling is worth."

And he did the reading, and it took about twenty minutes, and it produced four facts and none of them was a finding.

> Read on the nineteenth of this month, at about the ninth hour, at the seed house at Lantern Reach, on a bench, with a licensed man, a clip, his own book, and about eleven people present. **This is a contact and a reading and not a result**, and the use log is not written for it, because the use log is thirteen lines and records contacts and never readings and this is a piece of a machine and not the instrument.
>
> **One.** A collar section of a safety relay of a design of about nineteen years old. Steel and brass. Length, the width of a hand and two fingers. The socket at the upper end is empty. The bead is present and was not touched.
>
> **Two.** The cut is on one side. The metal at the edge of the cut is displaced from the cut and not toward it. It is not a wrench mark and it is not a shears mark.
>
> **Three.** The terminals stand proud. **A wire was in that socket and is not in it now.**
>
> **Four.** I can give no date for the cut. I can give no district and no schedule and no holder. **A reading of a piece of metal is not a witness.** I am licensed twenty-two years and I have said before that I cannot read intent in wood, and I will say the same thing about metal: I cannot tell you who held the blade, and I am not going to guess at it in a yard with eleven people in it.

And it went in with the hour and the man of about fifty read it back and asked for his initial to be put against nothing, and Coll Renner wrote the four of them in his own book in his own hand, and the fen entered that two men in that room now held the same four facts and that neither could be checked against the other, because one of them is a reading of a piece of metal and the other is a page.

And then the thing about the name, which she had not said out loud and which she said standing up in about thirteen words, and which is the standing from a long way back and is not negotiable in this yard or any other.

"Now the page," she said. "I want my name on it first and the engineer's under mine, and I want the reason in the same line, and the reason is the one I have given for nineteen years. **A licensed engineer's name on a thing is a promise that the thing is somebody's to answer for, and the somebody is me. I am a woman of fifty-six with no employer, no district behind me and no office that would stand in front of me at a hearing, and if this piece of metal is ever a matter for anybody then there has to be a person on it who cannot be reached by a letter.** And there is no certificate, because there is no certificate for a thing like this in any district in the world. Write both names and put mine at the head of the line, and let the man of about fifty read that back, and nobody has thanked anybody."

And then the finding, and it was found in public, at about the eleventh hour, in about four minutes, by the man of about fifty, who did not want to find it.

"We cannot copy it," he said.

---

**Sera Quill gave it away in a yard on the nineteenth of this month and this fen has tried four times in six hours to make a copy of it and cannot, and all four attempts are entered, because a body that has failed at something in public has to say so in public.**

Lissa Vale tried first, at about the eleventh hour, with a scribe, because a scribe is two shillings and a sheet of paper and is the oldest copying arrangement in the world. The scribe wrote out a description of a collar section, its length, its width, its two materials, the cut and the empty socket, in about nine words, and read it back, and Lissa Vale said no to the whole of it standing up in the yard.

Coll Renner tried second, with a tracing. He put a sheet of oiled paper on the collar and a pencil on the paper and got the shape of it in about a minute, and then he said the sentence, and a body that keeps a thing on a shape knows this sentence already.

"A tracing is a copy of a shape," he said. "**And the shape of it is the least of it. You could trace it, and I have just done it, and what you have in your hand is a sheet of paper with a silhouette of a machine on it. The cut is the thing. The socket is the thing. And a silhouette has neither.**"

Marek Vale tried third, and he did it himself, and he did it in a way that cost him something to admit, which is that he is the engineer of record and he tried to take it apart.

"There is a way to get at the middle of it," he said. "Two pins and a quarter of an hour and the shell comes off in three pieces, because it was built to be opened by a fitter in a shed. **And if I take it apart then what I have afterwards is three pieces of brass, and the cut goes with the piece it was cut in, and it is all of it still, and it is now in four bits in a bag in a seed house.** And if anybody in this yard is ever asked what was in the socket, I would have to say that I took it apart on the nineteenth of this month to see whether I could copy it, and I would rather be asked that than be asked what I did with the nineteen years it was in a drawer."

The man of about fifty tried fourth, by writing to a licensed man in a town two days off to ask him whether such a thing could be cast again, and he got as far as the sheet with the question on it, and he did not send it, and he said out loud that he was not going to.

"Because the answer would be a yes or a no and either way it is a document about a copy of a thing, and **this body has found out four times in six hours that it cannot put a copy of this anywhere, and the reason it cannot is the same reason it cannot list a sequence, and I am not going to be the man who works it out for a form.** So: it is not copied, it is not traced, it is not taken apart and it is not sent. It is in this building."

---

And the arithmetic, and it is Anneke Roos's standing, and she did it in the open in the yard on the twentieth of this month at about the tenth hour with about twenty people in it, and the sum got worse each time, four times, and the fourth time is the time the yard will remember.

"Right," she said, with her own book open on the wall side of the gate and a slate under it. "I am not going to do this with figures I have not got. I am going to do it with the ones she has said out loud, and where I do not have one I am going to write *not held* and read the *not held* out loud, and you are all going to hear how many of them there are."

"A red line on a relay of that kind is not a stop. **It is the announcement of a stop.** It sits at a fixed point in the middle of a run of country and it says, out loud, in that building, that the flow has gone above a line it is not to go above. The stop itself is at the top end of the corridor and it is mechanical and it takes a man and it takes longer than the pulse. The whole purpose of the red line is that it buys the man at the top end the length of the corridor in time to get there."

"**The western salt corridor is a hundred and forty miles.** A pruning pulse at full rate runs that in about **seventy minutes.** That is hers and she said it in the room on the seventeenth and it was not written down. So the red line gives a man at the top end of a hundred and forty miles about an hour and ten minutes, and if the stop takes longer than that then the pulse has already been somewhere it should not have been."

"**What an isolation buys.** One collar, closed by hand, takes about **twenty minutes** if a man is standing at it, and it holds the flow below the heartroots for about **four hours**, and then it has to be reset by hand and the same man is still standing at it. Four hours. That is hers too, and out of a mouth and not off a page, and it is the figure this whole morning has turned on."

"Now the sum, and this is where it gets worse, and I want the clerk to have all four of these."

"**Four thousand two hundred people died in the first winter.** In ten weeks, and the ten weeks came out of a mouth and is not in the nine lines. That is hers. And **nine thousand were displaced.** And that second figure is not hers, it is four districts and three offices, and I am going to say what it is made of before anybody puts a column on it: **nine thousand is a count of registrations.** It is a count of people who came and stood in front of a clerk and wrote their name down. **A person who cannot write does not get counted and a person who is dead does not get counted and a person who walked to the coast and stopped there does not get counted.** So nine thousand is a floor and not a total, and I will not do a sum with a floor in it and I will not do it in a yard."

"**The nearest ground high enough is thirty-one miles off the corridor.** That is hers, and out of a mouth as well. And a person walks about three miles an hour on salt flat in the dark, and I have walked on salt flat and it is worse than that and not better, so thirty-one miles is **about eleven hours and not ten.** Four hours is what the isolation buys. Eleven hours is what the ground costs."

So the sum is: **four hours against eleven hours, and the difference is seven hours, and four thousand two hundred people is what seven hours of a corridor that was not stopped comes to.** And I have got to the end of that sum three times this morning and I cannot finish it, and I am not going to finish it in a yard, and the reason is not that it is too hard. **The reason is that there is no version of the end of that sum in which four thousand two hundred is a figure anybody can reduce by a known amount.** She may be right. She may be right by a great deal. She may be right by four. **Nobody in this yard can tell by how much, and a body that wants the answer has to be willing to say out loud that it does not have it.**"

And then she said the other half, and it is the sentence of the twentieth.

"**A body that has spent a season putting every figure it has in the open is now going to have a figure in a yard that four people out of five were not in the room for. The fifth is a licensed man with a clip, and he has said he cannot read intent in metal, and he is right.** So the arithmetic has come down to a slate in a yard, and I have read out four figures and a *not held* on it, and there is one person in this yard who was in the room on the seventeenth and who is the only one in the country who can say whether the seventy minutes and the twenty minutes and the four hours are the figures she used."

And she asked, and the clerk entered it.

"Ask her to send them. Not the figures she is sure of, the other ones. The corridor's length from the district's own survey, and the pulse's rate, and the hour the pulse was logged at the top end, and the district's return on the ground above the corridor. **Because I cannot do this next part with a memory and a memory is not a column and I have said that in this yard before about a price.** Let the clerk write down that I asked."

> Entered at the eleventh hour of the twentieth of this month, at the north gate, on the standing of the money and the arithmetic, which is the standing of Anneke Roos: **the figures were asked for.** A request in the ordinary way, in a yard, with about twenty people in it, and no refusal, and no promise.
>
> **The figures are not in this book. They have not been sent. Nothing in this fen can say whether any of the four numbers spoken in that yard is the number it is.**

And on the same afternoon, in about the same hour, a man came off the change of horses with a sheet of paper in a woman's own hand, and the hire for him stood in the compost line at thirty-two shillings in two parts, four on the twelfth of this month and twenty-eight on the sixteenth, and the sheet was the well on a shelf four hundred miles off, and the figure on it was the one read on the sixteenth at the fourth hour by the occupier on her own nail, and it went into the ledger with the hour and the man of about fifty read it back, and the fen entered that it had been four days in the yard as a figure nobody in it had, and that the thirty-two shillings were for the paper and not for the figure.
