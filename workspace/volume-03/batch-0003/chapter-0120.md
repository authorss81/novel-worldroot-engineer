# Chapter 120

## What She Is Not Saying

The clerk asked her for a document on the thirteenth of this month at about the tenth hour, in the seed house, with the bay doors open and about eleven people in it, and he asked it standing, and he said the reason before he asked it, because that is the standing of this yard and because he is a man who learned it here in his first year and has never once let anybody talk him out of it.

"I am going to ask for a document and I am going to say why first, and I am not going to pretend I do not know what I am doing. This body can only enter a finding where there is a document to enter it from. That is not a rule anybody made. It is what a book is. **A thing that happened and that nobody can put a piece of paper behind cannot be entered, and what we do with it instead is stand in a yard and be loud.** And a return of inventories, a schedule of records held, a refusal clause, a count of mornings read out at a gate: every one of those is a document, and every one of them is a thing an office in nine miles can hold and put a part on. So: is there a document."

And she said no in eleven words, and the clerk counted them, because counting them is what clerks do and because he wanted the count in the book before anything else went in.

"No, because a woman who keeps a copy keeps a reason."

And she did not add to it. He waited about four seconds, in case she was going to, and she was not, and he wrote the count down and the hour on it and read it back before he initialled it, and the reader read it back after him.

> Entered at the tenth hour of the thirteenth of this month, in the seed house, the clerk present and the reader present, eleven people in the room.
>
> Asked for: **a document.** Refused. The refusal was eleven words. **The reason was in the eleven and not after them, and nothing was added to it, and the clerk waited four seconds for an addition and there was none.**
>
> The refusal is a good one and it is entered as a refusal. It is not softened and it is not numbered, because nothing in this chapter is numbered.

---

And then what she has, which is nothing, and it took about an hour to establish and it is the whole of the resistance of the day, because a body that cannot produce a document cannot enter a finding, and the whole of the machinery this body has built in two volumes is a machinery of paper.

"I have kept nothing," she said. "I want that said in the plainest words there is and I want it said by me and not asked out of me. **No copy. No extract. No log of my own. No note. No private ledger and no day book and nothing with a date on it. No letter to anybody about it, and I have not written to anybody about it, and there is nobody in the world I have told.**

"And I will tell you why, because you are going to find out the shape of it on your own in about a week and I would rather you had it now. **A woman who keeps a copy keeps a reason for keeping it, and the reason is never the reason she gives.** The reason she gives is that she may be asked. The reason is that she was not sure at the time, or that she wanted to be able to be sure afterwards, or that she was afraid. Every one of those is a thing I was, and I did not want a page in the world that said so, and that is not a principle. It is a woman of fifty-six doing the arithmetic about what a piece of paper does to a person who owns it, and doing it in nineteen seconds, the first time somebody handed me a form.

"And here is the part that is the part. In nineteen years there has never been a moment where the having of it would have helped anybody. Not once. I have gone over it. I did the whole of it again in my own head about a week ago on a road, on purpose, and I got the same answer twice."

"Then what have you got," said Coll Renner, from the back, with his own book shut under his arm.

"I have a piece of a thing," she said, "and it is not a document, and it is not paper, and you will be told what it is when I give it to him and not before. And I have told you that I have it, which is more than I have told anybody in nineteen years, and I would like the clerk to enter that I said that sentence out loud in a room and did not soften it."

---

And then the terms, and they are the card, and Lissa Vale wrote them out in her own hand on the fourteenth at about the ninth hour and the clerk entered them and the man of about fifty read them back and asked for his initial to be put against nothing.

> Entered at the ninth hour of the fourteenth of this month, in the seed house, by the steward, in her own hand, with the person named in the second line present and the clerk present and the reader present and a licensed man present with his own book. **Terms. Agreed. Not an order and not a request.**
>
> **One.** She will say what she did, in order, in a room, on the seventeenth of this month, in her own hand, and the steward will hold the page and a licensed man will be present.
>
> **Two.** It will be read out whole in that room and it will be read back to her in that room, and the figures will be read back as figures, and if a reader stumbles on a figure she will say it again and the reader will read it again.
>
> **Three.** **She will not be asked what she felt.** There is no column for it and she will not answer it.
>
> **Four.** **She will not be asked whether it was right.** There is no column for it and she will not answer it.
>
> **Five.** She will say the name of an office only if the name is on a paper in front of her, and if the name is on a paper in front of her she will not explain it, and the clerk will enter that the name was on a paper in front of her and will not enter anything further.
>
> **Six.** She is not a member of this body and has not been asked to be one and is not to be asked.
>
> **Seven.** She is to be given a room and not a hearing, and a room is not a hearing and a room is not a record, and the first of these was entered on the eleventh and this line does not take it back.
>
> The terms were read back to her once in a room with nobody in it and once in a room with about nine people in it, and the two readings agreed word for word, and the reader for the second reading was the man of about fifty and the clerk entered that he had compared them and that they agreed and that he is not going to be asked to do it a third time.

---

And the man of about fifty asked one question in the yard on the fourteenth, at about the fourth hour, and it was the wrong question and he knew it was the wrong question before he had finished it, and he asked it anyway, and it is in the book because he asked it in front of people.

"I am going to ask a question and I am going to say why first, because this yard cannot be trusted with a bare question and I have seen it try.

"**Who told you to do it.**"

And Sera Quill did not answer him.

And the yard did what a yard does, which is nothing, for about two seconds, and then Dunmar Fitch's pen went along the page, and the man of about fifty said the sentence that is his standing for the fourth time this season and that nobody in this yard has ever told him to stop using.

"Then it stands as asked and not answered, and I am not going to be the man who asked it twice, and I would like that in the book in my own hand. And I will say why I asked it, so that nobody can say afterwards that a man of about fifty went at a woman who has refused to take money. **I asked it because if there was somebody else, then the largest thing this fen is holding is not a woman and a piece of a machine. It is an office.** And an office can be answered, and I have spent a season learning that an office answers correctly and in seven days and does not have to be believed. **I wanted an office. I did not get one. That is a fact about me and not about her, and I put it in the book so that it is a fact about me and not a thing she has to answer.**"

> Entered at the fourth hour of the fourteenth of this month, in the yard, on a question asked by the reader of this body: **who told you to do it.**
>
> **The question was available to anybody in that yard and was taken by one person and refused by nobody. It was not answered. It is entered as asked and not answered.**
>
> **The refusal is not numbered. It has not been numbered in three years and is not going to be.** A refusal is not a currency. It has not been paid for, it buys nothing, and it is not going to be entered as though it were a thing somebody did for this body.

---

And the man of about fifty made the batch's first finding, standing, in the same yard, about ten minutes later, and it is the best thing he has said in a season and he would not be thanked for it.
"We have spent a season learning to publish everything," he said. "Every figure this holding has got has been read out in a yard with a person in front of it, and the man of about seventy has stood at a gate and made me read an entry out that I did not want read, and the steward has carried four hundred miles of arithmetic to a stranger's barn and read it out, and a body that does that is a body that has decided the only safe thing about a record is that everybody has heard it.

"And the one person who is going to tell us the largest thing we are going to be told has brought nothing to publish. **And that is not a defect in her.** It is the first thing anybody in three volumes has brought into this yard that could not be copied, and I do not know what to do with it and I am not going to stand in a yard and pretend that I do, because the alternative is that this body goes on publishing what is easy and calls it a practice.

"And I will say the other half, because this is the hour for it and because I voted against this body three times. **The half is that she is right, and being right is the worst of it.** A thing a woman says in a room cannot be checked by anybody. Not by an office in nine miles, because there is no paper. Not by a licensed man with a clip, because a reading is not a witness. And not by her, which is the part I have not been able to put down since the seventh hour this morning: **she cannot check it either.** She has put the only account of this in the world into a yard in a fen on somebody else's ground and she cannot take it back and nobody can hold her to any part of it, and if she has left one thing out then it is gone out of the world and the going of it is not on anybody's page, including hers."

---

The terms were read back to her twice and the second reading was in front of people, and the first one was not.

Lissa Vale did it herself, at about the eleventh hour, in the room at the back of the seed house with the door shut and no clerk and no reader and about a stove at the end of the corridor doing nothing for either of them. It took about four minutes. She read all seven lines off her own page and then she sat down on the stool, which she does not do in front of anybody.

"You will not be asked what you felt," she said. "That is the fifth line and it is mine and I put it in because you asked and I am not going to be the woman who hears you say it in a room with nine people in it and then spends a fortnight deciding whether it counted."

"I know what it is for," said Sera Quill. "I have never once had it in writing. That is not the same as never having had it."

"No. It is not the same. It is on a page now and it is a page with your name against the agreement and not against a promise, and a promise is worth what a person is worth at the end of a bad year and a page is worth what a clerk can find." Lissa Vale did not soften it. "That is the whole of what this fen can do for you and it is not much and I would rather say so on a Monday than find out on the seventeenth."

Then she read it again with nine people in the room, and the man of about fifty compared the two readings against each other on the page, word by word, and entered that they were the same.

---

And the man of about seventy was fetched at about the fifth hour, and he was fetched because two people went and got him, and that is in the book, and it is the fourth time in a season he has been brought out to a thing and the fourth time the thing has been about a person.

He was told what had been asked and what had been refused and what had been agreed and what had not, in that order, and he asked for the fourth one to be repeated, which was the piece of a thing that is not a document, and then he sat against the wall with the blanket over his knees for about a minute before he said anything at all.

"A body that has spent a year reading its own figures out loud in a yard has got no practice at holding a thing it cannot check," he said. "I have had that practice. I had it for about thirty years with a man who is not alive and I did it badly, and I am telling you so that you do not go and get it from him, because he cannot come.

"**You are going to get it from her, and she has had it nineteen years and has not done it well either, and neither of those is a reason to stop, and the two of them together are a very good reason to write down every hour of it.** Read that back and put my initial against nothing, which is my standing, and do not thank me, because I have been thanked twice in eleven years and both times it was a mistake."

Underneath the day's work, dated the fourteenth, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the thirty-second note under section nine of the charter schedule, and there is no charter, and the note says so on its face.

*A document asked for and refused in eleven words with the reason inside the eleven. A woman who has kept nothing for nineteen years and can show a body of this kind, on her own asking, why. A piece of a thing that is not a document and was announced in a room and not explained. Terms agreed between two people and written out in the steward's hand: what she did, in order, in a room, on a day she named herself, with a page held by a steward and a licensed man in it; the figures read back as figures; two questions that will not be asked and no column for either of them; a name that comes off a paper and no explanation. One question asked in a yard and not answered, and the refusal not numbered, and the reader of this body putting his own reason for asking it in the book so that it is a fact about him. And a man of about fifty standing in a yard saying that the largest thing this holding is about to be told has nothing to publish in it, and that this is not a defect in her, and that she is right, and that being right is the worst of it, because a thing a woman says in a room cannot be checked by anybody and cannot be checked by her.*

*This is a record and not an argument, and the sentence is off this note for that reason, and the reason is written here so that the chain cannot be seen to have dropped one.*
