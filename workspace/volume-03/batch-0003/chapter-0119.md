# Chapter 119

## A Woman On The Fen Road

She came into Lantern Reach on foot on the eleventh of this month at about the ninth hour, off the Glassward road and down the fen road, and she came the last of it alone, and the fen entered that she came the last of it alone because there was nobody at the gate but the ordinary business of a Monday and because a woman walking in alone at the ninth hour is not a thing anybody in this yard stops.

She gave her name once and did not give it twice.

"Sera Quill. Fifty-six. Retired root engineer, and I have not been a member of anything since I was thirty-one and I am not asking to be one now."

Dunmar Fitch is fifty-eight and is the clerk of this body and his standing is that he may enter an observation unasked, and he entered it with the hour and read it back before he initialled it. And then he did the thing that is this fen's practice with a stranger's name, which is to check it in front of the person, at the middle of the table, with the book open and the person standing on the side of the table where she can see the page.

"The name is in this body's own book in two places," he said. "I am not going to tell you it is nothing, because it is not nothing, and I am not going to pretend to you that it is a record, because it is not one either. Would you like to look at both of them."
She looked at both of them standing up, for about a minute, and she did not touch the page, and she read the second one twice.

"That is not a record of me," she said. "That is a record of somebody not being able to answer a question, and I have been the fourth one in a list of four for nineteen years and I would like this fen to have me in its book as the fourth one and not as a witness to a pear."

It went in with the hour, and the reader for it was the man of about fifty, and he read it back including that last sentence and asked for his initial to be put against nothing, which is his standing.

---

She was in the seed house at about the tenth hour with the bay doors open and about nine people in it, and Marek Vale was at the north end of the bench with a rule in his right hand and his left arm bare to the elbow, because the room is hot in the middle of a day and because that is the arm he works with. The four inches run along the inside of it and they branch twice and the color of a healed burn and not a bruise, and they have been the same four inches since the first man who wrote them down did, and a licensed man wrote them down on the twentieth of last month and again on the third of this month, and the second entry is longer than the first and is not better.

She looked at it for about as long as it takes to look at a sky.

And she said nothing about it, and the fen entered that she said nothing about it and did not enter why, and entered in the same place that the two accounts of that arm in this body's book do not agree and were not reconciled and neither of them is withdrawn, and that a body which cannot decide between two honest accounts of one arm is a body that has decided something.

"Eleven years I have had men tell me what their arms are like," said Lissa Vale from the door, with the ledger shut under her arm. "You are the first one who has looked at it and said nothing at all, and I would like it entered that I noticed, and not that I said it."

---

And then the three things, and the order they came in is her standing and is not a courtesy, and she said the first of them before anybody in that room had asked her a question.

"First. I have come without a fee and I am not going to take one, and I am going to tell you what a fee was for, because otherwise all of you will sit there for a week wondering whether I want something out of this."

"**A paid witness is a witness somebody bought.** That is not a way of talking and it is not something a district invented. A man who is paid is a man somebody has already decided what he is for, and he is paid the same whether he is right or wrong, and the only way anybody can tell the difference afterwards is by asking him questions until one of them hurts. I would rather be the other kind. I would rather be argued with, and I would rather be argued with in public and by name, and I would rather have somebody in this yard put a hand flat on that table and say *that is not true* while I am still standing in the room than be handed a fee and thanked at the end of it."

"Second. I am not a member of this body and I am not asking to be one and I am not going to vote on anything. I have not been inside one since I was thirty-one. If anybody wants me in a room I will come to the room."

"Third. I want a room. I do not want a hearing. There is a difference between those two things and the difference is the whole of what I am asking for, and I would like the clerk to write it down in those words and not in any others."

---

And the room, and the entry, and a small account of what an entry is not.

> Entered at the eleventh hour of the eleventh of this month, in the seed house, with the bay doors open, nine people in the room, the clerk present and the reader present and no form and no notice on the door.
>
> Asked for: **a room.** Not asked for: **a hearing.**
>
> **A room is not a hearing. A hearing is not a room. A room is not a record.**
>
> The asking was for a room. It is entered in those words, and the reader read it back including the third line, and the clerk entered the hour before he initialled the page.

And Lissa Vale gave her the small room at the back of the seed house, which has a bed in it and a stool and no stove in it, and the stove is in the corridor and belongs to the whole building, and she gave the standing reason out loud while she was doing it, and the reason is that this body does not put a stranger it is paying for a fortnight into a room with a person in the same building at night.

"Three things, in the order she says them," said Dunmar Fitch from the gate afterwards, with the book shut under his arm. "I have had four of those in one room in twenty-six years and the order is the same in all of them. They tell you what they are first, and then what they are not, and then what they want, and if you reverse it you have got them for an hour and a half instead of for a week. **She did not tell me what she is for. She told me what she is not, and that is the same thing and it took her nine words.**"
---

And then the fee, which is the friction of the whole day, and it is the same act as the third of last month, and the steward of this body stood up in a yard and said so out loud.

Lissa Vale does it the steward's way and the steward's way is that she refuses a figure and pays it anyway and everybody in the yard knows she refused it.

"You have not asked for one," she said.

"No," said Sera Quill.

"Then I am not going to offer you one either, because you have not asked, and I am not going to put a figure on a table in front of a woman who has told me in nine words that a paid witness is somebody's purchase. I will say the other half. This body has a sum that is six months short and four pounds five in a tin, and a fortnight of your keep is going into that sum, and if you find out in a week that I paid it you will find out from a page and not from me, and that is deliberate and I would like it entered."

"And here is the third thing and it is the last thing I am going to say about money. On the third of last month I said no to a licensed man's figure in this yard and paid it inside four minutes and read the payment back with the hour, and I did not say one word to anybody about having done it before. **A body that does a thing twice and says nothing about it is a body that has not changed and only has not noticed.** Enter that with the hour, and put my name against it, and let the man of about fifty read it back."

She paid it inside four minutes and she read the payment back with the hour, and the clerk entered it, and the reader read it back.

> Entered at the twelfth hour of the eleventh of this month, at the north end of the bench, by the steward, on her own standing and not at anybody's asking: a fortnight's keep, board and washing and firing and a room, at the figure this holding has used for two summers.
>
> **The figure was not offered. The figure was entered.**
>
> This is the second time in five weeks that the steward of this body has refused a figure and paid it, the first of the two being the third of last month, and the two are entered on one line of this body's own book, and the steward's reason is entered with them and the reason is given above and is not improved on.

---

And the sum, and it is Anneke Roos's standing, and she is the last word on money in this yard, and she was asked to put in a line she has never had a line like.

"Three shillings and eightpence," she said, standing at the bench with the ledger open and the pen in her hand and not writing yet. "That is my figure for one woman on her own feet for a fortnight. It is two summers old and I have not moved it, and it is not a wage and I am not offering anybody a wage, and I would like that entered first, because a line in a compost line that looks like a wage is a line somebody in four years is going to read as a wage and then either pay it or stop it, and I do not want either."

"So. Three shillings and eightpence for the fortnight. And eleven shillings for the licensed man, who will be here three days and who will give me his own figure and will not reduce it, and who I am not going to ask to reduce, because the last time I tried to reduce a licensed man's figure he came back four days later and it was in the compost line anyway and in a different hand."

"**Fourteen shillings and tenpence.** There are four pounds five in the tin, and that is about a tenth of what is in the tin. And the shortfall in that line has run six months and has not been discharged in six. And there is no line in that book for a woman reading this body's own record in a room at the back of a seed house, and I have entered the first half of the fortnight and I will enter the second half on the twenty-fifth, and the second half is three shillings and eightpence and a second blanket at twopence which I am putting in this week and not at the end of it."

And she initialled the line as an opinion in four words, which is her standing, and the four words have never been explained to anybody in eleven years and are not going to be.

And then she said the other thing, and it is the reason this day is hard, and she said it to the yard and not to the ledger.

"You will all get to the end of this and one of you is going to say that a woman walked in here for nothing. So let me have it said now while the clerk is standing here with his book open."

"On the fifteenth of last month this holding refused a free offer in writing, in one line, in front of a records officer of a district who came up a road to be refused properly. And it is now paying a stranger eighteen shillings and sixpence to come and tell it something."

"**Both halves of that are in the same book and I am the one who writes the money into it, and the fen does not get to be only one of them.**"

Nobody in that yard answered her, and the fen entered that nobody answered her, and entered the reason, and the reason is that there is no answer to that in a yard and anybody who has one is standing somewhere else.
---

And a confession was scheduled, in a room, and nothing had been admitted.

It was put to her at about the fourth hour of the twelfth and she named the day herself, and the fen entered that the day was named by the person who is going to speak in it and was not named by this body.

"The seventeenth," she said. "A Sunday, and I have said that on purpose. On a Sunday this yard is emptier than it is on a working day and there is one fewer person in that room who will want to be fair to me than there is on a Monday, and I want a working room and not a generous one."

"Read out what I write, in the room, in the order I wrote it, and read the figures back as figures. If a reader stumbles on a figure then somebody in that room has to stop me and make me say it again, and I will say it again, and I will say it three times if you want it three times."

"**What I will not do is be asked what I felt, and I will not be asked whether it was right.** Those are two questions a body asks because it wants an answer it can put in a column, and there is no column in this world for either of them and I have looked, and the looking took nineteen years and it was not short work."

It went in with the hour, and the man of about fifty read the whole of it back, and asked for his initial to be put against nothing, and the fen entered that the seventeenth was named by the woman who is going to speak in it and that this body did not choose the day and did not ask for the day.

And the fen entered, in the same place, that the compost line for the month now begun is not in that book and cannot be, because it falls on the first of next month, and that this holding does not know what the figure is and did not guess at it and will not enter a figure it does not know.

---

And then the twelfth, which is a small day, and it fills with two things and neither of them is the confession.

The second blanket went in at twopence, and Anneke Roos entered that line herself and would not let the clerk do it, and it is the smallest line in the compost line for four years and it is going to be there as long as the book is.

And in the afternoon, at about the fourth hour, a woman who had come four hundred miles to give a statement asked the clerk for one thing at the gate, and she asked it in the ordinary way, and the clerk answered it in the ordinary way, and the whole of it took under a minute.

"May I read your book," she said. "The one anybody may read. Not the fieldbook, and not the shut drawer, and not the seven sheets on the door. The one with six seasons of a holding in it."

"You may," said Dunmar Fitch. "It is on the shelf behind you and you may take it into the room at the back and read it as long as you want. I will not come and stand in the doorway. I will not ask you what you have found in it. And if anybody else in this yard asks you what you have found in it, you are not obliged to tell them, and I would like that entered because I mean it."

"Entered at the fourth hour of the twelfth of this month," said Dunmar Fitch, from the gate, writing. "A person who came into this yard on the eleventh to make a statement asked to read this body's own record before making the statement, and was given the whole of it inside a minute, and nobody in this yard thought to ask her why, and I would like that last part in my own hand, because I have been a clerk for twenty-six years and I have never entered a thing like it and I expect to enter it again."

The man of about fifty read that back, and asked for his initial to be put against nothing, and nobody thanked anybody, and it is not in the book that the woman was thanked, because she cannot be thanked for being readable.
