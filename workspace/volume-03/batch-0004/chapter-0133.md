# Chapter 133

## What A Hundred And Forty-One Trees Are Worth

Anneke Roos did a sum in the yard at about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the eighth of this month, a Sunday, with about twenty people in it, and it was the first sum she has done in eleven years that is not about this holding's own ground, and she said so before she did it and she said why, and the clerk entered both with the hour on them.

"I am going to do a sum about a hundred and forty-one trees that are not ours," she said, with the slate on the wall side of the gate and her own book open under it. "**Eleven acres, a hundred and forty-one trees in bearing, four harvests in four seasons, the last of them sold, and a fifth set aside last season for four houses. And I am going to do it in a yard in front of twenty people because that is what this yard is for, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that this is a figure about us.**

"And here is why I am doing it, and I have thought about it for two days and I am not going to pretend it is a better reason than it is. **On the second of this month an office in nine miles wrote us a line that says a record we do not classify is going to be classified for us. I cannot stop that. I cannot argue it. I cannot go and stand in a yard four hundred miles off and be asked about it. And the figure I want is not about that at all and I want it anyway, and I am going to say that out loud in front of you rather than have it look like a finding.**"

> Entered at the fourth hour of the afternoon of the eighth of this month, at the north gate, by the clerk: **a sum was done in a yard about a hundred and forty-one trees which are not this holding's.** The person who keeps the money said before she did it that it is not a figure about this holding, and gave the reason, which is that a line in a sheet in nine miles cannot be stopped or argued and the only place left where the figure is available is a yard in a fen four hundred miles off, **and that that is the wrong place and she is doing it anyway and said so first.**

---

And the sum, and it is in two halves, and she read both out, and she read the second one last and she read it in the same voice as the first.

"**The first half, and it is the half that can be done.** A hundred and forty-one trees, eleven acres, one harvest weighed at a barn door, two men and a scale and the count said out loud: **one hundred and nine hundredweight.** A fifth of it was set aside for the four houses before anything went out, which is about twenty-two hundredweight, and it is theirs and it went nowhere and it was not ours to weigh.

"And the price is **nine shillings the hundredweight.** And the same nine shillings four seasons running, and the man has never put it up in a bad year and has never put it down in a good one, and he is not a villain and he is not going to come back.

"So: **one hundred and nine hundredweight at nine shillings the hundredweight is forty-nine pounds and one shilling.** That is the figure. It is in this body's own book. It was read out of the seed-route ledger on the eighth of the seventh month in a barn on a shelf, to about twenty people, by the reader of this body, and it has been read out twice since and it is the largest figure this body has ever said out loud in a yard.

"**And there is my sum and here is why it is not one.**

"**I have not got the price in this book.** I have got the weight and I have got the product and I have not got the price, and the reason I have not got the price is that a price in a book that this body did not hear said by the person who owes it is a price this body has guessed at, and I do not guess at prices. **I asked for the price to be sent to me on paper in a month two months back and it was not sent, and the clerk entered that I asked, and it has not been sent and I am not going to ask again this month.** So I am about to do a multiplication in a yard in front of twenty people in which one of the two figures is a number this holding heard said on a shelf and not from a person, and the other one is a number this holding has never heard anybody say.

"That is the half that can be done and it gets worse, and **the worst of it is not the number.**

"**This holding has spent eleven years putting every figure it has into the open, and it has never once published a figure about what a person four hundred miles off is losing.** Not once. Eleven years of a door read out in a yard and a book anybody may read and a line of shortfalls on the first of every month, and there has never been a page in any of it about a woman of fifty-four and her four houses and her hundred and forty-one trees. **And the reason there has not is that it was not our money.** That is the whole reason. It was not our money, so it was not our arithmetic, and I do not blame anybody for that and I am not going to stand here and say we were wrong, because we were not wrong, we were somewhere else.

"And the reason I am doing it now is that an office has started a clock. **That is the only reason and I would like it written down as the only reason, because a sum that begins because somebody is frightened is not a sum anybody should trust, and I have just done it anyway and I would like both of those on the same page.**"

---

"**The second half.** The same trees. The same eleven acres. The same hundred and forty-one.

"At the price this body heard said, and not the price it holds: **not held.**"

She wrote the two words on the slate and did not say them for about four seconds, and then she said them.

"**And I am not going to add them.** I want that in the yard and I want it in the book in the same line. **The first half and the second half are not two numbers and you may not add them and you may not average them and nobody in nine miles is to be handed this pair as one figure.** The first half is a count of a hundred and forty-one trees at a price this body heard said. The second half is a count of the same hundred and forty-one trees at a price nobody has sent it. **The honest total is the first half and the two words, and the honest total is forty-nine pounds and one shilling and not held, and there is no sum on this slate that makes that into a figure.**

"And the second half is not a guess and I want that said too. It is not zero. **Zero is a thing I entered on the first of this month for a hundred and forty-one trees because a zero is a real figure and it is the only figure in this book that is about that shelf with any honesty in it. This is not zero. This is two numbers where there is one, and the one that is missing is a number and not a value, and I have written *not held* because that is what this yard writes when it has not got one.**"

> Entered at the fifth hour of the afternoon of the eighth of this month, at the north gate, by the clerk, read back by the reader of this body: **a sum in two halves.**
>
> **First half, and it can be done:** a hundred and forty-one trees, eleven acres, one harvest weighed at a barn door, one hundred and nine hundredweight, a fifth of it about twenty-two hundredweight set aside for four houses; at **nine shillings the hundredweight**, being the price this body heard said and not the price this body holds. **Forty-nine pounds and one shilling.**
>
> **Second half:** the same trees, the same acres, at a price nobody has sent. **Not held.**
>
> **The two halves were not added and were not averaged and the pair was not presented as one figure.** The reader read both back and asked for his initial to be put against nothing, and it went in, and he did not ask what the two words on the slate meant and the clerk entered that he did not ask.

---

And then somebody asked the obvious thing in a yard, which is how a yard works, and it was Jonas Byre and he asked it flatly and he was not being clever and he wanted an answer he could stand behind.

"Could we go and weigh it."

"No," said Anneke Roos.

"Right," he said. "**Then the number we have is a number we have been told, from four hundred miles, by a man reading a book, and the number we have not got is the one number that would let us say anything at all.** And I have been in this yard since the first of a month two months back and I have watched every figure in this holding get read out and argued about in front of people, and this is the first one in a season that a man could have checked by walking up a road and did not walk up a road."

"**I have got a reason and it is not a good one and I am going to give it in the yard rather than have it found out in a year,"** she said. "On the second of this month this body decided in front of twenty people not to write to that household about a ledger. **The reason was that a warning is a document. And the first thing a man does when you tell him you cannot check his arithmetic is go and check it, and if you cannot check it you have to say so, and if you say so in a yard at a windlass then you have told four hundred miles of people that the fen's one big figure off that shelf is a figure a man was told.** I am not going to do that to a woman of fifty-four who has a buyer behind her and a day letter on a stone. **So: we cannot check it, and the reason we cannot check it is a rule we made ourselves nine days ago about not sending documents up a road, and I am not going to pretend that rule is a principle. It is a rule. I made it. It has cost us a number.**"

And then the clerk entered an observation of his own, which is his standing, and nobody asked him for it and the man of about fifty read it back and did not ask him what it meant, because he has never once asked.

> Entered at the sixth hour of the afternoon of the eighth of this month, by the clerk, on his own motion, unasked: **a number we cannot check.** The clerk has not explained it, will not explain it, has not been asked for an explanation of it and has not offered one, and the reader did not ask, which is his standing.

---

And the question, and it is the steward's, and she gave it standing up with her hands behind her back and she recommended nothing.

"Who loses," said Lissa Vale.

"**Four houses. A woman of fifty-four and a man with a spade and three others and a well that is three and a half feet shorter than it was, and there is not one person in this fen.**" She said it flatly and she did not go on and she did not soften it, and after about three seconds she said the other half, which is hers and which is not a recommendation either.

"**And the man of about fifty is not one of the people who loses and I am going to say that in a yard because nobody else is going to.** He asked me on the fourth of this month, in the ordinary way at the bench, whether I thought the sheet about the buckets was going to be read by anybody at four hundred miles, and I told him what I thought. He did not send it. **I did not ask him to send it and I did not tell him not to and I have gone through those two days and there was no hour in which either of us said a word to the other about it, and the clerk is to enter that and I am not going to say one word more about it.**"

---

And then the sheet, and it went up the road on the ninth of this month, a Monday, at the change of horses, in an envelope with nothing on the outside but a place and a day, and the man who took it was engaged there and the whole of a hire is thirty-two shillings at four shillings a day for eight days of a road that is four days, **and four of them went into the ledger on the morning he starts and the remaining twenty-eight when he is paid off, and the line they fall in is the line of this month, which is not written until the first of next month, and this holding did not have thirty-two shillings and took them out of it anyway.**

> Sent up the plateau road on the ninth of this month at the change of horses. Written out in the steward's own hand and read back by the reader of this body in a yard with about eleven people in it.
>
> **One question. Are the rows going to be worked next season or not.**
>
> There is no advice on this sheet. There is no figure on it. There is nothing on it about what this body would prefer and nothing on it about what a hundred and forty-one trees are worth, and nothing on it about a buyer who is gone.
>
> **The question is about your ground. It is yours and we cannot answer it and we would rather have your answer than our arithmetic.**

And the man of about fifty read it back, and he did not like it, and he read it back anyway, and he said the thing that had to be said and it is the reason the sheet is in the book at all.

"That is not advice," he said. "**That is a question about a household's own ground, put by a body that has no standing over that ground and no claim on those trees.** And I want two things entered together. **This body did not tell a household what to do with a hundred and forty-one trees. This body asked them a question about their own ground. Those are different acts and only one of them is in this book, and the one that is in this book is the smaller one, and I would like somebody in four years to find this page and understand that the fen sent a question up a four-day road and not an opinion, and that the difference between those two things is the whole of what this holding has learned in a season.**"

> Entered at the seventh hour of the evening of the ninth of this month, by the clerk: **a sheet went up the plateau road with one question on it and no advice.** The reader of this body read it back and stated that a body which has no standing over a hundred and forty-one trees asked a question about them and did not tell their owners what to do, **and the fen entered that those are different acts and that only one of them is in this book and the fen entered which one.**
