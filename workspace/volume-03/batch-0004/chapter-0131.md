# Chapter 131

## Four Hands And Two Roads

The four copies went out on the fourth of this month, at about the seventh hour in the evening, and the man of about fifty counted them onto the bench one at a time and said the place out loud for each one before it went, and about fourteen people were in the seed house and the bay doors were open and the wind off the ice was coming under them.

"**One. The ordinary post to Cray.** It goes on the Tuesday, which is the ordinary day, and it is the same envelope it has always gone in and there is nothing on it but the four lines of a covering and the copy, and the covering does not ask for anything and does not say anything and names no person."

"**Two. The sub-office at Fennmere.** Down the fen road, nine miles, with a covering line that names the office at the head of the form on the third page and asks nothing, which is the line that went down on the eighteenth of a month three months back and came back in an envelope with a register entry in it. **That one will be read. It will be read twice, by a clerk who is not entitled to refuse it, and nothing will be taken out of it and nothing copied out of it, and he will enter that he read it and close the tray over it, and that is the best that has ever happened to a copy of anything this body has sent anywhere.**"

"**Three. A hired man on the plateau road.** Four days up and four days down, at four shillings a day, which is **thirty-two shillings for the whole hire**, and four of them go into the ledger tonight, on the day he is hired, and the remaining twenty-eight when he is paid off. **And the cost is in the ledger before he goes out of the yard**, because a body that pays for a road after the man has come off it is a body that finds out what it cost in a yard with fourteen people in it."

"**Four. Inside a licensed man's coat.**"

Coll Renner was at the end of the bench with his own book shut under his arm and he did not open it and he said one sentence, and the clerk entered it.

"**That one is mine and it is the one that cannot be relied on and I said so in an office nine miles off a season ago and I am not going to say it twice in a room.** I have said it and it is in a register in a small square hand and it is in this body too, and it does not need me to say it again to be true."

And the clerk entered that a licensed man said it once and would not say it again.

---

And the sequence went out in the same four hands, at about the same hour, on the same bench, and it is two pages and it names nothing and no one and it carries no part and it has no *you* in it, and it is the same two pages that went on the tenth of a month three months back.

"Not classified," said Lissa Vale, before anybody could ask, and she said it standing at the bench with her hands behind her back, which is her standing. "**It is not on the return of the fourteenth and it is not going to be, and the clerk is not to put it in the satchel with the rest, and I will not have a form go up that road with a thing in it that has no part.**"

And Dunmar Fitch put it in separately, in a second bundle, and tied the second bundle with a second piece of string, and entered that there were two bundles and what was in each, and **the fen entered that this is the second time in three years this body has put two bundles on a bench and that it has never once regretted the second and has twice been glad of it and that glad is not a reason and is entered as a fact and not as a justification.**

---

And then the count, and it is the count the man of about fifty insisted on having said out loud with the names on it, and he said it standing, with the ledger shut, and about fourteen people heard it and it is in the book.

"**Four places, and I am going to say them slowly and I am going to name them, because a number nobody can enumerate is not a number and this body has been found out once already this season by a figure that was not a figure.**"

"**One: Cray.** A counting house, nine hundred miles, and a hand goes into it and nobody at this bench has ever been told whose."

"**Two: the sub-office at Fennmere.** A counter, nine miles, and a clerk with a tray, and the tray is in a building and a building can be got at."

"**Three: a hired man.** A man on the plateau road with a satchel, for eight days, who is going to be paid off when he comes back and who is not a member of anything."

"**Four: inside a licensed man's coat.**"

He put his hand on the bench.

"**That is the whole of it. This body is not nine places and I am not going to let it be nine places in a book because nine sounds like what we are. Four. And I would like the clerk to enter that the four were counted out loud on the fourth of this month in a room with fourteen people in it, and that every one of the four is a place a thing can be lost, and that three of the four are buildings and a man, and that the fourth is a man.**"

> Entered at the seventh hour of the evening of the fourth of this month, in the seed house, by the clerk, on the standing of a count: **four places.** Cray; the sub-office at Fennmere; a hired man on the plateau road; inside a licensed man's coat. **Counted out loud with the names on them, with the reason given first, which is that a number nobody can enumerate is not a number.** The fen is not nine places. Three of the four are a building, a counter and a man. The fourth is a man.

---

And Tova Reed said the rule, and she said it in a yard and not in the seed house, and she said it at about the eighth hour with her own case shut and her hands on the lid of it, and the clerk entered it whole because she told him to enter it whole.

"**A readable thing survives in more places than one.** That is the rule and it is not mine and it is older than both of us and I have used it for twenty years on seed and I have never once had to explain it. One place is a house. Two places is a habit. **Three places is a body. Four places is a thing that can be got at by an office and cannot be got at twice by the same office in the same week**, and that is the number we have and we have got it in four days and I did not have to argue anybody into it because the argument had already been made for us by an office in nine miles in seven days on their own letterhead."

"And I am not going to soften the other half. **The rule is about readable things. It says nothing about a thing that is not readable, and it says nothing at all about a woman on a shelf who keeps a ledger in her own hand, and it does not apply to a piece of brass with a cut in it, and if anybody in this yard thinks it does I would like to hear it now and not in a year.**"

Nobody heard it now.

---

And the thing that did not go anywhere was the description of a piece of brass with a cut in it, and it is in the book because somebody in the seed house asked at about the seventh hour why the other bundle had a second string on it, and the answer took four minutes and it is Dunmar Fitch's.

"It goes in neither bundle," he said, "and it goes nowhere, and I have been asked to say why in a room and I am going to."

"**Two places.** A drawer in the well house, which is the one room in this holding a stranger may walk into at any hour and which the fen entered four days into a month as a hiding place with a lid, and gave that because the other description of it is worse. **And a person, named once in a room on the twenty-eighth of last month and not entered, who is not of this fen and has not been asked and will not be told anything until somebody asks her.**"

"And the count of places for that one is **two**, and that is the whole of it, and it is not in the four and the difference between four and two is the count this fen entered on the twenty-seventh of last month. **Four and two. And the two is a count of places and not of people, and a place is not a person, and a drawer is not a person, and one of the two is a woman whose name is not in this book and is not going to be.**"

"And I will say the other thing and it is mine and I would like it in my own hand," said Dunmar Fitch. "**I tried to think of a third place tonight and I could not, and I sat with it for about a minute and a half and I did not find one, and a man in this yard who has been in this trade twenty-two years says that is not because there is not one, and I am not going to argue with him in a seed house with the door open.** The count of places for that description is two and two is the whole of it and I have written *two* on the page and I have not written *two, so far*."

> Entered at the seventh hour of the evening of the fourth of this month, in the seed house, by the clerk: **the count of places for the description of a collar section is two.** A drawer in the well house, and a person whose name is not entered. **The clerk was asked in a room for a third and entered that he could not find one, and entered that the word *so far* is not on the page.** The difference between the two counts and the four is a count of places and not of people. A drawer is not a person and neither is a counting house.

---

And the man of about fifty stood up, on the fourth, with the ledger shut, and made the argument against it, and it is the best thing he says in this volume and he is not thanked for it and was not thanked for it and did not want to be.

"I am going to say the half," he said, "and I am going to say it before anybody else can, and I am going to say it in the ordinary words, and I am the reader of this body and I have read the door out in a yard nine times this season."

"**We are not protecting a record any more. We are spreading a rumour of one in four places.** A rumour that is true and a rumour that is not true cannot be told apart from outside a yard, and a body that has made itself into four places is a body that has four things to be wrong about and four things that have to be right, and we cannot read four of them because we cannot read one of them. The counting house at Cray is nine hundred miles off. The tray at Fennmere is nine miles off. **The one we can read is a man's coat and the man is walking toward a plateau in the dark and we cannot read a coat.**"

"So I am going to say the other half and I am not going to soften it. **It is still the right thing to do and I know exactly why and I am not going to pretend I do not know why.** It is right because there is an office in nine miles that can take a class of record away by listing it, and there is no answer to that which any of us in this yard can write. **And I would like it entered that the reason this body is doing this is not that it believes in the truth. It is that it is afraid, and it has worked out that being in four places is the only shape of safety available to it, and that is a reason and it is not a principle.**"

"And I would like the fourth thing entered as well, because this body keeps everything and the fourth thing is the one nobody is going to want. **Every one of those four places was reached because somebody on this bench was told a thing they could not check and decided that the safest thing to do with a fact you cannot check is to be in more rooms than the man who is asking you for it.** That is not a rule for a body. That is what a frightened set of people do in the third week of a season with no charter and four pounds five in a tin."

> Entered at the eighth hour of the evening of the fourth of this month, in the yard, by the clerk, on the standing of an argument and entered whole and at the request of the person who made it:
>
> **This body is spreading a rumour of a record into four places and the reason is fear and not a belief in the truth, and being in four places is the only shape of safety available to it, and that is a reason and not a principle.** The person who said it is the reader of this body and asked that the word *afraid* stand in the book in his own hand and not be improved on by anybody, and nobody improved on it.

And then the clerk's count, and the arithmetic, which is the ordinary business of the ledger and the last hour of the fourth.

> Entered at the ninth hour of the evening of the fourth of this month, in the seed house, by the clerk, read back by the man of about fifty, with the hour on it, on the standing of the money and of a count of places:
>
> **Hands: four.** Two bundles, four copies each, one bundle to Cray, one bundle to Fennmere, and two copies carried by hand and by coat.
>
> **Roads: two.** The fen road, nine miles, and the plateau road, four days. Everything that goes to Cray goes on the fen road first.
>
> **Days out: one to Fennmere, six to Cray on the ordinary post, four to the shelf, by a man hired tonight at the change of horses and going out in the morning.**
>
> **Days on the road, of the man: eight.**
>
> **Shillings: four on the fourth, the day he was hired, and twenty-eight when he is paid off. The whole of the hire is thirty-two.** This holding did not have thirty-two shillings and took them out of the line of this month anyway, and the line of this month is not written until the first of next month.
>
> **People who now hold one of the four: one.** The man. A counting house, a counter and a coat are not people and the fen has entered that it has entered three buildings and a man and has called it four places, and that the difference is the whole of what is on this page.
>
> **People who could not be found with it, if it came to that: one.** The man in the coat. He is licensed, he is twenty-two years in the trade, he has been walking this fen road for a season, and **he may be run over in a month, and then this body holds three copies of this reading and two of them are in its own name and can be listed, and the third is on a plateau in a case in a stone house and nobody in the world will be able to say whose hand it went into.**

And the man of about fifty read all of that back out of the book into a room with about nine people still in it, word for word, and he read the last line twice, and he asked for his initial to be put against nothing, which is his standing, and **the clerk entered that he has now asked for it on eleven separate occasions this season and that the fen has entered the count and that the count is not a complaint and is not going to be made one**, and nobody thanked anybody and the yard went quiet and the wind off the ice came under the bay doors and went out again.
