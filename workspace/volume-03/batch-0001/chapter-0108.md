# Chapter 108

## The Fieldbook

On the sixteenth, at about the tenth hour, four hundred miles off, in a yard with nobody in it, Ilsa Kern and Jonas Byre read the well house door out, because the rule is the first and the middle and the last whether anybody comes or not.

> Well house door, read at the tenth hour of the sixteenth, in an empty yard, by two members of this body. The schedule, all seven columns, and the declared figure and then the measured figure, and the count of the readings. The notice, four lines. The four sheets, four parts. The refusal clause, from the north gate. The six clauses of the second of this month, the fifth read twice.
>
> **The count of the readings of the seventh column went to twenty-two.**
>
> Nobody was in the yard. It was said out loud before the counting and not after. **Nobody at Cauldron Reach knew it had been read and nobody at Lantern Reach knew a man there was reading nothing, and the two ends of this body could not have checked each other, and that is entered as a fact about a day and not as a fault.**

And on the fourteenth, at about the seventh hour, four hundred miles off, the fourteenth logged itself, and nobody on a plateau hears of it for a week.

> Pressure, at the seventh hour: the fourteenth. Nine in the quarter hour with one run of three, at the rail. Cause not established. Licensed person present, none. Nothing written underneath it.

Gael Senn wrote it in her own book and entered the decision underneath it, which is her standing, and the decision is four words long and the fen has not asked to see it. On the seventeenth she read it out in a yard, because somebody who had gone nine days up a road asked.

---

The fieldbook came out of the press on the seventeenth, at about the second hour of the afternoon, in a stone barn at Cauldron Reach, on a trestle that Coll Renner hired and Lissa Vale paid for and put in the ledger against the road. It is a fragment: a front board, a living root bead, a handful of pages. The back board and most of the survey pages are gone and nobody in this holding has ever known where, and the last time it was opened it was put on a chair in a seed house in the month before the flood. That is two volumes and a hundred and seventy days ago.

Lissa Vale opened it.

Nobody asked her, and that is why it is hers.

> The fieldbook was opened at about the second hour of the seventeenth at Cauldron Reach by the person in whose custody it is, in the presence of a licensed reader, two witnesses, a steward, an engineer of record and about nine other people. **Nobody asked her to open it. The engineer did not ask her, the steward did not ask her, the licensed man did not ask her, and the fen entered the sentence on the seventh with her name on it and not his, and it has been carried four hundred miles and obeyed.**

And then the cost of it, entered on the seventeenth and never explained.

**There was no line in the use log.** It is thirteen lines and records contacts and never readings, and the fieldbook was opened with a licensed reader, a witness, a date, an hour, an instrument and a purpose, and **there is no use-log line for the fieldbook and there never has been and there is not going to be, and nobody in that barn asked for one and nobody said why, and the whole room knew it and not one of them said a word.**

Coll Renner fitted his own clip, did not touch the bead, and said so out loud first, because it is the standing of the instrument and because he said it in a yard in the spring and the fen has entered both occasions.

Then he read, and Lissa Vale wrote it in the seed-route ledger, the reader for the entry being Tova Reed, and the whole of what is on the surviving pages is this, and there is nothing else and never has been.

> **Page the first.** A column head, and a figure under it: *confirmed — set — 4.* And above it, in the same ink, Aldren's last line of figures, on the last page of a fieldbook in nine other books in this county, and the only line in this fragment anybody here can recite: *Seventeen. Set in the low. Eleven. Watch the fourth beat in the small hours.*
>
> **Page the second.** An impossible year, in the same hand, and beside it, in a later hand: *probably a copying error; to be treated as one, and no inference to be drawn from it.* The second hand is Lissa Vale's, and she has never told anybody she wrote it.
>
> **Page the third, which is the last page, and which is a form.**
>
> It is a work order, on district paper of a shape not used here for a long time, a sheet of three, and the head of it carries the name of an office this holding has seen on one other document in two volumes. The name is not explained anywhere in this book, and it is a name at the head of a column.
>
> It is a direction to a named node, on a named day, in a named week, of a year that is on the page and cannot be made to agree with the impossible year two pages back, and the year is eight years ago and it is the year the shaft was. At the foot, under a printed head for a signature, there is a hand, and the hand makes the letter *W* the way it made the *W* in *weir* and the way it made the *W* in *wire*, and a woman in this district who has not seen that hand in thirty years said the same thing of a different hand in a column of a register of authorisations in the spring, and has not been asked about it and is not going to be.

And the direction is one line, read out in a barn at about the third hour. Here it is.

> *Third week, Thursday. Take the branch at the upper collar, the whole of it, and dress. Flow at the head is not what the board says and the board is not mine. **Necessary.** Do not enter this sheet against the node. Enter it against me.*

And the man of about seventy read it back from about eleven feet with his hand up, because that is his standing, and he read it correctly, word for word, including the last line, and asked for his initial to be put against nothing.

And nobody in that barn spoke for about a minute, and then Marek Vale said the only sentence anybody says in this chapter, and Lissa Vale entered it and read it back and softened nothing.

"That is my father," he said. "And he wrote *necessary*, and he gave no reason, and he told a clerk not to put it against the node. **I cannot find out whether he was right and I am not going to be able to, and I have been trying for eight years to find out and I have got a piece of paper with a word on it.**"

And then Tova Reed said the last of this chapter's argument, and it is the standing of this volume, and it is not tender and it is not a consolation.

"Then you have the same as anybody else in this barn," she said, "and no worse, and no better, and I want it said in front of the nine of them. **The record does not say he was right. The record does not say he was wrong. It says a man was in a hurry and a word on a form, and the word is the only reason the form is worth anything, and it is not a reason.** A man who writes *necessary* and hides the sheet from the node has done the correct thing and told nobody anything, and there is no reading of this page on which anybody in this room learns anything. We have a piece of paper and not a father, and a body that cannot tell those two things apart will be told what it wants by the first man who comes up a road with a bag."

And the man of about seventy said the sentence that the reader of this body carried four hundred miles and read out in a yard on the twentieth with nine people standing in it, and it is not the closing sentence under section nine and has never been confused with it.

"Nineteen hundred and forty years old," he said, "and a graft on every one of them put in by somebody who is dead, and a hundred and forty-one trees on that shelf, and a woman of fifty-four with a buyer's day in nine days, and a well three and a half feet down on her bucket, and a burnt man in a barn who cannot taste a lemon. **Eleven cuts and a word and a shelf.** I would like somebody in a room to write those three on the same line, because that is the whole of what this volume has found and it fits on a line."

---

The copying was done badly and fast, and it took the rest of the seventeenth and all of the night, and it is why this batch exists.

Four copies of the reading, in Lissa Vale's hand, because there is one hand in this volume that can write a form out fair and the other three cannot, and because a form in three hands is a form nobody can swear to.

One to Cray, on the ordinary post, in an envelope with no number on it.

One down the fen road to Perrin Osse at the sub-office, with a covering line naming the office at the head of the form on the third page, which asks him nothing and tells him nothing.

**One in Merrid Ollvar's own hand. She can write, and she has never signed a piece of paper in her life, and she signed this one at the foot with her name and the date and nothing else, and the fen entered both facts on the same line of the same book on purpose.**

And one that is not this fen's at all, which went down the plateau road on the eighteenth inside Coll Renner's coat, and it is a copy of a reading and not of a book, and the reason it is his is in the ledger on the eighteenth in Tova Reed's hand.

> The fourth copy is the licensed man's and not ours, and it goes inside his coat, and it is a licensed man carrying a copy of somebody's reading up a road in his own name with his own license on it, and no office can call that a record of a holder.
>
> **This is the one that is safe. The other three are ours and ours can be declared part three. That one is his, and the man of about fifty voted against giving anything away in this volume and I would like his vote read at that line.**

And the seventh column was copied as a line. Not the count. **The line** — head, rows, and the ruled blank underneath, because a form anybody may copy is a form anybody may copy including the blank, and that is the whole of what this fen has learned in two volumes, and it is now in four counties.

---

The fieldbook was closed at about the fourth hour of the eighteenth, put into its own box, and carried to the first house on that shelf, to a room with a lock in it that Merrid Ollvar has and has never used. It is not going back down the road, and nobody argued about that, because a record is not safe in one place and this holding does not get it back because it is theirs.

And Lissa Vale entered the decision with her reason, and that entry is the twenty-sixth note of this volume and it is an argument and it carries the standing sentence.

> The fieldbook is closed and may not be opened again in this season. Not by me, not by a licensed man, not by an officer of any district, and not by a person who comes up this road and says they are from an office.
>
> The reason is that a book open twice in one month is a book somebody can say was altered between the openings, and I have not got a copy that would answer that. I have a lock and a room at the first house, and a licensed man carrying the reading up a road in his own name.
>
> And the second reason, entered because it is the true one. It is my husband's book and I had it in a press under a ledger for nine years and did not open it once in nine years because I was afraid of it. I have opened it twice in three days. That is not a record-keeping decision and I have written it in the book because I am the only person who will write it down.

And on the twentieth, in a barn at Cauldron Reach, at about the fourth hour, with the wind on the door, Marek Vale stood up in front of nine people and let Coll Renner read him, and the reading is in the licensed man's book and in the ledger, and nothing was softened.

> Arm, left, inside and outside, to above the elbow. Not the mark: the mark is where it was, four inches, branching twice. Fever on the day, none on the fourteenth, the eighteenth or the twentieth.
>
> Sensation: three fingers of the left hand, restored on the second morning, tested on the fourteenth, the eighteenth and the twentieth, and on the twentieth he could not tell a sour thing from a sweet one and said so himself. I wrote that at his word and not at mine. I have been licensed twenty-two years and have not before put a patient's words in a book of mine, and did now because he asked.
>
> The hour at about the fifth on the thirteenth is not accounted for and he does not intend to account for it.
>
> **Recommendation, in my own hand, and it is my first in twenty-two years: fit for ordinary work. Not fit for a draw, and I would not answer for a draw, and not in a room with a third person in it either, and I would not say so in a letter, because a letter is a thing a person can keep.**

And the well was read at the fourth hour on the sixteenth of this month, by the occupier, by her own rule and her own nail, and it was **five feet and five inches**, and on the twentieth it was **five feet and four inches**, and both readings are in the seed-route ledger in the ordinary words and **there is no entry in this volume for that well coming back, and the fen has decided it will not be predicting one.**

And on the twentieth the number cut into the two boards over the bay at Lantern Reach was one hundred and four, and Ilsa Kern cut it, and said it out loud on her way past to an empty yard and told nobody, and the telling was not hers to do, and she did it anyway.

---

And the fruit is in a stone room on that shelf in crates, and a buyer is on the eleventh, and nobody on that shelf has been told which of two things came up that road. One is a row of eleven cuts in a week dressed by a man who did not come back. The other is a piece of paper with a word on it. And the plain reason on the door of the third house is that a thing nobody can enforce is the only kind of thing that can be refused, and a record nobody else holds is the only kind of record that can be read.

Underneath the day's work, dated the twentieth, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the twenty-sixth note under section nine of the charter schedule, and there is no charter, and the note says so on its face.

*A door read out in an empty yard on the sixteenth, the count of the readings of the seventh column at twenty-two, and the two ends of this body unable to check each other's count. A fieldbook carried out of a building, told to a sub-office in person, opened on the seventeenth by the person whose it is with nobody asked, and **no line in a use log, because there has never been one and nobody said why and the whole room knew it.** A page of columns; a page of an impossible year with a sentence in a wife's hand telling the reader to draw no inference; and a form, an office at the head that is not explained, a direction to a named node in the year of the shaft, a hand that makes the letter the way it made it in *weir*, the word *necessary*, and an instruction to a clerk not to enter the sheet against the node. Four copies, one not ours, the reason given, the seventh column copied as a line including the blank. A man read aloud on the twentieth and not softened, and a recommendation in a licensed man's hand. A well at five feet and four inches and no entry in this volume for it coming back. A hundred and four days cut into two boards in an empty yard. And eleven cuts, a word, and a shelf, written on one line by a man of about seventy.*

*An argument. This is worth nothing against a cut.*
