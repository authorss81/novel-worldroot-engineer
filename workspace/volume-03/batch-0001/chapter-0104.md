# Chapter 104

## What A Cut Is

The wind came off the ice on the tenth at about the second hour and they read the face in the lee of the north wall with the barn door open and Renner holding a lamp against the sky at an angle that made the healed wood throw a line, and it took the whole of the day and most of the evening, and the face they read is a cut face on a pear limb about the thickness of a wrist, dressed, and healed over, and by anybody's count it was cut this season, and nobody in that barn counted it any other way.

Marek Vale cleaned it with a knife and did not cut into it further than he had to, and he cleaned nine of them over two days, because a face is worth nothing on its own and a face is worth a great deal next to eight others done by the same hand in the same week.

And then he counted, out loud, with the yard listening, and it is the sentence that turns everything after it, and it is a working sentence and not a speech, and he said it about the fourth hour on the tenth with nine people and a lamp in a barn doorway.

"That is not damage. That is a method."

Say what a wound does, first, because that is the ordinary thing and it is what everybody in that barn was expecting. A wound on a tree is ragged, and it dries, and it holds a pocket of dead wood at the bottom of it where the water sat, and it heals from the sides inward over a slow number of years, and the healing wood goes round the pocket in a roll and the roll is wider on the inside of the bend than the outside, and any man who has dressed a graft in his life can read that in a minute. **This wound is not that.** The face is flat across the whole of the cut. The bark grew back over the cut edge and met the bark below it, clean, in one season, with a line you could put a knife in. The wood behind the face is the wood of the limb and it is continuous with it, and the limb is carrying, and carrying well, and the growth above the cut on the ninth is four tenths of an inch in a season, and the growth below the cut on the ninth is five tenths, which is *more*, because a dressed wound is a wound the tree has stopped arguing with.

"And so the order is this," he said, and he went along the limb with a finger and did not touch it. "**Taken. Dressed. Closed. Left alone long enough to close. Taken again.** And that is the same order eleven times on this row and it is the order on the next row and I have looked at the next row from the gate and there are four more of them and they are older and I have not read those and I am not going to say anything about those until somebody with a rule has stood at them.

"That is a method. **Nobody cuts eleven branches on one row in one week by accident, and nobody dresses a wound eleven times by accident, and the fact that it is dressed is the whole of the finding.** An undressed cut on a pear limb this old would have torn out in the first wind after and taken the limb with it. He dressed them. He dressed them properly. He came back to them. And he did it eleven times in a week, and the week is this season, and the season is not ours."

And then the tool, which is the second half of the chapter and which is a small thing that will not let go of him for the rest of the volume.

The face is smooth. Not rough-cut and not sawed; smooth, on the flat of it, and on one edge only there is a bevel, a single slope of about an inch and a half across, and the bevel is on the same side on all nine.

"That is a chisel," said Marek Vale, "or a draw-knife, or a blade with a shoulder on it, and I do not know which and I am not going to guess. And I know which side the bevel is on, and I know it is the same side on all nine, and that is not a man with an axe and it is not a man with a saw, and I have looked at a good many wound faces in my life and I have never seen that bevel on a pear."

Coll Renner said that he had, once, and then stopped, and then said that it was not his and that he would rather not say whose it was in a barn in front of four houses, and Lissa Vale entered that and did not ask him again, which is her standing.

And the man from the second house went down the road four hundred yards at about the fifth hour and came back with the district's own hire list, which is a printed sheet nailed inside a hut at the change of horses and is the only list of tools anybody in that country answers to, and he put it on the bench and Marek Vale went down it with a finger at about the sixth hour and stopped at the bottom and put his hand flat on it.

"There is no chisel on this list," he said. "There is no draw-knife. There are two axes, a bill, a saw, a spade, a bar, and a thing called a bark knife that I have not heard of and would not use. **And this list was printed in a town nine days down the road and it does not carry the tool, and the district's own hire sheet at the foot of this plateau is the same sheet and I have seen it and it is the same print.**"

"So somebody brought it," said the man at the second house.

"Or somebody had it and nobody has ever needed to hire it," said Marek Vale, "and those are the same sentence with the middle taken out, and I am not going to say the middle out loud in a barn."

---

And then the argument, and it is the real middle of the road, and it is a real disagreement between two people who are not being polite, and neither of them softened anything, and the clerk could not enter it as a finding because it was two findings and the fen's own book does not hold two findings under one line.

Marek's is that these are the actions of a man in a hurry with a season to save, and he gave the reasons and they are the reasons an engineer gives: eleven, at intervals, on one row, all of them dressed within the same week, which means the man was not deciding tree by tree, he was working to a plan, and a man working to a plan in a hurry is a man who is working against a clock and a clock is a thing that happens in a year.

Tova's is that a man working against a clock cuts what is in the way. He does not dress a wound. **He dresses a wound when he intends that tree to still be standing in nine years, and he dresses it twice, and he comes back on the sixth day to check the first one, and a man in a hurry does not come back on the sixth day because a man in a hurry has other things on the sixth day.** She said that a list and a hurry are two different things and that the whole of the argument is which of the two it is, and that she knows which one she would rather it was and that wanting it is not a reason, which is the man of about fifty's sentence from a yard four hundred miles off and which she gave back to him without knowing she was doing it.

And then Anneke Roos asked the question that reframed the whole of it, and she asked it in a barn on a plateau at the seventh hour with a lamp going, and she is not here and this is how her question gets here: it is read out of the ledger by Lissa Vale because Lissa Vale read the ledger aloud on the road every evening for nine days as the fen's practice when the fen is not in it, and everybody at Cauldron Reach has heard the whole of a fen's arithmetic for nine days and is bored of it.

"Which of the two would you rather it was."

Marek Vale did not answer for a while and the wind came and went and the lamp did not move.

"I would rather it was a man in a hurry," he said. "**And I would rather it on purpose, which is the part I want written down, and I have been carrying it since the eighth and I did not know I was carrying it until you asked me in a barn.**

"The hurry is a man who saw a thing coming and had a fortnight to do something about it and took down eleven branches in a week and dressed every one of them and expected to be back, and who is now not here and there is nothing on that row that says he is coming back, and that is a thing I can live with. I have a father who is not here either and I have lived with that for eight years.

"And the other one is a man who wrote down a list. Who took a branch off a tree for a reason he had written down before he started. And then the reason is somewhere and it is not on the tree, and it will not be on the tree, and if I cannot find a reason then I have a man who cut nine live branches on purpose and can tell me nothing, and I will not know whether he was right, and I will not be able to stand in a yard and say to any of these four houses that he was right or that he was wrong.

"So I would rather the hurry. **And I know that that is a man preferring the answer that lets him off, and I have put it down flat, and I would like somebody in this yard to come at me with it tomorrow morning and not let me have got away with saying it out loud in a barn.**"

Merrid Ollvar came at him with it the next morning, and what she said is at the top of the twelfth, and it is the reason the rest of this is about a record and not about a tree.

---

And the money, in the same day, done in the open at about the second hour by a woman who is not of the fen and has no reason to be careful with any of them.

She brought out a paper with four lines on it and a figure at the foot of it and a name at the head, and she put it on the bench beside a rule and a lens, and she said the thing that no person at Lantern Reach had understood about her for eleven weeks.

"I have not shown this to anybody in eleven weeks," she said, "and I am showing it to you, and I am not going to hold anything back, and I am going to read the figure out loud because I have read it to myself every morning and I have never once said it out loud to another person.

"And here is what it is. A price the hundredweight, the same four seasons, and a day on it, and a clause on it that says the buyer takes the fruit as it stands at picking and does not warrant it against frost, hail, or any fault of the ground. **He has not warranted the ground.** He has warranted himself against the weather. And the day on the paper is the seventh of next month, and this barn is the tenth of this month, and it is in twenty-seven days, and there are a hundred and forty-one trees on this shelf, and the trees that fruited in a month three months back are fruiting again, and I do not know whether that is a fortune or a debt and I am not going to find out in twenty-seven days with a horse on the road."

And then she did the sum herself, out loud, at the second hour, with a scrap of paper, and she did it well.

"It is more than this holding has taken in since the charter was made," she said, "and I know that now because a woman told me so in a yard and I have not forgotten it, and I am not saying it to be fair. I am saying it because I want one thing said out loud in this barn by somebody who is not of my household and who has not been on this shelf thirty-one years.

"**This fruit is the only money these four houses will see this year, and the man who cut those branches is dead and does not have a claim on it, and if I sell it I am not robbing anybody, and if I do not sell it my neighbor's boy does not get a cart.** I have thought about that sentence every day for eleven weeks and I could not get it out of my head and that is why I walked nine days down a road with a crate of pears."

And Marek Vale said the thing that is the restraint the whole of this turns on, and he said it standing up, and it is short, and it is the first time this season he has said a thing in a yard and then not argued it.

"I am not going to tell you what to do with the pears," he said.

And Tova Reed said, from the barn door, in about nine words, and it is the standing of this body from that day on.

"That is the first useful thing he has said since we left the fen," she said, "and I am entering it with the hour on it, and I am entering also that it cost him something, and that I watched it cost him, and that the reason it cost him is that he has got a row of cuts in a barn and a woman with a figure and a man with a flat face, and the two things are in the same basket and one of them is his and the other one is hers, and he has spent a season being a man who makes the call in a yard, and he has just not made it."

Lissa Vale entered both halves and the hour and read them back, and Anneke Roos's observation went in from four hundred miles away in four words, and the four words have never been explained to anybody.

Underneath the day's work, dated the eleventh, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the twenty-fourth note under section nine of the charter schedule, and there is no charter, and the note says so on its face.

*A cut face read on a plateau, nine of them, cleaned with a knife and not cut further than was needed, in the lee of a north wall with a lamp held at an angle by a licensed man. Taken, dressed, closed, left alone long enough to close, taken again, eleven times, on one row, in one week, in this season. The healing wood continuous with the limb and the limb carrying, and the growth below the cut greater than the growth above it, and a dressed wound read against a ragged one and not confused. A smooth face with a bevel on one side only, the same side on all nine, and no chisel and no draw-knife on the district's hire sheet at the change of horses or on the district's own sheet at the foot of the plateau, and a licensed man who has seen that bevel once and would rather not say whose it was in a barn, and was not asked again. Eleven cuts read as a method and not as damage, and a man in a hurry against a man with a list, and no finding entered because the fen's book holds one finding under a line. And a figure read out loud in a barn by a woman who has read it to herself every morning for eleven weeks, and a buyer on the seventh of next month, and a clause that warrants the weather and not the ground, and an engineer of record standing in a barn at about the second hour who did not tell anybody what to do with a crate of fruit.*

*An argument. This is worth nothing against a cut.*
