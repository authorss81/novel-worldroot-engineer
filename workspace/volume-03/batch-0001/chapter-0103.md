# Chapter 103

## Eleven Acres On A Shelf

The going is one paragraph in the seed-route ledger in Lissa Vale's own hand, and it is the only paragraph in the whole of this volume that is about distance, and it is here because the fen's practice is that a thing somebody has to come back from is entered by the person who came back.

> Up. Nine days. One change of horses at a place with a name that is not entered, and Merrid Ollvar has it in her own ledger. Board at both ends taken by her and refused by us. Two of the nine days on ground that is not ours and one of them with a wind off it that stops work after the second hour. Down. Four days. The bench hired at the far end. Eleven shillings a day of horse and man. A case, a clip, a trestle, and a pot of a pear tree wrapped in sacking with its own soil on it. No sums are given here because the sums are on a page of the fen's own paper at Lantern Reach and are in the compost line and the two are not to be added together by anybody.

They came onto the shelf on the eighth, at about the third hour of the afternoon, and the first thing was not the orchard.

The first thing was that you could see the ground. In the fen everything a person looks at goes away thirty feet, because there is water standing in it and reed and willow and a hedge in between, and a man can stand in a yard his whole life and not know which way the water goes. On the shelf there was nothing between a person and the next thing but air, and the air was thin and cold and came off the ice from the second hour, and the whole of the middle distance was laid out like the inside of a bowl with the low side away to the south and the shelf on the north side of it.

The second thing was the wood. Rootwood stands out of the ground here and it does not stand the way a tree stands, and there is no tree on this shelf and there has not been one in living memory. It comes up out of the thin soil in long pale seams, some of them higher than a man, and it goes across the shelf in a line and it goes down off the edge of it and there is a place two miles along that line where it stops dead in a row, all of it, at the same height, with clean ends.

Nobody in that party said anything about it for about half a mile, and then Tova Reed said the sentence that is the reason the rest of the volume happens.

"That has been cut," she said.

"It has been cut," said Marek Vale. "And it is not one man with an axe, and I am not going to say what it is until we are on the row, and I want to say it on the row with a rule in my hand, and if I say it here I will have said it twice and one of the times will be a guess."

---

Cauldron Reach is four houses and eleven acres on a south-facing shelf, and there is a stone wall round the orchard that was built by somebody no record in thirty-one years of Merrid Ollvar's own bad ledger mentions, and the wall is three courses higher on the north side than on the south, which is the wrong way round for a wall and the right way round for a wall, and Marek Vale stood in front of it for a while and said nothing about it and Tova Reed said one word about it and then said nothing else about it either.

Against the north wall of the yard there is a stack.

It is pressed rootwood in bales, rotted to a grey pulp, stacked higher than a man and longer than the barn and going round two sides of the yard, and it has been stacked against that wall for longer than anybody at Cauldron Reach can date. Merrid Ollvar has never opened it. There is no reason to open it. Her father's name is on the wall of the third house and he is the second one and he stacked it or his father did, and the stack is not firewood because nobody at four houses has ever been short of firewood, and it is not building stone because it is not stone.

"That is a record," said Lissa Vale, standing in front of it, from about four feet off, and not touching it.

"It is a stack of old wood," said the man at the second house, who had come out to look at them and had not come out to be pleasant.

"It is both," said Lissa Vale, "and I am not going to touch it, and I am going to say one thing about it and then I am going to leave it alone, because I have been in a building with a book in a press that a licensed man had to be asked about before anybody opened it and I am not going to do to a stranger's stack what this fen did to its own book for a season.

"A stack of pressed rootwood against a north wall, rotted, and nobody knows when, is a thing somebody cut on purpose and put out of the weather and then forgot about. And the people who forgot about it were not the people who made it, because it is rotted, and it has been rotted for a great many years, and nobody who could have told you has ever been asked."

The man at the second house said that if this fen wanted to open a stranger's stack it could open it, and that his family had been on that shelf thirty-one years and had never once thought about it, and that this was apparently what people came up the road for.

And Lissa Vale said thank you, which she says to a person who has told her the truth about their own ground, and she asked him one question, and the question was whether the family had ever burned any of it, and he said no, and would not say why not, and did not have to.

---

The wind came off the ice on the eighth at about the second hour and it did the same on the ninth and the tenth and the eleventh and it will do it on every day there is, and it is the single fact about this country that governs the whole of what follows and it is entered at the top of the ledger on the eighth in Lissa Vale's hand, and it is in the ordinary words.

> The wind comes off the ice from about the second hour every afternoon and it does not stop. Work with instruments is done between the first and the second hour. Work with a rule and a lens and a lamp is done all afternoon in the lee of a wall. A man holding a lamp at an angle can keep it for as long as his arms last and this is the whole of what a licensed man is for on this shelf.

And the fen read the ledger aloud that evening in the barn, at about the ninth hour, standing, to whoever came, and it is the fen's practice and it is the practice of the seed-house table and it travels.

Lissa Vale read the whole of it — the going, the money, the compost line and its two figures, the four-word decision in Gael Senn's book that nobody has seen, the ninety days cut into two boards that nobody is cutting, the reading of the door on the sixteenth that nobody will be standing in front of, the sub-office at Fennmere and the date and the hour, and the note of the twenty-third with the argument at the end of it. She read it to about eleven people in a stone barn on a plateau, and the reader for it was Coll Renner, and he read the numbers back after her because he is the reader of this body wherever the body is.

And by the fourth evening the arithmetic was in that barn four hundred miles from the fen that had made it, and the man at the second house came in on the second evening to listen and stayed, and on the third evening he brought a stool so that an old woman at the first house could sit, and on the fourth evening he said the sentence that made Merrid Ollvar decide to have the fieldbook opened in her own yard.

"You are the first people who have read anything out loud in this barn since my father's time," he said, "and I have not said that to make you welcome, and I want it understood that I have said it so that you know what it is going to look like when you do the thing you are going to do in my yard in front of my family."

---

The row is the third one off the north wall, and it is a row of pear trees about two hundred and forty years old by anybody's guess, and there is a graft on every one of them, put in long before Merrid Ollvar came, put in by somebody with a knife and a strip of bark and no hurry.

Marek Vale did not touch the trees. He asked permission of a woman who did not have to give it and then he looked at one of the cuts for about ten minutes standing back, and then he went and got the rule.

He said the method out loud before he used it, which is his standing and which Lissa Vale entered in the ledger on the ninth with the hour on it, and it is the whole of the batch's science and it is not magic.

"A tree makes wood in a ring in a year while it is alive," he said, "and the ring is a year, and the years stack, one outside the other, and if you cut across the stack you can count the years because they are in order and there is no way to get them out of order. That is a fact about wood and it is how a miller dates a plank and it is how I dated a stake in your low quarter in the spring and got it wrong, and I am telling you I got it wrong so you know the size of the tool.

"So I am going to count the healed growth over a dressed cut on your pear, and I am going to divide it by what this ground puts on in a year, and I will get your year by cutting a twig off this season's extension and measuring it, and it will not be a round number and I do not want a round number, I want the number that is actually there."

He cut a twig and measured it and then he went along the row on his knees with the rule and a lens and a piece of chalk and he did nine of them, and he did not touch a tenth, and he was at the ninth for about twenty minutes and the wind came off the ice at the second hour and Coll Renner said that instruments were done for the day and Marek Vale said that a rule was not an instrument and went on and finished the ninth at about the fourth hour with his hands dead.

Then he stood up and said the number, and he said it to the yard and not to the woman, because the woman is not of the fen and the fen's numbers are read back by its own reader.

"Eleven," he said. "And I will give you the figure I divided by, which is four-tenths of an inch on this ground in this year, and the healed growth on the first cut I came to is about two and a quarter inches, and two and a quarter over four tenths is between five and six, and the cuts are not five or six years old. **They are one.** **This season.**"

And the wind came off the ice and the pump on the plateau was not running because there is no pump on the plateau, there is a well, and the well is two hundred yards downslope and four houses drink from it, and the only sound in that yard for about ten seconds was nine people breathing.

Then the man of about fifty's voice, from four hundred miles away, in the head of the reader who was standing there, in the mouth of Anneke Roos reading the figure back to a yard in which she was not standing:

Then somebody asked the obvious question in the wrong voice, which was the man at the second house, and it is the question the whole volume is built on, and it was asked in this yard and not in that one.

"Why would a man cut a live branch on a pear tree and dress the wound."

And there were two answers available at the second hour of an afternoon on a shelf in the high country, and both of them were true, and the chapter did not choose between them, and the fen did not choose between them, and the plate is four hundred miles from anybody who could.

A bad cut. A branch that was going to die anyway and a man who took it early and dressed the wound because a dressed wound does not tear in wind.

Or a good cut, with a reason, on a day somebody knew about beforehand, made by a man who expected to be back at that tree in an hour.

And Tova Reed said, out loud, in front of four houses and a stranger and a licensed man, the sentence that is the hinge of this chapter and of the whole of the volume after it, and she said it flatly and without any performance about it, because she is a person who says the whole of a thing in one sentence and then waits.

"A bad cut has a reason in the man's head," she said, "and this is nine trees with the same cut on them and the same dressing and nobody dressed a wound nine times by accident, and I am not going to stand in a yard in a book and I am not going to say the next part, because the next part is that if it was a good cut then somebody knew, in advance, that these nine trees were going to have to lose a branch, and that is a list, and a list is a thing you make before you start."

And Marek Vale said the only sentence he says in this chapter and it is not an argument.

"I am not going to say which one it is," he said, "and I am not going to say it in a yard to a woman whose eleven weeks are in a crate on my bench, and I will tell you both the reason. If I say bad, then we have found a wound and we have a method and we can go home. **If I say good, then this row is the first thing anybody has ever found that a dead man wrote down on purpose, and I will not know what to do with my afternoon.**

"So I am going to read the face, and the face will say which of the two it is, and I would like the reader to be Mrs Ollvar, because she has been on this shelf thirty-one years and she is the only person in this yard whose thirty-one years are about this ground."

And the reader was Merrid Ollvar, and she read the figure back, and she read it back exactly, and she asked for the number of the day it was read on to be written at the bottom of it, and it was, and the clerk was not there and Lissa Vale wrote it and signed her own name and not the clerk's, and the fen entered afterwards that the reader's hand wrote it and not the clerk's, and that this is a thing a body with a clerk does not do and did once.

---

The buyer comes on the eleventh, which was the eleventh of a month two months back, and it is nine days off, and the fruit is in a stone room on that shelf in crates, and nobody on that shelf has decided whether it is a harvest or a fault, and nobody on that shelf has been told which of the two readings this fen has come down the road to make, because a woman of fifty-four has not yet been told either and is not going to be told tonight.
