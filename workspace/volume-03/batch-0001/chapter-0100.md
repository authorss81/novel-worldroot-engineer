# Chapter 100

## A Crate Off The Plateau

They opened the crate in the yard on the third, at about the ninth hour, standing, in daylight, with six of the fen there and four people who were not, because the fen does this with anything that arrives at that gate and the gate is the place where it happens.

Thirty-one pears.

They were cold and dry and sound. No bruise, no rot, no soft place, and the stalks on them were still on, dried brown and brittle, and the skins had gone the color that pears go in a stone room in the third week. They were, as far as anybody in that yard could tell by looking at them, good fruit, and they had been picked, and the picking was visible in the way the stalk had been broken rather than pulled.

"That is a harvest," said Ilsa Kern, from the gate.

"It is a harvest of something," said Tova Reed.

And then a woman of about fifty-four told them her name and where she was from, in that order, because she had been asked to and because she is the kind of person who answers a question in the order it was asked, and the fen entered both and the hour.

"Merrid Ollvar. Cauldron Reach. Four houses and eleven acres on a south-facing shelf, and I have been on it thirty-one years and my father's name is on the wall of the third house and he is the second one and he is not in any record you have got."

There is no record of Cauldron Reach in this holding. The clerk checked, in front of her, at the middle of the table, which is the fen's practice with a stranger's name, and there is not a page in eleven years and a half of the fen's own book with those two words on it, and he said so and read it back, and Merrid Ollvar said that she had expected that and had not come about it.

"I have a ledger of my own and it is a bad one," she said, "and I have four houses and thirty-one years, and I am not here about the ledger. I am here about the pears."

---

She had picked them on the sixteenth of a month three months back, and had kept them in a stone room under the north wall of her own yard, and had laid them on boards off the ground because that is what you do, and she had eaten none and had sold none and had counted them twice.

"Eleven weeks," she said. "That row has not fruited since the same month last year, and the year before that it gave a stone to a basket, and the year before that nothing anybody would pick. And on the sixteenth of a month three months back I went up that row with two baskets and I filled them, and the fruit came off trees that were bare of it in the spring, and I picked it and I brought it down off the plateau on a hired horse and I have kept it eleven weeks in a cold room and I have not eaten one and I would like somebody to tell me what I have."

Nobody in that yard said anything clever for a while. The pump ran. Somebody's cart went past the gate and did not stop, which is ordinary and was not entered.

"May I ask you the question you came to ask," said Tova Reed, "and then I will ask you four hundred and you will answer all of them, and I want the order respected because I have watched people answer the second question and lose the first one."

"Ask it."

"What do you want."

And Merrid Ollvar said it in one sentence, and it is the sentence the whole of this is standing next to, and she said it without any performance at all, because she had said it to herself every day for eleven weeks.

"I want somebody to tell me whether it is a harvest or a fault," she said, "and the reason I want to know is that I have a buyer who has offered me a price the hundredweight for four seasons running and has never once asked me why the fruit is odd, and he is going to be on this shelf on the seventh of next month, and he will buy every pear on it at that price whether they are a fault or not, and that is eleven weeks of my life and every pear on this shelf and it is the only money these four houses will see this year."

"And you have not sold them."

"I have not sold them because I have not told him I have them, and I have not told him because I do not know what I am selling, and I am fifty-four and I have been on that shelf thirty-one years and I have never in my life sold a thing to a man who had not told me what it was."

---

Anneke Roos did the arithmetic in the open on the fen's own paper because that is what this yard does with a number, and she did it standing, and she read it out twice, and the second time was not for the fen.

"A hundred and forty-one trees," she said. "Eleven acres, one hundred and forty-one trees, of which thirty-one are in the row she picked, and I am taking her thirty-one because thirty-one is what is in the crate and I am not going to do a whole orchard in a yard on a stranger's word.

"Eleven weeks in a stone room, laid on boards, none spoiled, none bruised, no rot in thirty-one, which is a keeping figure and not a selling figure and I want that said before anybody hears a number. And she has two of them here and I have opened one and I have cut it and the flesh under the skin is sound and the core is sound and there is nothing in it that a man could call a fault from the outside of it.

"Now the money. She has told me the price is four seasons the same and she has not told me the price, and I am not going to guess a price in a yard, and if she tells me the price this afternoon I will do the sum in front of her and I will do it now, at the fourth hour, with a clerk writing it, and I will not be tidy about it."

So she told them the price. And Anneke Roos did the sum twice on the fen's own paper, and the yard was very quiet for a minute.

"Four seasons the same price and he has never once asked why the fruit is odd, and he has a name on a paper and the paper has four lines on it and I am not going to say the name twice in this yard, and it is not a name anybody here has any standing to say anything about."

And then the sum, and it is the third time this building has done a sum in a room and it got worse each time, and Anneke Roos read it out with the pen in her hand.

"Thirty-one pears at that price is a figure I am not going to say out loud in a yard with a stranger standing in it, because it is her money and not ours and it is not my place to make a woman of fifty-four hear her own figure said in front of six people.

"Here is what I will say. The whole of it, at the figure she gave me, is more than this holding has taken in since the charter was made. Not more than the repair cost. More than everything. I have the fen's own book here and I have gone back four years in it this morning and it is not a close thing and I am not going to be asked to defend the arithmetic twice this afternoon, and I am going to say one more sentence and then I am going to stop talking about money in this yard, which is my fortnight's standing and I am spending it on a stranger, and I would like that entered.

"It is more than the repair cost, and the repair is standing, and the two things are four hundred miles apart and they are not in the same account and anybody who puts them in the same account is doing it on purpose."

That went in with the hour and the clerk read it back and Lissa Vale read it back after him, and nobody thanked her, and the standing is that nobody in that yard is thanked for being readable and cannot be.

---

The two explanations everybody wanted were both wrong, and the fen wanted one of them to be right for about an hour and a half, and it is worth saying that it wanted them and did not have them.

The first was a graft. Coll Renner had come up the fen road at about the second hour to look at the pears because he was in the district on another matter and had heard about a crate, and he stood and looked at them and said the sentence that killed it in nine words.

"A graft does not do that."

"Say the rest of it," said Tova Reed, "because nine words is a judgment and I want the reason under it."

"A graft carries the thing that was put in," he said, "and it loses the thing that was there, and what you are looking at is a tree that has gone on being a tree. Whoever cut that row cut it and the row came back and the row is the same row. That is not a graft. There is nothing in a graft that remembers being a tree, and if there were, the beards in this district would not be worth the sixpence they are worth."

The second was a trick by the buyer. That one died on its own, in about a minute, because Tova Reed asked the only question anybody asked all afternoon that was about money.

"You said the buyer has offered the same price for four seasons and has never asked why the fruit is odd. When did he first offer it."

"Four seasons back. The first spring I was on my own with the place after my father died."

"Then he has offered the same price four seasons and you have had an odd harvest in this one of them and not in the others, and he does not know about the odd one, because he has never seen it, because it did not exist four seasons ago and you have not told him." She put her hands flat on the crate. "A man who is paying a flat price for four seasons is a man who has priced a bad year as though it were an ordinary one. He has not tricked you. **He is about to be tricked by you, and you did not mean to do it, and that is a different thing and it is worse, because you will have to decide what to do about it before the seventh of next month, and you will have to decide it in a yard with a buyer in it.**"

Nobody in that yard had thought of it that way and the yard went quiet again, and the man of about fifty said, out loud, that that was the first time anybody had said anything in two days that made him feel less certain and not more, and that he was the man who had voted against the book in the spring and that this was a different complaint.

---

And then Lissa Vale asked the question the whole of the going turns on, and she asked it at the fourth hour with the light off the door and the pears cut open on the bench in front of nine people, and it is her question and it is the same question she has asked four times this season and it is always the same question.

"Who is going to be asked what it means."

There was no answer in that yard, and the reason there was no answer was that there was no such person in it, and Coll Renner said the only useful sentence anybody said all day.

"A thing like that needs a licensed man and not a guess."

"Then you are licensed," said Lissa Vale, "and there is no money in this holding and there is a shilling a month going out to a district, and I am going to ask you now, in front of these people, what it costs to be asked, and you are going to say a figure and not a sentence, and then I am going to say no to it and pay it anyway, and you will know that I said no, and that is the arrangement and I am not going to improve on it."

And Coll Renner said a figure, and Lissa Vale said no to it, and paid it, and read the payment back with the hour, and the fen entered that the steward of a trust standing nine hundred miles from the ground refused a licensed man's figure and paid it inside four minutes, **and that the refusal was the standing and not the objection**, and that nobody in that yard thanked either of them, and that is the sixth time she has entered an act against the Dryfoot Trust's own interest this season and she has said she has stopped counting and will not start again, and the clerk entered the sentence and the hour and did not enter a number because she asked him not to and because he is her clerk.

---

And the last thing that happened on the third of this month happened at about the sixth hour, when Merrid Ollvar was asked how far Cauldron Reach was, and said nine days up and four down with one change of horses at a place with no name that she has written down in her own ledger and not in anybody else's, and that there was a buyer on her shelf on the seventh of next month, and that the wind on that shelf comes off the ice from about the second hour and that instruments do not work in it after the second hour, and that the first of the four houses has a stone barn with a long bench in it that a man can stand a trestle on.

And then she said the thing that made the going a decision and not an offer, and she said it to Marek Vale and not to the yard, and she said it in a low voice because she had been in yards before.

"You are the one who fixed your own well," she said. "The gatekeeper's daughter told me in Fennmere, on the road down, in the rain, and she said it the way people say a thing they have decided is a story and then find out is not. And I am not asking you to come up that road for nothing. I am asking because I have four houses and a stone barn and thirty-one pears and a buyer coming, and I cannot do arithmetic about the buyer, and I can do arithmetic about a well."

And Marek Vale said the thing that put four hundred miles of road in front of him, and he entered it in his own hand in the seed-route ledger that night with the hour on it.

"Nine days up," he said. "And there is a row of pears on that shelf that fruited eleven weeks out of its season, and you want to know whether it is a fault, and I have to tell you before you decide to spend money on it that I do not know, and I have a graft and a licensed man and two links and that is all I have got, and I have never in my life read a tree that has done that, and neither has the man in this yard who is licensed and has been doing it for twenty-two years."

"Then I will put you up there," said Merrid Ollvar, "and I will not pay you, and you may tell anybody you came free, and I will not correct them."
