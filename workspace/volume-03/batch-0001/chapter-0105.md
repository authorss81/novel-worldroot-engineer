# Chapter 105

## The Graft

The third tree in the row is dying, and the cut they wanted to read is under it.

That is the whole of the twelfth of this month and it took about twenty minutes to establish and it is not a complication somebody put there. On a shelf where the wind comes off the ice from the second hour, a pear two hundred and forty years old with a graft at the collar put in by somebody who is dead, on ground four inches of topsoil over pale rootwood, in a season that has already given one crop eleven weeks out of its time, is a tree with a great deal of work left in it and not many reasons left to do any. The union at the collar is four seasons old and it is a bad one, put in on the wrong side of a north wind by a man who was not in a hurry because the graft was somebody else's graft and somebody else was dead. The limb carrying the row's extension is on the far side of the cut, and the root under the cut is the root the whole limb is fed from, and the root is going.

"If we lose the root under that face this week," said Marek Vale, at about the ninth hour, in the lee of the wall with the rule in his hand and the wind not yet up, "then the face closes over and we have a dead tree and a mark on it, and there is no way on this earth to get a healed face under a dead tree. **You cannot read a thing you cannot reach.** And the reading is why we came up nine days of road and it is eight days after I said I did not know what I would do with my afternoon."

"And if the root goes this week," said the man at the second house, who had come over and had a spade in his hand and had not been asked for it, "then I have a dead pear in a row of a hundred and forty and a hole in the ground, and the buyer's day is the seventeenth of next month and he does not care about a row of one, and I would like somebody to be careful."

"I will be careful," said Marek Vale. "I will be as careful as this can be done, and I am going to do it in daylight in front of you and a licensed man and a witness, and I am going to write it down before I do it, and I am going to read it back after."

---

What he did was a graft. A small one, a scion of the same pear taken from the second tree in the row and set into the limb of the third about a foot below the face, cut-bark to cut-bark, and bound wet, and it took about forty minutes and he did it with a knife and a strip and his left hand was not much use and he did not ask for it to be.

The rules of the thing, in the fen's own book, in his own words, and the reader for it was Tova Reed, and the witness was Coll Renner and he wrote four lines and the hour in his own book and handed it to the clerk, who is four hundred miles off, so he read it into the seed-route ledger in Lissa Vale's hand and she signed it and he read it back and did not soften it.

> One. Of links a man can keep without help, three, and a fourth fails and is known to fail and is not attempted. **One was made in the spring and died in the spring and it is not a link and it is not counted. Two is counted, and it is the collar of the second tree in the row, which is the tree in the pot in this yard, and it was read on the fifth.** And the dead one is entered here in its number because a thing that is dead in a pot is still a thing somebody will ask about in ten years.
>
> Two. A third link is attempted at about the ninth hour of the twelfth, a scion of the second tree set into the limb of the third a foot below the face, and the face is not to be cut into for any reason, and the scion is not to be read with an instrument in this season.
>
> Three. Two and three are the whole of what there is and the number does not go up. A fourth is not attempted and the reason is that it fails and the reason it fails is on a page in this fen's book from a month two months back and is not restated here because it does not need restating and a man who restates it is a man who thinks he might be the exception.
>
> Four. The record of the graft is entered in the licensed man's own book and in this ledger and there is no line in a use log for a graft, and there has never been one, and it is not proposed.

Nobody in that barn argued with any of it, and the man at the second house asked the one question worth asking, which was what happened if it worked.

"Then it lives," said Marek Vale, "and the limb above the face is fed off a scion out of the same row, and the face under it stays open to a knife for as long as this tree is alive, and the tree that is being saved and the tree we came to read are the same tree, and that is the whole trick and it is a graft and not a miracle and it has been done for two thousand years."

"And if it does not work."

"Then I will have put a piece of the second tree into the third tree and killed the third tree's own supply slowly, and I will have lost the face and the row and the buyer's eleven weeks along with it, and I will have written it down here and I will not have written it in a good light."

And Tova Reed asked one question about it at the seventh hour, which is her standing, and which was about who loses, and the answer was the scion.

"It is out of the second tree," she said, "and the second tree is the one that fruited eleven weeks out of season, and if the second tree has put something into the third that the third has never had, then in four years this row will not be one row of pears. It will be one row of pears and a question with a scion in it, and nobody in this country will be able to tell which is which from the ground. **I am not saying take it out. I am saying write down that it is in there, because the whole of this body's argument is a readable thing, and a thing nobody wrote down is a thing nobody can check, including us.**"

And that went in on the twelfth with the hour on it, in her hand, in the seed-route ledger, and Dunmar Fitch read it back four hundred miles off on the fourteenth at about the ninth hour and the reader after him was the man of about fifty, and it is the first entry this body has ever made in advance of the thing happening, and the fen entered that fact about itself in the same breath.

It took.

That is the sentence and it is a fact and it is on the page and it is entered with the hour and it is not entered as a triumph. The bound strip held through the afternoon and through the night and through the ninth hour of the thirteenth, and on the thirteenth the scion's own extension above the band was three-tenths of an inch, and the root under the face was still going, and the face was still open to a knife, and the third tree of the row was not going to be dead by the fourteenth.

And the bind is the difference between that and the other thing, and Marek Vale put it in the ledger in the ordinary words and it is the whole of the difference between a thing that worked and a thing that would have.

> Worked. Two and a half inches of extension on the day of the graft, four-tenths on the day after, three-tenths on the second morning, and the union is holding at a quarter of the extension it should be holding at, and the band is wet, and it will hold. **A union at a quarter of what it should be is a union that is holding and is costing the tree, and the cost is being taken out of the extension, and the extension is what the face is being kept open with, and so the row will hold this tree at about three-quarters and not at the whole.**

Coll Renner read that back and did not soften it, and Lissa Vale entered it, and the man of about fifty's reading of the ledger in a barn on the fourteenth read it back to eleven people, and a woman at the first house said the word *quarters* out loud and was the only person at Cauldron Reach who said anything at all about it.

---

And the party was then two links down on a plateau with one licensed man, a hired bench, a trestle in a stone barn, and a woman whose buyer's day was in twenty-five days and closing.

And Marek Vale did the arithmetic out loud, on the twelfth, at about the sixth hour, standing in the barn with the wind shaking the door, and it is in the ledger and it is the whole of why the thirteenth of this month is what it is.

"The scion is three-tenths a day and it is a third of what it should be and it is all the extension this limb has," he said. "**The face under it is going to close.** Not today. In eight or nine days, at that rate, and the buyer is in twenty-five days, and the buyer does not care, and the face is the only reason this trip cost nine days of road and thirteen weeks of a compost line.

"And I have one thing I have not done and I know how to do it and I have never done it and there is no person in this barn who has watched me do it and one person in this barn has told me twice tonight not to do it."

Coll Renner said the honest thing, standing in the barn door with the case under his arm, and he said it the way he says everything, which is that he says it and then he goes home and you may do it or not do it and it will not be his name on it.

"**A man who has never done it should not do it on a tree four houses drink from.**" He said it flatly and without any performance. "And I will say the rest of it because I have been licensed for twenty-two years and I have watched two men learn this in a barn with somebody else holding the other end, and both of them were in a room with a third person in it who had done it, and one of them is carrying the mark and one of them is carrying something worse than the mark, and neither of them learned it in a barn on a plateau with four houses and a well.

"And I will say the thing that is the actual objection and not the caution. **A draw takes it out of somewhere.** That is the whole of what it is and you know it as well as I do. You are proposing to hold a limb open for a day with something that is not the weather, and there is no such thing in this world, and whatever is holding that limb open at the fourth hour of the fourteenth is coming out of the ground under this yard, and this yard has no water in it and never has, and there is a well two hundred yards down this slope with four families' names on the bucket."

"And I will say the other thing too, because I said the first thing and it would be easy of me to stop there. If you do it and it goes well, then you have done a thing on my license in my presence and I will have to write it in my book and hand it in, and a licensed man's book is not a private book. And if it goes badly, then there is a well on this shelf that is shallower than it was and four houses that drink from it, and there is no appeal from that anywhere in this country or in yours."

Marek Vale heard all of it, and did not argue with any of it, and the fen entered the objection entire with the hour on it and did not enter a reply to it, and the reason the fen did not enter a reply is that there was not one.

And then he said the thing that is the decision, and he said it standing, in a barn, at about the sixth hour of the twelfth, in front of a licensed man, a steward, a woman of fifty-four, a man with a spade, and eleven other people who had come in.

"I am going to try to hold it," he said. "**And I want it written in three places that I have been told no by a man who knows more about it than I do, and that I am doing it on his objection and not over it, and that the objection is a better argument than anything I have got and I have not got one.**

"And I want one more thing written, and Lissa Vale is to write it and I am to say it slowly. **If the well two hundred yards down this slope is shallower on the fourteenth than it was on the sixteenth of last month, then it is entered as my cost and not as the weather's, and it is entered in the seed-route ledger and not in a book anybody can lose, and it is read out loud to this household and to the three others within a fortnight, whatever I say at the time and whatever I am like at the time.**"

And Tova Reed said the one thing in that whole evening that was not a procedure.

"You have just given away a well," she said. "Not the fruit, which is hers, and not the road, which is yours. **A well.** In daylight, on the record, before you have done it, and not afterwards, and that is the first time anybody in this yard has given away the thing before doing it instead of after, and I am going to write it in the ledger in the seed-route and not in the fen's own book, because the fen's book is a record of what this body intended and **that was an intention, and this is a cost, and a cost goes in the other one.**"

And it went in the seed-route ledger on the twelfth, in her hand, in the ordinary words, with the hour, and Dunmar Fitch read it back four hundred miles away on the fourteenth at about the ninth hour with nine other people in a yard, and Lissa Vale read it back after him, and the count of the readings of the seventh column did not change, because the rule is the first and the middle and the last, and the first was read on the first and the middle is the sixteenth and has not come.

And that is the whole of the twelfth of this month, and there is no note under section nine for it, because there is nothing in it that anybody can check, and the fen entered that as a fact about the fourteenth and not as a virtue.
