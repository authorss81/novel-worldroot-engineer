# Chapter 102

## The Road Up

The party was decided standing at the gate on the sixth at about the seventh hour, in daylight, and the fen decided it the way it decides everything now, which is that nobody has standing to decide it and so somebody says a thing out loud and everybody who does not object is in it.

Marek Vale. Tova Reed. Lissa Vale. Coll Renner, licensed, with the case and the copper clip and a day rate the fen argued about for forty minutes and settled in one line.

Gael Senn stays and keeps the books, and the fen entered that this was decided standing in a yard at the seventh hour and not by a vote, and that a vote was not taken because the person who would have asked for one knew she was the person being left and had a standing about being asked, and that both halves of that are in the book.

Nobody argued about Tova Reed. Nobody argued about Lissa Vale either, and the reason nobody argued about Lissa Vale is that she said the thing herself before anybody could, standing at the north end of the seed house bench with her hand flat on the ledger, and it is the fourth time this season she has put her hand flat on that bench and it is the reason nobody in that yard ever argues with her about leaving.

"I am going, and I am going because there is nobody else in this building who can read a variety book and tell you whether thirty-one pears are a fault in the keeping or a fault in the ground, and I have been reading one for thirty-one years and I have never claimed to be able to read a tree, and I am not going to start on a plateau with a buyer coming."

"And I am going to leave the books to Gael Senn, who is twenty-four and who has kept the reading of a door that has no charter behind it since the second of last month, and who has never once been thanked for it, and I would like the not-thanking left exactly where it is when I come back, because if it moves while I am gone then I have come back to a yard that has learned something false about itself."

---

The going cost money and the going also cost feeding, and the fen put both in the same paragraph and did not separate them, which is a thing this yard learned in the spring.

"Eleven shillings a day of horse and a man at the change of horses and board at both ends and the hire of a bench for a man to stand a trestle on, and the fare down again at the end whether we are still going or not," said Anneke Roos, with the pen, in the ordinary words. "Which is a figure I am not going to repeat, and it is more than the holding has, and it is a quarter of what a good harvest on that shelf would bring if the fruit is a fault and we have to pay for the fault out of the same tin that the fault is in."

"And now the other half and I am not going to let it be a separate line. Nine days of horse. Nine days of four. What a horse eats off ground that is being brought back up to nothing is a hundredweight a week of something, and a hundredweight of what is four shillings, and thirteen weeks at four shillings is a hundred and four shillings, and I have put it in the compost line and I have not taken it out, and the shortfall is not three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight this month and it is not going to be three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight next month, and nobody in this yard is to read a number in that line and think the seventh of next month bought anything."

"I have written it in the ordinary words and I have not made it a fault. **Going to look at a thing is not a fault. It is a cost. The fault in this line was put there in a month four months back by a well and it is still there and we are adding to it with a road, and I would like somebody to write the two together in a year and see which of them a person could have done without.**"

That went in with the hour, and the clerk read it back, and Lissa Vale read it back after him, and the man of about fifty read the shortfall figure back and asked for his initial to be put against nothing.

Ilsa Kern cut ninety into both boards over the bay on the sixth, and said the number out loud on her way past and did not stop.

---

And then the hard part, and it was not the money and it was not the going.

The fieldbook was in the press under the ledger on the north end of the seed house bench, and it had been in that press since before the charter was made, and it is a fragment — a front board, a living root bead, and a handful of pages — and the back board and most of the survey pages are gone and nobody in this holding knows where.

It is Lissa Vale's. It came to the seed house in her hands and it is her husband's and it may not be moved and it may not be sold and it may not be lent and it may not be put into another instrument without telling the sub-office at Fennmere, and the person who told the sub-office last time was a woman who was not Lissa Vale, and that was nine years ago and nobody in that yard has asked her about it and nobody is going to.

"It comes with us," said Lissa Vale.

And the yard did not argue with it, and the reason the yard did not argue with it is that the argument had already been had in four minutes and had been had in the only way this yard has arguments, which is somebody says the whole thing out loud in a long sentence and then somebody else says the other whole thing in a long sentence, and then a person with a rule reads them both back.

Anneke Roos said it first and she said it in the steward's way, which is that she asked one question about who loses.

"If it comes with us then the sub-office has to be told in person, and a woman of fifty-four has to walk to Fennmere in the rain and come back, and that is a morning and half a mile of somebody's day, and that somebody is not going to be the clerk because the clerk is needed at the gate and it is not going to be Marek Vale because he is going to be carrying a case up a hill and there is no courtesy in sending a man who is already loaded, and it is not going to be me because I would like it seen that somebody with a stake in it went, and the only person left in this yard with a stake in it who is not loaded is the reader of the sub-office, and I am not asking a reader of a sub-office to walk half a mile in the rain for a book she is not allowed to open and I have just said so and I am not going to say it twice."

"I'll go," said Lissa Vale.

"You will not," said Anneke Roos. "You will go to Fennmere in the rain, and Perrin Osse will write it into the register at the counter with the date and the hour, and you will stand there while he does it, and he will read it back to you, and it will take a morning, and I have just told you I will not ask you to do it and I am asking you to do it, and I would like the clerk to enter both halves of that sentence in the order I said it in and not to tidy it."

And Dunmar Fitch entered both halves in the order they were said in and read them back, and Lissa Vale read the entry back after him, and the yard did not tidy it.

And then Marek Vale said the thing that puts four hundred miles of road in front of the whole of them, and it is nine words, and it is the reason the going has a cost in it at all.

"I am not going to ask you for it."

"Enter that with my name on it and not yours," said Lissa Vale, "because if it is in the book with your name on it then in four years a person is going to read that the engineer of record opened his father's book, and I did the opening and nobody asked him and he did not ask me, and those are two different facts and one of them is true."

The clerk entered it and the hour and read it back, and Lissa Vale read it back, and Anneke Roos initialled it as an opinion in four words, and the four words have never been explained to anybody.

---

And then there was the part of a going that nobody puts in a book, and the fen put it in a book anyway, because the fen has learned that the part nobody puts in a book is the part that decides whether a thing can be done twice.

Jonas Byre asked who was going to read the door on the sixteenth, and Anneke Roos answered him in about nine words, and the answer was that it would be read because the rule says it is read, and that the rule does not care whether there is anybody in the yard, and that the fen had kept seven practices for two months on exactly that basis and was not going to break one of them on a Thursday because four people were four hundred miles off.

Then she said the part that was in her mind, and she said it to the yard and not to Gael Senn, and the clerk entered it and she did not initial it, which is her standing when a thing is not an opinion.

"I want the reading of the sixteenth entered as a reading with no body in front of it, and I want the count of the readings of the seventh column entered as it comes, whatever it is, and I want whoever reads it to say out loud that there is nobody in the yard, and I want that said before the counting and not after it, because a reading that nobody heard is worth exactly what it is worth and this yard has spent a season finding out what that is."

"And I want the first of next month read whole, and the last of this month read whole, and if I am not back for the last of this month then the last of this month is read by whoever is in the yard, and that is not mine to decide and I am not going to decide it now while I am standing here with a crate to pack."

Gael Senn said that she had already decided it, in her own book, on the second of this month, and that the decision was four words long, and that she was not going to say what they were because a decision of four words that is read out loud becomes a decision somebody else has to keep. The clerk entered that there is a four-word decision in the examiner's own book with a date on it and that the fen has not asked to see it and does not intend to.

And Ilsa Kern said the other thing, standing at the bench with the boards behind her, and it was the only thing anybody said in that yard that was not a procedure.

"Ninety into both boards on the sixth," she said. "And nobody is going to be here to cut ninety-one, and I have said the number out loud every morning since the flood and I am going to keep saying it, and it does not need anybody to hear it and I am not going to stop saying it because four people have gone up a road."

Nobody in that yard argued with that and the clerk entered it, and there is no note under section nine for it and there never will be, because it is not evidence of what this body intended and it is evidence of a woman in a yard, and the fen has decided that those are two different kinds of entry and the second kind is worth more.

---

They went up on the seventh, at about the sixth hour, and the man of about fifty read the fifth clause of the new writing back at the gate as they went, from the page in his own hand, which is his standing and which nobody in that yard has ever once tried to talk him out of.

> Five. The record of this route is one record. It is kept at this end, and a copy of it is sent to Cray on the ordinary post, and a copy of it is sent to Cauldron Reach in the Glassward when there is anything in it to send, and no person holding any part of it keeps a private copy of any part of it, and there is no part of it that is anybody's own.

"That is mine and I voted against it," he said, "and I am reading it out because I am the reader of this body and a clause that goes out of a gate without being read is a clause that has not been agreed to, and I would like whoever is at the other end of nine days of road to know that one man in Lantern Reach did not agree to it and is still standing in this yard holding the pen up."

Nobody answered him and nobody was going to and that is also in the book.

Underneath the day's work, dated the seventh, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the twenty-third note under section nine of the charter schedule, and there is no charter, and the note says so on its face.

*A party of four, decided standing at a gate in daylight, and the clerk entered that a vote was not taken and why, and that the reason has a person in it. A licensed man's day rate, refused and paid. Nine days of horse entered in the compost line in the ordinary words and not as a fault, and the shortfall figure in that line entered twice and asked for by a member of this body and given. A fieldbook carried out of a building for the first time since it came into it, and a sub-office told in person at a counter in Fennmere by the person whose it is, standing there while a clerk wrote it into a register and read it back, and the cost of the telling entered as a morning and half a mile of somebody's day. And a sentence entered with the reader's name on it and not the engineer's, and a line read out at a gate by a member of this body who voted against it, to a road.*

*An argument. This is worth nothing against a cut.*
