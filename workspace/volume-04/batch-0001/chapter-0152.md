# Chapter 152

## Ninety-Four

The engineer of record, the man from the north row with the cough, and the examiner went out of the north gate at about the fourth hour of the morning of the seventeenth of this month with a pail between them and a rule and two watches and a sheet, and they were back at about the eighth hour of the evening, and they timed three places and the three numbers do not agree with each other and none of the three is a measurement.

**The pail was the same pail for all three.** That is the first thing and it is the only thing in this chapter that makes the other two things mean anything, and the clerk entered it before the figures and not after them, at the request of the examiner, who said that a figure about a pail with no pail against it is a figure about a shape.

Gael Senn kept the time and she did not round and she has never rounded and she said out loud, in the low quarter, at about the sixth hour of the morning, in front of about nine people, that she was going to write the numbers as they came and that if anybody wanted them rounded they could come and do the rounding themselves.

The nine people in that yard were not nine people who had been gathered. They were nine people who were already standing there, because it is a yard people stand in in the morning, and the examiner said the thing about rounding into a yard that had not been told she was coming, and four of the nine were the households who had written the bill on the eleventh, and one of those four was the woman who had walked it up the fen road and back down again and who had still eaten nothing and had not been offered anything and had not mentioned it.

They asked what the three of them were doing with a pail and a watch and the engineer of record answered them in the ordinary way, and then one of the men in that yard asked the question that four of the men in that yard had already asked the other three, and it is worth putting down exactly because it was the same words four times.

"**Is the road taking it off us?**"

Four men said it. Not one of the other forty-odd people in that quarter said anything at all, and the four who said it said it in the same words and in the same tone, and one of the four is a man of about thirty and two of them are men of about sixty and one of them is a man who has a cart and does not haul.

And the engineer of record said the thing that is now in this holding's book as the answer, and he said it four times, once to each man, in the same words each time, and he said it before he looked at anybody's face.

"**I have got a pail and a watch and I have not got an answer, and I am not going to give you one today, and I would rather say that than give you a figure that belongs to me.**"

And one of the four asked the second question, and the second question is the one the man of about fifty said in a yard on the eighteenth was the question in this yard, and it went on the sheet inside a week.

"**Then who is taking it off us?**"

And the engineer of record said: "**I do not know, and I have not got a charter and I have got no standing on that road, and if I told you a name I would be telling you a name I got from a sheet with no address on it, and that would be worse than nothing, because you would use it and I would have given it to you for free.**"

And the fen entered on the eighteenth of this month that four men in a yard nine miles off said the same sentence in the same words, and that the engineer of record answered the same question four times in the same words, **and that the count of men in that quarter who have said that sentence to somebody is four and the count of people in that quarter is four hundred and forty-one, and that the fen is not going to divide one into the other and is not going to pretend that four is not four.**

> Entered at the ninth hour of the evening of the seventeenth of this month, in the seed house, by the clerk, on the standing of a count and not of a measurement: **one pail, of this holding's, brought back filled from the north row this morning and carried to the other two places and brought back from them, and the same pail at all three. The person keeping the time states that a pail is not a measure, that a count of strokes is a count of strokes, and that these three figures are three counts and not a rate and not a head and not a yield.**
>
> **One. This holding's own head, at about the seventh hour of the morning, at the north row: ninety-four.**
>
> **Two. The low quarter, at about the sixth hour of the morning, at a well in that quarter with water in it: a hundred and fifty-one.**
>
> **Three. Carried to the third place on the eighteenth, at about the second hour of the afternoon, at the Pans well on the dry road, eleven miles: two hundred and nine.**

The engineer of record did the differences out loud in a yard of about nine people the same evening, and he did them the way he has done every difference in this book for two years, which is by naming the two figures before the result and saying that the result is only a third number and not a fourth fact.

"**Ninety-four, a hundred and fifty-one, two hundred and nine.** So the low quarter's own well is fifty-seven strokes slower than this holding's head for the same pail, and the Pans well is a hundred and fifteen strokes slower than this holding's head, and the Pans well is fifty-eight strokes slower than a well in a quarter nine miles off that has four wells off around it.**"

"**And here is the arithmetic I want in the book and I want it in the ordinary words, because the ordinary words are the ones that cost something. Two hundred and nine against ninety-four is two and a bit times. It is not two and a quarter times. Two and a quarter times ninety-four is two hundred and eleven and a half, which is a number this body does not have, and I am not going to stand in a yard and round a figure to make it tidy, and I would like the clerk to enter that I did not.**"

"So: **a man is hauling water eleven miles out of a well that gives a pail more slowly than the well in the quarter he is hauling it into, and about a fifth slower than this holding's own head, and he is charging a shilling a pail, and the man who charges a shilling a pail is not cheating anybody, because that is what the road is and there is not one other thing there is.**"

And then Jonas Byre said the thing that the four of them had been talking about since the sixth hour of the morning, and he said it in the yard and it was a sentence about his own cough and not about anybody else.

"**I have been in that quarter thirty-one years counting a wheel in a yard and I know what a slow well is. I do not know what a fast one is. In that quarter this morning the well they were drawing off was slow and the four that are off are off, and I stood there and I timed the pail myself with the examiner's watch and I got a hundred and fifty-one and I got it twice.**"

"And here is what I have been chewing on since the sixth hour and I will say it in front of nine people because I am forty-eight and I am not going to be a man who finds out late. **In that quarter, the well that is on is slower than this holding's own head. And this holding's own head has been on a leak of five-eighths of an inch since before the flood and has a beat in it that has not been logged since and and there was a draw out of it on the twelfth of last month at the fourth hour of the morning that this holding is still paying for in sixteen households' water, and I have been in this yard every one of those days.**"

"So I do not know what I have got and I am not going to say the rest of that sentence in this yard, because if I say the rest of it somebody will write it down and in four years it will be a thing this body knew. **What I am saying is that the two numbers I have just given you do not fit any story I have been told in eleven years, and the one they do fit is that the water under that quarter was never as good as anybody in that quarter believed and somebody has been selling them the difference for a season.**"

Nobody wrote that down. The reader of this body asked that it be entered and the clerk entered that the sentence was made and entered that it was not entered as a finding and entered why, **and the reason is that a man who has timed a pail twice in a quarter nine miles off has established a count and has not established where the water came from, and the difference between those two is the whole of the last two volumes of this book and this body has been on the wrong side of it more than once.**

Then the reader of this body stood up and said the other half, and he said it slowly, and about nine people were in that yard and one of them was a woman of a household from the low quarter who had walked nine miles again and had not asked to be fed and was not fed.

"**Then I am going to say the thing this yard has been not saying since the eleventh, and I am going to say it in the plainest words I have got and I am not going to be thanked for it.**"

"**A man is not stealing from a quarter. He is emptying a well, and there is a difference between those two that this holding has spent a season learning and has not yet learned well.** Taking a thing that belongs to somebody is theft and everybody in this yard knows what to do about it. **Drawing a thing out of a place until the place stops, and charging by the pail, and being a man with a cart and not a man with a licence, is a supply, and a supply is a thing the law has an opinion about and the law is nine miles away in a building and does not live in a yard.**"

"And that is the whole of the difference and it is why this holding is not going to be a body that says he is a thief in a yard. **Because if we say he is a thief then the next thing anybody in that quarter will say is that the well was always ours, and the well was not always ours, and there is a charter on it and the charter is somebody's and the somebody is Garrow Nye, of no address this office holds.**"

"So the question in this yard on the eighteenth of this month is not whether a man is honest. He is honest. **The question is whether a quarter of four hundred and forty-one people can be told the truth about where their water comes from by a body that has no charter, no standing, four pounds and fivepence, and three counts of a pail in its own hand.** And I do not know the answer and I am not going to pretend to nine people that I do, **and I want the count of times this holding has been asked a question in a yard and has not got the answer entered, and that count is two, and one of the two is four days old and one of the two is now.**"

Nobody answered him. Nobody was asked to answer him, and the fen entered that nobody was asked, and entered that the reader asked for his initial to be put against nothing and it went in and the count of that is seventeen in a season.

And then, at about the tenth hour of the evening of the eighteenth, with the sheet of the three counts on the bench, the engineer of record wrote the forty-third note under section nine of the charter schedule, and there is no charter, and the note says so on its face, and it was read back before it was initialled.

*Three counts of one pail, one pail, at three places, on the seventeenth and the eighteenth of this month, kept by the examiner and not rounded and not converted. Ninety-four at this holding's own head, a hundred and fifty-one in a well in the low quarter nine miles off, two hundred and nine at a well on a dry road eleven miles off that belongs under a charter to a man nobody in this yard has met. Fifty-seven strokes and a hundred and fifteen strokes, and the two differences stated with the two figures before them and not after them, and two and a bit times and not two and a quarter, because two and a quarter times ninety-four is two hundred and eleven and a half and this body does not have that number. A man from the north row with the cough, who has counted a mill wheel for thirty-one years, said in this yard that the two numbers do not fit any story he has been told in eleven years, and that the one they do fit is that the water under that quarter was never as good as anybody in it believed and that somebody has been selling them the difference for a season, and it was entered as a sentence made and not as a finding, and the reason is entered with it. The reader of this body said that a man emptying a well and charging by the pail is a supply and is not a theft, and that the difference between those two is the whole of the last two volumes of this book and that this holding has been on the wrong side of it more than once, and that the question in this yard is not whether the man is honest and is whether a body with no charter can tell the truth about where a quarter's water comes from. Nobody answered him. Nobody was asked. The count of questions asked in a yard and not got is two. A pail is not a measure and the three figures are counts and not a rate and not a head and not a yield, and the examiner said that in front of about nine people before any of it was written down.*

*An argument. This is worth nothing against a cut.*
