# Chapter 190

## Three Places And Three Numbers

The twenty-seventh of this month was a Thursday, and at about the fourth hour of the morning the engineer of record, the examiner, and two men of this holding's crew went out onto the pan with one pail, a stopwatch, a rod eight feet long with a line and a weight, and a sheet of the same paper the plan is on, and the plan was in the engineer's coat because he was not going to leave it in a cart shed for a third month.

The pan is a floor of salt and clay and it is harder than a road and softer than a field and it rings under a boot heel in a particular way that about nine of the people who have stood on it have described in three different words. It goes about nine hundred yards by two hundred at the low place. There is no holder of it. It is on ground the district calls Crown-held and has never held, and the district has never been asked, and a man with a spade who has not spoken to the other three since the seventeenth of last month cut a sod off it in a month three months back and took it home for his own floor, and nobody asked him to and nobody asked him not to.

They put the pail in at three places, at the top of each of them, and the examiner kept the time and said out loud, before anything was called, that she keeps time and does not round, and the man of about fifty wrote the numbers down as they were called and did not round them, and the first two of them were two hundred yards apart because the rod is eight feet and they measured with the rod and then walked it, and the third was a further two hundred yards on the bearing the plan gives, which is north-east.

A hundred and forty-six seconds at the first. Two hundred and two at the second. And a hundred and eighty-eight at the third.

The engineer of record had the numbers in his hand before the pail was dry and he read them out loud in the yard at about the ninth hour of the evening, in about nine people, with the sheet, and he said the third one out loud twice because he wanted it heard twice.

"**A hundred and forty-six. Two hundred and two. A hundred and eighty-eight.**"

"**The third one is faster than the second one and I want that said before anybody has done anything with these numbers.**"

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything.

"**Water going slower is water going uphill. That is not a figure of mine, that is what a pail does. And the ground is not flat and the second place is the top of it, and the second place is two hundred yards from the third, and the third is where the plan says the corridor comes down to.**"

"**So the plan is wrong in the second half mile. The plan has the corridor running down to the second place and then going north-east along a line, and the ground between the second and the third is going the other way, and I drew that line at the ninth hour of the evening of the twenty-ninth of a month two months back without standing on the ground at all.**"

The man of about fifty said the part about the sheet, and he was not thanked.

"**Then the sheet is a record of what you thought, and not a record of the ground, and you wrote the hour on it, and that is worse than not writing it down.**"

"**No.**"

The reader of this body said it.

"**The sheet is a record of what I thought with the hour on it, and a person in four years can look at the hour and at the ground and see that I was wrong at that hour about that ground. If I had not written it down there would be nothing to be wrong about except me, and me is a thing I have to carry and a sheet is a thing I do not.**"

Then the sums, out loud, in the yard, twice, and the two agreeing, and the woman who does the sums had come out to the gate for this and had said so.

"**One thousand and forty-five hundredweight went through that licensed draw in eleven days. One thousand and forty-five over eleven is ninety-five a day, and eleven times ninety-five is one thousand and forty-five, and the two agree.**"

"**Now. A corridor of about two inches over nine hundred yards and forty yards wide. I am going to write the number down and say the working, because a number nobody can check is a word, and a word is what this yard has been arguing about for a month.**"

She wrote a figure on the back of the engineer's own sheet, which he allowed, and read it out.

"**Nine hundred yards by forty yards is thirty-six thousand square yards, and two inches is a yard in eighteen, and that is two thousand yards of water. A yard of water is twenty-seven feet at sixty-two and a half pounds to the foot, and that is a thousand six hundred and eighty-seven pounds and a half, and a hundredweight is a hundred and twelve pounds, and two thousand of them is about thirty thousand hundredweight.**"

"**And the other way, because I do sums both ways. Two thousand yards is fifty-four thousand feet, and that is three million three hundred and seventy-five thousand pounds, and at a hundred and twelve the hundredweight that is about thirty thousand hundredweight. The two agree.**"

"**And that is not a figure anybody in this yard can use, and nobody is going to improve on it, and it is on the sheet because a plan with a number on it is a plan somebody can disagree with, and a plan with no number on it is a story.**"

"**And I am not going to compare it with the ninety-five a day, because the ninety-five a day is a licensed draw in a holder's ground and the thirty thousand is this holding's own figure about a pan that belongs to nobody, and the two are not the same and adding them is the same as adding a supply to a want.**"

Then the second thing, which is the one that decides the chapter, and the engineer of record said it standing up in a yard of about nine and the examiner was there and two of the crew were there and it is the first time in two months that he has said a whole sentence about the rule.

"**Where the water comes from. Not the Pans well. It cannot be the Pans well, and I am not going to say that man's name in this yard today, and the reason I am not going to is a thing I said four times in a yard and I am not going to say a fifth.**"

"**The reason is that the draw at the Pans well is the only water going into a quarter that has four wells off, and if this holding takes a fifth of that water for a corridor on ground that belongs to nobody, then the nine miles of people between here and that water are drinking a fifth less, and this holding does not know what that is to them and has said so in a yard and will say so again.**"

"**So it has to come out of this holding's own head. And opening this holding's own head is a licensed act under a form and at a rate with a licensed man standing there with a book, and it is not a draw, and it does not touch living rootwork, and no living thing is cut and no seed and no bead and nothing living is touched at all.**"

"**Which is why the four lines of our rule are not used and why the count of the uses of that rule stays at two. That rule is about a draw. A gate is not a draw. I have spent two months and a rule about a stop on a thing this holding was never going to do, and I found that out on a Thursday morning in a salt pan with a stopwatch, and I am not going to make a better sentence out of it than that one.**"

> Entered at the ninth hour of the evening of the twenty-seventh of this month, in the yard, by the clerk, on the standing of a measurement taken on ground with no holder: **one pail was timed at three places on the pan at the head of the dry flats, at the fourth hour of the morning of this day, by the examiner keeping the time, the numbers being one hundred and forty-six seconds at the first place, two hundred and two at the second and a hundred and eighty-eight at the third. The third is faster than the second and the fen entered that the ground is not flat and the second place is the top of it and the plan of the twenty-ninth of a month two months back is wrong in the second half mile. The plan stands in the book with the hour on it and was not torn up. The licensed draw at the Pans well is one thousand and forty-five hundredweight in eleven days, derived twice, the two agreeing, and the engineer of record entered that the water for the corridor is not to come from that draw and is to come from this holding's own head under a licence and a rate, and entered that the standing rule is a rule about a draw and is not used and that the count of the uses of that rule is two and stands at two.**

Nobody thanked anybody. The man of the north row with the cough asked one question and it was asked in the ordinary voice and it was the first question anybody had asked in that yard for about a quarter of an hour.

"**Then what is it for.**"

And the engineer of this body said it in about four sentences and he did not say it to the man.

"**Nine hundred yards of dry salt between a quarter with four wells off and everybody behind it. A person cannot cross that in a season carrying what they are carrying and a cart cannot cross it at all, and a road on it would be a road that goes nowhere in a wet month.**"

"**Water in a corridor is not a road. It is a road for about as long as the water lasts, and I do not know how long that is, and that is the second thing on the sheet I have not been able to finish.**"

"**And I have a sheet with a corridor on it that I drew without standing on the ground, and I have just been out on the ground, and the ground does not have my corridor in it. So the plan is finished and the work has not started and those are two different days and I have written the first one down and I am not going to write the second one down until somebody has walked it.**"

On the twenty-eighth, the Friday, they went out again with the rod and the line and did not take the pail, and the engineer of record wanted a level and not a time, and the difference between those two things is the whole of what he had got wrong and he said so in the yard that evening.

"**A pail tells you where the water does not want to go. A rod tells you which way it is uphill. I had one of them on the twenty-ninth of a month two months back and I did not have either, and I drew a line anyway.**"

They ran a line two hundred yards at the second place and the rise in it was eleven feet, twice, at two bearings, and the examiner wrote both numbers and the man of about fifty read them back, and a rise of eleven feet in two hundred yards is about one in fifty-five, and the engineer of record said that aloud and then said what it meant.

"**One in fifty-five is not a difficulty. One in fifty-five is a slope. A man can walk one in fifty-five. What I could not walk is one in fifty-five for a mile and a half, and that is what I drew, and I drew it from a window.**"

The man of about seventy was not in any of this. The man of the north row with the cough was, and on the Friday he carried the rod two hundred yards and came back and put it down and said the thing about the salt that the man with a spade proved in a month three months back when he cut a sod off it and took it home for his own floor.

"**The sod went for his floor and his floor has not washed. That is the only thing anybody knows about this ground and it is known by a man who took a piece of it home without being asked.**"

The engineer of record said the arithmetic of that out loud in about nine people and did not improve it.

"**Then the pan has a crust and the crust is what is holding it and a crust is not a floor and it is not a channel and it is not a road. If I put nine inches of water on it in one place it will not go where I drew. It will go where the crust is thinnest, and I have no instrument for where the crust is thinnest, and the man with a spade has a spade and has not spoken to the other three since the seventeenth of last month and I am not going to go and ask him.**"

And that was entered too, with the reason, and the reason is the same sentence it has been for a month, and the fen entered that the sentence is the same sentence and that a sentence that keeps being the right one is a rule nobody wrote down, which is a thing the reader of this body said in a yard in the ninth month of this year about a different sentence, and that he is aware of it and is not going to do anything about it this month.

On the twenty-ninth, the Saturday, the crew cut a trial channel at the first place, four feet long and a hand wide and a hand deep, with a mattock, because a mattock on salt clay is a thing that can be done by a man in a morning and a pick is not, and the engineer of record was at the top of the slope and did not go down, and the reason was given out loud before anybody asked and it is the reason from the twenty-seventh of a month two months back and the count of times he has given it is two.

"**A body that stands over a crew is a body the crew talks to. This holding has four people on a floor and no custom for it and I was told that on the nineteenth of last month and I have not improved on it since and I am not going to start on a channel four feet long.**"

The channel held about an inch of water for about an hour and a half and then the salt came up through the bottom of it and it went, and nobody said that was a failure, and the clerk entered what happened with the hour on it, and the engineer of record read it back out loud in a yard of about nine on the Saturday evening and said the thing about it before anybody could make it into a lesson.

"**That is the first measurement of a channel on that ground and it is on this page with the hour on it. It is one inch for an hour and a half. If somebody writes that down as a result I will read it out again and I will say that it is a man with a mattock and a hand's depth of water and a floor that is not a floor, and that the number is the number and the sentence about the number is mine and not the ground's.**"

> Entered at the seventh hour of the evening of the twenty-ninth of this month, in the yard, by the clerk, on the standing of a trial on ground with no holder: **a channel four feet long, a hand wide and a hand deep, was cut at the first place on the pan at the second hour of the afternoon of this day with a mattock and held about an inch of water for about an hour and a half, and the salt came up through the bottom of it. A line run two hundred yards at the second place on the twenty-eighth gave a rise of eleven feet twice at two bearings, being one in fifty-five. The engineer of record was at the top of the slope and did not go down and entered the reason before he was asked. Nobody said the trial was a failure and the fen entered that nobody said it and that the number is a number and the sentence about the number is his.**

And so the twenty-ninth of this month went down with a plan that is wrong in its second half mile, a rise of one in fifty-five, a channel that held an inch for an hour and a half, a sentence about a rule this holding was never going to use, and about four hundred people from the migration on the far side of nine hundred yards of dry salt who are not going to be on the near side of it tomorrow either, and nobody thanked anybody, and the sheet is still in the engineer's coat and the corridor on it is still in the wrong place, and it is a record of what a man thought at the ninth hour of the evening of the twenty-ninth of a month two months back, with the hour on it, and it is not torn up.
