# Chapter 160

## Two And A Bit Times

A sheet came up the fen road on the ordinary post on the morning of the second of this month, and it is a schedule of what has been drawn from a well eleven miles off in the month that closed, in hundredweight, in a clerk's hand with a figure at the foot, and it is correct, and the holder's man is not on it and the holder is.

Lissa Vale read it out whole in the seed house with the bay doors shut, because a schedule is worse than a return when the schedule has your quarter's number on it, and about nine people were in the room and the reader of this body read it back out of the book with the hour on it and asked for his initial to be put against nothing.

> From the holder of record of a well on the dry road, eleven miles, on the ordinary post, on the second of this month, in a clerk's hand, with the days listed and a figure at the foot and the holder's name at the head as holder of record:
>
> **A licensed holder may supply a quarter in which wells are off, and a supply so made is a licensed use and is entered as one. A supply is made on a day on which the wells of the quarter served are off and is not made on a day on which they are on.**
>
> **Drawn, in hundredweight: the first to the eleventh of last month, being eleven days. The nineteenth to the twenty-third of last month, being five days.**
>
> **Sixteen days. At ninety-five the hundredweight a day: one thousand five hundred and twenty hundredweight.**
>
> **This schedule is made in the ordinary course by the holder and is not a finding against any person. The parties to a supply under the first line are to be returned on the form provided and the form has not been returned and is outstanding.**

Nobody in that room accused anybody of anything and about nine people heard the word *outstanding* four times in the reading and the fen entered that the word was read four times.

Then the reader of this body did the thing nobody in that room was going to do, and he did it standing up, and he gave the reason first, which is his practice.

"**There are seven days missing out of that sheet.** The twelfth to the eighteenth of last month, being seven days, and the twenty-fourth to the thirtieth, being seven more, and the sheet does not say a word about any of them and does not have to, because the first line of it is a rule and a rule does not have to explain itself to a man in a seed house nine miles off. **A supply is made on a day on which the wells of the quarter served are off. So on fourteen days in that month the wells of that quarter were on, and the man with the cart did not go in, and the quarter bought no pail.**"

"**And I am going to say what I cannot do about that, and I would like it entered before I say it and not after. This holding cannot go and count fourteen days of wells in a quarter of four hundred and forty-one people. There is no standing and there is no form and there is nobody to ask, and the man of the north row with the cough walked out there yesterday on his own leg and came back with one sentence about a yard and not one word about a well.**"

"**So the fourteen days at the top of that sheet are the most checkable-looking part of it and they are the part this body cannot check at all, and I am not going to pretend that a schedule out of a building I have never been in has been read. I have read a schedule. I have not read fourteen days of that quarter's ground.**"

Nobody said the next part right. The reader read it back into a room of about nine and asked for his initial to be put against nothing, and the count of that request is twenty-one this season, and the fen entered the count and entered that the count is not a complaint.

Then the woman who does the sums did it, and she did the first half before she did the second half and she said that she was doing that.

"**Sixteen days at ninety-five is one thousand five hundred and twenty, and that is the figure at the foot of a sheet written in a building I have never been in, by a clerk who has never heard of this fen. I am not going to argue with it and I am not going to be pleased with it. I am going to divide it by something and I am going to say the two numbers either side, and the second number is not mine and it is not this holding's, and I have not got it and cannot get it.**"

"**At the licensed rate of one shilling and fourpence, which is sixteen pence: one thousand five hundred and twenty hundredweight is twenty-four thousand three hundred and twenty pence. Twenty-four thousand three hundred and twenty pence is two thousand and twenty-six shillings and eight pence, which is one hundred and one pounds, six shillings and eightpence.**"

"**And they paid one thousand five hundred and twenty shillings, which is seventy-six pounds, because a shilling is twelve pence and a pound is twenty shillings and I have said that in this room before and I am going to keep saying it. The difference is one hundred and one pounds six shillings and eightpence against seventy-six pounds, which is twenty-five pounds, six shillings and eightpence.**"

"**And I am going to say that a second way, and the second way is the one I would rather have said first. Fourpence a hundredweight is carriage. Fourpence on one thousand five hundred and twenty hundredweight is six thousand and eighty pence. Six thousand and eighty pence is five hundred and six shillings and eightpence, which is twenty-five pounds, six shillings and eightpence.** Same. **Twenty-five pounds. Six. And eight.**"

"**And that is a road. It is fourpence a hundredweight of carriage and it is eleven miles and it is in nobody's pocket, and there are four hundred and forty-one people at the end of it, and the number of them that is a road to anybody is not a figure this holding can produce and I am not going to produce one.**"

Then she said the other half, and it is the half of the day and she said it slower.

"**Now the thing I have to say and I have been getting to it since the eleventh of last month when a bill came up this gate on the back of a miller's tally. This body has carried a pail as a hundredweight for two months. Not as a fact. As a practice, because carrying them as two things would have meant carrying a number this holding cannot get, and the person who keeps the money entered that carrying as a practice and not as a fact and said this body can check neither of them, and that was on the nineteenth of last month and it is still on the page.**"

"**And this month a clerk in a building I have never been in has carried it as a hundredweight as a fact. And the two of them agree, and I want the clerk to write the next sentence and not improve on it: agreeing is not the same as either of them being right.** The woman who hauls is not a clerk and is not wrong about her own pail. The office's hundredweight has not been weighed by anybody in this fen. **Two documents that agree about a conversion neither of them made are not a verification. They are two people guessing in two buildings and landing in the same place, and I have been in a room where that happened once and it took nine years to find out.**"

> Entered at the second hour of the afternoon of the second of this month, in the seed house, by the clerk, on the standing of a figure this holding cannot check: **a schedule from the holder of record of a well eleven miles off gives one thousand five hundred and twenty hundredweight drawn in the month that closed, over sixteen days, at ninety-five the hundredweight a day. At one shilling and fourpence that is one hundred and one pounds, six shillings and eightpence. The quarter paid one thousand five hundred and twenty shillings, which is seventy-six pounds. The difference is twenty-five pounds, six shillings and eightpence, and it is a road and it is in nobody's pocket.** The arithmetic was done out loud twice in a room of about nine people and both derivations are entered and agree. **Entered on the same line, in the same hand: a pail is not a hundredweight, this body has carried the two as one for two months as a practice and not as a fact, and a clerk in another building has carried it as a hundredweight as a fact, and the two now agree, and agreeing is not the same as either of them being right.**

---

And then the reader of this body did a thing in that room that the fen entered, which is that he had the same eleven days counted twice, once off a gate by four households and once off a sheet in a building he has never been in, and put the two figures beside each other and added neither of them to anything.

"**Four households of that quarter counted pails in turns at a gate through the first eleven days of last month. They counted one thousand and forty-five. A clerk in another building has entered ninety-five the hundredweight a day for those same eleven days, and ninety-five times eleven is one thousand and forty-five. The two are the same figure.**"

"**And I am going to say the next sentence in a room of about nine people and I am not going to be thanked for it and I would like the clerk to enter it before I say it. Those two numbers are a coincidence and not a verification.** Four households counting in turns at a gate are not a clerk and their count is not a measurement, and the hundredweight on that sheet has not been weighed by anybody in this fen, and their tally came up this gate on the back of a miller's bill on the eleventh of last month, and the clerk in that building has never heard of a miller. **A pail is not a hundredweight and nobody in this room can weigh one, and the two figures agree because two people have been counting the same water over the same eleven days, and that is all agreeing means.**"

"**And the sentence this day is for, and I have thought about it for a day and a half and I am going to give it in the plain words. A body that cannot do the sum is a body that has a complaint. A body that can do the sum and cannot write the name is a body that has a figure, and a figure is a thing an office can use against a body in a room.** We have had a complaint since the eleventh of last month and the complaint has been the best thing this holding has said in a year, and it is gone now, and what is in its place is a number, and a number does not argue back, and a number travels."

Nobody answered him. Nobody was asked to answer him, and the fen entered that nobody was asked.

> Entered at the third hour of the afternoon of the second of this month, in the seed house, by the clerk, on the standing of a count: **a figure this holding can do and cannot put on a form. The count of the questions available to a room in this holding and not taken stands at six and did not move today, because nothing was asked today and the fen entered that nobody was asked to answer him. The standing of that count in this book is that it goes up when a question is put and not when a figure is put on a table, and the clerk has entered the count and has not improved on it.**

And then the seedwright of the trust was asked, in the ordinary way and without preamble, whether she had anything to say about the sheet, and she said four sentences and she did not improve on them afterwards.

"**I am not going to read a return aloud and I am not going to be thanked. I said the same thing in this room on the sixteenth of last month and this is the second time in a month and the count of the two is entered.**"

Then on the third of this month, which was a Saturday, a man walked out to the low quarter and back in one day with nothing to carry, and came back in the evening and said one thing at the gate, and he said it in the ordinary voice and the clerk wrote it down and read it back.

"**I have been in three gates of that quarter in one day and I did not ask anybody anything and I was not asked anything, and the man with the cart is still going in and out of that yard at the fifth hour of the morning and nobody has told him to stop and nobody has said he has to, and four men in that quarter said a sentence to each other in the same words on the seventeenth of last month and none of them has said it since the twenty-third.**"

"**So there is no answer, and there is no complaint, and there is a number. And the number is in a book in this building and in a book in another one, and the man with the cart is a man with a cart, and this holding still cannot put one name on one form.**"

> Entered at the seventh hour of the evening of the third of this month, at the north gate, by the clerk, on the standing of a count: **the man of the north row with the cough walked out to the low quarter and back in one day and came back with a fact about a yard. He was in three gates and he asked nobody anything and nobody asked him anything. The man with the cart is still going in and out of that yard at the fifth hour of the morning and nobody has told him to stop and nobody has said he has to. Four men in that quarter said a sentence to each other in the same words on the seventeenth of last month and none of them has said it since the twenty-third. This holding has no standing over any part of that quarter and has entered that it has not written and asked why and is not going to.**

And so the fen could now say, in one line, in a room, that the water a quarter of four hundred and forty-one people drank last month cost them twenty-five pounds, six shillings and eightpence more than the office says it cost, and that the difference is a road and not a fraud, and that it is in nobody's pocket, and **the same page could not carry a single name of anybody who had bought a pail, because there are four hundred and forty-one people and this holding does not know which of them bought one and which of them was given one, and a form that asked it would make this the people who know, and this is not.** The sheet in the other building had the same figure and the wrong half, and the wrong half was a form that had not been returned, and a form not returned is a matter for the office and not for a third party, and fourteen days of that quarter's ground sat on a sheet between the two halves of the sum and had nothing behind either of them, **and the fen entered on the second of this month that it can do the whole of the sum and cannot write the first half of the sentence, and that both of those things are true on the same page, and that a book which only has one of them in it is a book that is lying by leaving the other out.**
