# Chapter 169

## What A Holder May Do

The seventeenth of this month was a Saturday, and the ordinary post came up the fen road at about the tenth hour of the morning in a leather bag with a carrier in it who went on up the road afterwards and is not in this book's book, and about eleven people were in the yard when it was opened because it is a Saturday and it is the day the post comes.

What was in the bag was four lines on one sheet, and it was not a schedule, and **the reader of this body entered in the room before anybody read it that it is the first thing a holder of record has ever sent into this book that was not a schedule, and entered that a body which has spent a month reading a holder's paper is about to read four lines that are not paper about water at all.**

> Return made under a charter of supply. Made on the seventeenth of this month by the holder of record of the Pans well, on his own paper, and sent up the fen road by the ordinary post at the ordinary cost of that post.
>
> The holder states what he is going to do under his own charter, on his own ground, at his own cost. He is going to take his own well apart and put it back together.
>
> The work begins on the twentieth of this month. The holder gives the day and the hour and does not give a figure. The reason he gives for not giving a figure is that a cost entered is a cost that anybody may read.
>
> This return asks no person for any form, no certificate and no answer, and no part of it is a finding against any person.

The form on the bench in the seed house was not looked at by anybody in that yard on that morning, and it was not returned, and it was not torn up, and nobody in that yard mentioned it to anybody, and the fen entered all four of those separately and then entered the reason for the fourth of them, which is the whole reason and takes one sentence: **a body that has already said it will not put its name to a form and will not refuse to either does not get to change its mind on a Sunday because a piece of paper came in the post, and the difference between not knowing and refusing has been the whole argument of this book for a year.**

Then the person who keeps the seed house stood up and said what the sheet was, before anybody read it, and she said it in about two sentences, and the reader appointed was the man of about fifty, and he read it back out of the bag in the ordinary voice with the hour on it, and the clerk entered it, and nobody thanked anybody.

"**It is a return. It is four lines and it is correct and it is not about a person. He is telling us what he is going to do and he has told us the day he is going to do it and he has not told us what it costs him because he does not want us to know.**"

"**And the thing that is on the face of it and that everybody in this yard can read without me saying a word is the last line. He has not asked us for anything. In a month, that is the first sheet that has come into this building that has not wanted something.**"

And then he said the thing the volume's frame needs said once more, in a yard of about eleven, standing up, and he gave the reason first.

"**A charter holder is a supply and is not a thief, and I have said that in this yard three times in a month and I am going to say it a fourth time and then I am going to stop saying it, because a thing said four times is a rule and a rule that nobody wrote down is the worst kind.**"

"**And here is what that man has done, and nobody in this yard has to do anything about it. A holder who takes his own well apart and puts it back together at his own cost on his own ground under his own charter has not become anything else by doing it. He has not become a good man. He has not become one of us. He has not done it for this holding and he has not said one word to this holding about it.**"

"**And I cannot thank him, and I am not going to stand in this yard and pretend to a person in it that not being able to thank a man is the same as not owing him something. Those are two different sentences and I have only got one of them and the one I have got is the first.**"

And then about four minutes later, in the same yard of about eleven, one of them said the word.

He said it out loud, and he said it to nobody in particular and about a man nine miles off whose return was in a bag on the table, and what he said was **thank you**, and the reader of this body had about four seconds to decide in front of eleven people whether this holding corrects its own yard.

Then Dunmar Fitch entered what he decided and the reason, and read it back, and did not improve on it.

> Entered at the eleventh hour of the morning of the seventeenth of this month, in the yard, by the clerk, on the standing of a decision: **a person in a yard of about eleven said the word thank you, out loud, about a holder of record whose return had come in the bag, and the reader of this body was asked by nobody to do anything about it and considered in front of eleven people whether this holding corrects its own yard.**
>
> **It does not, and the reason is in two lines and it stands. The man did not do it for this holding. A thing a man does to himself is not owed a word by a yard, and a yard that says a man's name out loud on a morning like this one is a yard that has taken a thing done to a man nine miles off and made it into a thing done to us, and in about four years there will be four hundred and forty-one people in a quarter nine miles off who have been told that a body of sixteen households with no charter said a holder's name in a yard and meant it kindly.**
>
> **And entered on the same line: about four people in that yard are owed something by a man nine miles off. This holding cannot say what and will not guess, and has not written and asked, and the count of four is a count of a reader's belief and not a count of debts and the fen entered that difference and is not going to improve on it.**

Then the fen entered the other half of that day, and it entered it on purpose and it said so on the page.

> Entered at the twelfth hour of the noon of the seventeenth of this month, in the seed house, by the clerk, on the standing of a count: **the count of the things this holding has been told no by this season stands at six. This is not one of them. Nobody said no to anybody in that yard this morning and nobody said yes to anybody either. The fen entered that it is not one of them on purpose, and entered the reason, which is that a thing a man does to himself is not a refusal and must not be counted as one, and which is the same reason the fen entered on the twenty-second of last month in a different yard about a different man, and the two are the same sentence and there have been two of them and there is not going to be a third without a form.**

---

And on the eighteenth, which was a Sunday, the seed house had the bay doors shut and about eleven people in it, and the woman who does the sums had been sitting with four lines on a sheet and a sheet of her own since the Friday, and she said the word before the figure, and she said it in the room and not in the yard, and nobody thanked her and she did not ask.

"**I am going to say what I can do and then I am going to say the number, and the number is four, and I have been sitting with it since Friday and I am not glad about any of the four and I am not going to pretend to be glad in a room with eleven people in it.**"

"**One. The rate. One shilling and fourpence the hundredweight, which is sixteen pence, off a return out of a sub-office nine miles off. I can do that and I have done it four times this month and it does not change.**"

"**Two. The days. It is the seventeenth and he says the twentieth, and that is three days, and the twentieth is a Tuesday. I can do that and the woman who keeps time has told me twice this week that she keeps time and does not round, and I am not going to be the person who rounds a date in front of her.**"

"**Three. The licensed sum. One thousand five hundred and twenty hundredweight at one shilling and fourpence, being sixteen pence, is twenty-four thousand three hundred and twenty pence, which is two thousand and twenty-six shillings and eightpence, which is one hundred and one pounds six shillings and eightpence. And I am going to do it the other way because I have been doing sums one way since I was a girl. Fourpence a hundredweight on one thousand five hundred and twenty is six thousand and eighty pence, which is five hundred and six shillings and eightpence, which is twenty-five pounds six shillings and eightpence, and that quarter paid one thousand five hundred and twenty shillings, which is seventy-six pounds, and seventy-six pounds and twenty-five pounds six shillings and eightpence is one hundred and one pounds six shillings and eightpence.**"

"**So the two agree. That is the first time in nine months that two of my derivations have agreed with each other on the first go and I am not going to be pleased about it in a room, because two sums that agree is a thing about arithmetic and not about a quarter of four hundred and forty-one people.**"

"**Four. The gate count. One thousand and forty-five pails through the first eleven days of last month, counted by four households in turns, and it is a count a household could make at a gate. And it is not the sixteen-day figure and may not be put beside it, and I have known that for a month and I am writing it down this morning so that a person in four years does not have to be told.**"

And then she said the thing she had been sitting with, and about eleven people were in that room and the clerk was one of the eleven and wrote all of it.

"**And there is a fifth thing I cannot do, and I am going to say the word for it and I am not going to write the fifth line down. A repair of that well is at that man's cost and he has not entered the cost and is not going to, and there is not one figure in this room that is his cost, and this holding cannot compute a cost eleven miles off and is not going to be told one by a return.**"

"**So: four. And here is what I cannot do with the four, and I am saying it in a room and I would like it in the book. Every one of those four is a figure this holding can hand to an office. That is a new and unwanted property of a figure. A figure used to be a complaint. A complaint is a thing you say out loud to somebody who can do something about it. A figure is a thing that travels, and it travels on its own, and nobody has to carry it anywhere, and I do not have a column in this book for a figure I am glad that I can check, and I am not going to build one.**"

> Entered at the second hour of the afternoon of the eighteenth of this month, in the seed house, by the clerk, on the standing of a sum: **the woman who does the sums entered the number of things this holding can compute in the four lines of a holder's return, being four, and named all four, and derived the licensed sum twice out loud and the two derivations agree, being one hundred and one pounds six shillings and eightpence against seventy-six pounds paid, and entered that a gate count of one thousand and forty-five pails over eleven days of last month is not the sixteen-day figure and may not be put beside it.** She did not enter a fifth thing and said why, which is that a repair eleven miles off is at the holder's cost and that this holding cannot compute a cost eleven miles off.
>
> **And entered on the same line, in her own words, read back: every one of the four is a figure this holding can hand to an office, and that is a new and unwanted property of a figure, and a figure used to be a complaint and is now a thing that travels, and that she does not have a column for a figure she is glad she can check and is not going to build one. Nobody thanked her and she did not ask to be thanked. The reader of this body did not improve on it and was not asked to.**

Then the examiner did the only thing she does and she did it in front of everybody in the room, which is to take the second derivation and go through it a third time with her finger on the paper, and she said out loud before she started that she keeps time and does not round, and the two of them agreed, and the reader of this body asked one question and got one answer and did not ask a second.

"**What does it cost him.**"

"**I do not know, and I am not going to guess at it in a room with eleven people in it, and I have written the fifth thing in my own book and not in this one, and if a person in four years wants to know what that man spent on his own ground on his own money then the only honest answer anybody can give them is that the man did not write it down and would not.**"

Nobody said no to anybody in that room and nobody said yes, and the man of about fifty asked for his initial to be put against nothing and it went in, and the count of that request is thirty this season, and the fen entered the count and entered that the count is not a complaint.

And so the seventeenth and the eighteenth of this month went down, and a man eleven miles off has a day and an hour and a piece of his own ground to be on, and about four hundred and forty-one people at the end of an eleven-mile road do not know yet, and a body of sixteen households with no charter has entered four figures it can hand to an office and has not entered one it cannot, **and the form is on the bench, and the day the answer is given on that form a holder of record will have an answer from a body with no charter, and the fen entered that it knows the date of nothing and is not going to guess at a date.**
