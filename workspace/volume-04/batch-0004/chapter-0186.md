# Chapter 186

## What The Road Is For

The fourteenth and the fifteenth of this month were a Friday and a Saturday, and the fen took stock in the ordinary way at about the eleventh hour in a yard of about nine people, and the man of the north row with the cough went out to the low quarter at about the fifth hour of the morning on the Friday and came back at about the seventh hour of the evening and came into that yard without sitting down and had two things, and he asked nobody anything about either of them, **and the fen entered that he did not and entered that nobody tried to make him and entered that on the twenty-eighth of last month the same man did the same walk and had two things, and the fen entered that too and did not improve on either.**

The first thing was about that quarter's water, and it is a thing that has been in this book for a month, and he gave it in about four sentences and in the plainest words he has.

"**The pails have got longer and the walks have not. That is the whole of what I have got and I have been up and down that lane since the fourth hour and it is the only sentence I can put on it. Whatever is going on, the water is further off than it was and the distance a person walks to it is exactly what it was.**"

"**And the quarter is not being supplied on a charter. It is being supplied on a class. I do not know what the difference is in a court and I know what it is at a gate: a charter is a thing somebody can point at and a class is a thing somebody has been put in, and the four of them that stopped saying it and the one that started asking who is paying are all still in those houses, and nobody out there has been told a word about any of this week.**"

"**And there is a second man with a cart in that lane. He is not the man with the cart. He has been on that lane four days and he does not come from this district and I do not know where his water comes from and I asked nobody, and I would like the clerk to enter that I did not ask, and I am going to be asked why and the answer is the same one as the last time, which is that it is not my row.**"

Nobody said anything about the second cart. The fen entered that nobody said anything about it and entered that the man of the north row with the cough had said a man was on a lane and had not said a word about how he got the water, and entered that those two halves of one sentence are in this book and are not to be joined up by anybody in four years.

The second thing was about a household in this fen, and it is smaller, and he gave it in about four sentences before anybody had got the words out for the first one.

"**The household that has been counting its own pails at a gate for eleven years was told on Tuesday, by that officer with the book, that a gate count is not a record. He said it in the yard with about twenty-five of us in it and he was not being cruel, he was doing his business, and it is not one of the four lines on his sheet and it is not wrong, and it is going to be in a room somewhere in about four years and this holding has entered it and does not know where.**"

"**And that household has kept the count anyway. Every day. And on the morning of the fourteenth the woman of that household got up at the fourth hour and walked up this road with the count written on a sheet in her own hand, nine years of it, one line a month, and she stood in this yard at about the fifth hour of the morning and gave it to the clerk and said she would like it entered, and it is entered, and she did not want anything else and she did not stay.**"

"**I am not going to say what she said. I am going to say that she counted pails for eleven years for her own house and on the morning of the fourteenth she counted them again for a body she is not part of and has never been asked to be, and nobody in this yard asked her to, and the count is on the page and it is in her hand and not in anybody else's.**"

> Entered at the seventh hour of the evening of the fourteenth of this month, in the yard, by the clerk, on the standing of a thing handed over: **a household of this holding, being one of the four that has counted its own pails at its own gate for eleven years, delivered a count in its own hand, nine years of it, one line a month, at about the fifth hour of the morning of the fourteenth of this month, and asked for it to be entered and was not asked for anything else. The fen entered the count and did not price it, and the man of the north row with the cough said in a yard of about nine on the evening of the fourteenth that the household was told on the fourth of this month by an officer of a district office, in the course of an inspection, in about twenty-five people's hearing, that a gate count is not a record, and that it is not one of the four lines on that officer's sheet and is not wrong, and that the household kept the count and counted again on the morning of the fourteenth for a body it is not part of and has never been asked to be. Nobody in that yard asked it to. Nobody thanked it. The fen entered that the count is in the woman's hand and not in anybody else's.**

What a line of it looks like, and the fen entered that description and did not improve on it, is this. **A date at the left, a number at the right, and nothing in between, and the numbers are all three figures or all two, and the hand does not change in nine years except once, in the fourth year, where a five has been made into a three and the three has been made over the five, and there is no note, and the woman said on the morning of the fourteenth that the fifth one is the one she is not sure of and that she is leaving it as it is.**

Then the sums, in the seed house with the bay doors shut on the Friday night, and they are the second sum of the month and they were not asked for and the woman who does the sums put them on the table herself and said the word before the figure.

"**I am going to do a sum I did not want to do, and I have never in my life not wanted a sum, so I am going to say that out loud first, and I am going to do it because a sum is for doing and not for wanting.**"

"**The gate count came up in a woman's hand and it is two hundred and fourteen pails for a month, and I am going to do the only thing I can do with it, which is put it against a rate. Sixteen pence a hundredweight, because that is the licensed rate and it is the only rate in this book that anybody has agreed to. Two hundred and fourteen at sixteen pence is three thousand four hundred and twenty-four pence.**"

"**The other way, because I have done sums one way since I was a girl. Two hundred and fourteen at a shilling is two thousand five hundred and sixty-eight pence. Two hundred and fourteen at fourpence is eight hundred and fifty-six pence. Two thousand five hundred and sixty-eight and eight hundred and fifty-six is three thousand four hundred and twenty-four. Same. Three thousand four hundred and twenty-four pence is two hundred and eighty-five shillings and four pence, which is fourteen pounds five shillings and fourpence. The two agree.**"

"**And here is the part I did not want, and I am going to say it in this room and not in a yard. That figure is the only number on that sheet. That sheet is nine years of a household counting its own water at its own gate in its own hand, and this holding has taken four words off the top of it and turned it into a rate, and the rate is a rate this holding can hand to an office.**"

"**That household asked for the count to be entered. It did not ask for the count to be priced. I have priced it. I would like the clerk to enter that I priced it, and enter that I priced it in a room of about eleven with the doors shut, and enter that nobody in that room stopped me, and enter that the woman who keeps the money did not say one word about it while I did it, and enter that she said one word about it afterward, and enter the word she said.**"

Anneke Roos, who is the woman who does the sums, said the word. She said **now** and she said it once, and she did not elaborate on it and she was not thanked and did not ask to be, **and the fen entered that the woman who priced it said the word and that nobody in that room could have stopped her and that she did not need stopping and that the two of those facts are on the same line.**

> Entered on the same line, in the clerk's own hand, read back: **a count of two hundred and fourteen pails for a month, delivered in a household's own hand, was put against a licensed rate of sixteen pence the hundredweight and derived twice, and the two derivations agree at three thousand four hundred and twenty-four pence, being two hundred and eighty-five shillings and four pence, being fourteen pounds five shillings and fourpence. Anneke Roos said one word about it, being now, once, and did not elaborate. Nobody thanked her. The fen entered that the household asked for the count to be entered and did not ask for it to be priced, and that this holding priced it anyway.**

Then the sentence, said out loud in that room with about eleven people in it, and it is the sentence the day is for.

"**A figure that a body cannot check is not a protection. It is a debt. And this holding has had those four for a month and this month we found out whether a form helps, and a form does not help.**"

"**They are the same four. What that quarter drinks now. The strip about two paces wide in front of four houses and what comes up out of it. The eleven a man gave in a yard on the twenty-seventh of last month, which is a number of the ones who came to a decision and not a number of anybody. And what a man spent on his own ground eleven miles off and has not written down and is not going to.**"

"**And they are not one more than they were on the first of this month and they are not one fewer, and I am not going to be praised or blamed for a count that did not move. And the count of four figures this holding cannot check and the count of the four things this body does not know are two different counts and are still not to be run together, and I have said that twice this season and I am saying it a third time and the clerk can enter that I said it a third time.**"

Then the fault line, out loud, in about five sentences, in a yard of about nine on the Saturday morning at about the eleventh hour, and the standing rule was read out in full, four lines, in that same yard before the figures were closed, and the reader of this body said the fault line standing up and the clerk was one of the nine.

"**The fault line in this body used to be between two people. Then it was between a body and a job. And it is now between a body and a class, and I am going to say why that is worse than the other two and I am not going to pretend that I know.**"

"**Two people can refuse each other. A body can refuse a job, and we have watched that happen, and it cost us a woman and a book and it was survivable. A class cannot be refused. A class is a thing an office puts on a form, and this body cannot get out of one by refusing it, because refusing a form is not a thing anybody can do twice.**"

"**I put this holding in that class in my own hand on the fourth of this month in front of a man with a book, and I would do it again, and the reason I would do it again is that the alternative was being put in it by somebody else, and I have never yet worked out which of those two I would rather have, and I have had a month to try.**"

Nobody answered that and nobody was asked to. The man of about fifty asked for his initial to be put against nothing and it went in, **and the count of that request is forty-three this season, and the fen entered the count and entered that a count of this kind is not a complaint and is not going to be a complaint at the eleventh hour of a Saturday.**

> Entered on the same line, in the reader of this body's words, read back: **the fault line in this body has moved three times in two months. It was between two people. It was between a body and a job. It is now between a body and a class. And the reason the third of those is worse than the other two is that a class cannot be refused, because a class is a thing an office puts on a form, and this holding cannot get out of one by refusing it, and refusing a form is not a thing anybody can do twice. He said he put this holding in that class in his own hand on the fourth of this month and would do it again, and said the reason, and said that he has not worked out which of the two he would rather have and has had a month to try.**
>
> **And entered on the same line: the standing rule was read out in full again, four lines, in that yard on the fifteenth, before the figures were closed. The count of the times it has been read out in full in this month is four. The count of the uses is two, and the two counts are two counts and are not the same count, and the fen entered that and did not improve on it.**

And so the fourteenth and the fifteenth of this month went down with a second man on a lane whose water nobody knows the source of, and a household's eleven years of counting in a woman's hand, and a figure of fourteen pounds five shillings and fourpence that nobody asked for, **and a fault line that has moved three times in two months and has stopped in a yard of about nine people instead of in a room, and the fourth cart was not asked about on either of those two days, it went up the plateau road on the seventh of a month three months back and has not come down, the not asking is a month and a half old and is entered as a month and a half and not as a day, and the count of the things this body does not know stands at four and was four a month ago.**
