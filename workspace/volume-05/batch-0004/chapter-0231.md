# Chapter 231

## The Woman Who Counts A Gate

The twenty-eighth of this month was a Thursday and the fourth line of that sheet came round at the second hour of the morning, and there was nobody at the headland above Marden, and the fields waited, and that is entered by whoever enters anything with the day and the hour on it, and this holding entered it on the thirtieth of this month in the words of a person who was in the yard, and did not ask for the rest of it.

About nineteen people were in that yard at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, which is the hour the seven decide, and it is not the hour the gate is counted at, and the gate is counted on the first and the fifteenth at about the tenth hour, and both of those are the woman's own hours and neither of them is this one.

She is about thirty-eight and she is not one of the seven that decide and she has never once wanted to be, and she told the reader of this body that in a road on the eighteenth of this month and it is in this holding's book.

She had a sheet of her own on the flat place at the foot of the bank, on the stone the size of a bread oven with the mason's mark on the back of it, and the man of about fifty of this party was not there and the reader of this body was not there and the licensed man was not there, and the fen entered that all three of those and entered that the man of about fifty was not there because he is at the node and this is a village, and the reader of this body was not there because he is on the cart road with a bag and a rule and nothing else in it.

She read it out herself and did not let anybody else read it, and the man of about seventy-four with the stick sat on the stone with his stick across his knees and did not say anything while she did it.

> **THE BACK OF THE GATE COUNT, in the hand of the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden, who keeps that count, on the twenty-eighth of this month at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, in Marden's own yard, in about nineteen people, read out by her and read back, and entered whole and not summarized: A COUNT OF THE MORNINGS, kept eleven years, in this hand, at this gate, of the mornings on which anybody at all stood at the headland above this village at the second hour of the morning for this village's turn.**
>
> **NINE.**
>
> **CHECKED TWO WAYS AND THE TWO AGREE. First, the gate count on the first and the fifteenth, which is a count of adults above fifteen and which I have kept for eleven years, and out of a hundred and thirty-two counts of fifty-two there are one hundred and twenty-three mornings on which the men of this village who could walk to that headland were in this village, and on one hundred and fourteen of those there was no such man out on the ground. Second, the field, which does not lie: a field that has had water in it in the second hour of a morning has a line on it you can see from the gate at noon, and I have walked to the head of that field at noon on the first and the fifteenth for eleven years and I have got the same number both ways.**

And the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden said the other half of it, and she said it to the yard and not to the sheet, and the fen entered that she said it to the yard.

"**Nine, in eleven years. And six of the nine are the same man and I am not going to tell you which man and I am not going to be asked twice, and the reason is the reason I gave in a yard at the node on the twenty-seventh of last month and it has not got better for being said twice. A name at a gate is a thing you put in a book. A name in a rota is a thing a man says with his own body at the second hour of a morning for two hours. They are not the same name and I have watched a book be read once already in this story and the book was right.**"

"**And I am not going to say that name because if I say it then six of those nine mornings is one man doing a fourth village's turn for six years, and then everybody in this yard has got a number about a person, and they will all go home and do a thing with it, and none of them will be thinking about the field.**"

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything.

And then somebody in that yard asked her a question about a person, and the fen entered that it was asked, and entered that she answered it, and entered the length of it, which was about four seconds.

"**Which of the nine was the one you were not at home for?**"

"**No.**"

"**I only asked whether it was the first one.**"

"**I know which one it was. I have known which one it was for eleven years and so have you and so has he, and the reason you asked me in a yard of about nineteen people is that you wanted a sentence you could repeat, and a sentence I say in here is a sentence about three hundred people for the rest of the year.**"

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything, and entered that nobody in that yard asked her anything else, and entered that the man of about seventy-four with the stick shifted on the stone and did not say anything either.

Then the old man of about seventy-four with the stick said one sentence from the stone, and he gave the reason for it before he gave it, which he has not done in about two years.

"**Nine against about nine hundred and ninety. A hundred and thirty-two months is a hundred and thirty-two tens of days, and thirty days is the month and not thirty-one, and three thousand nine hundred and sixty days at one turn in four is nine hundred and ninety. And there are nine. I have been on this bank for about twenty minutes and I have wanted to say that arithmetic out loud in a yard for about nine years and I have never had nine to say, because nobody counted.**"

And the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden said the sentence that stopped him, and the fen entered that she stopped him and entered that this is the second time in ten days that she has stopped a man in a yard from finishing a thing, and that the first one was at a gate and was in this holding's book in her words.

"**You can say it and you cannot say what it is for. Nine hundred and ninety is a number about a schedule and nine is a number about a field. You have just put one of them on top of the other and they are not the same height and the difference between them is the whole of what this village is and I am not going to have it turned into a figure about a ditch.**"

And then she said the thing that made the day, and she said it standing, and she said it to about nineteen people and not to a sheet.

"**I am going to the room at Fennmere. I am going because a man of sixty-eight with a cough said on Sunday at the node in a yard that it would be this village and him and why, and he did not say it to me and I have not thanked him and I am not going to, and he was not wrong and I would have come anyway.**"

"**And I am going to say what it costs, in this yard, now, while there are nineteen people here and not later in a road, and I am saying it so that there are nineteen people who have heard me say it and not one of them has been asked to do anything about it. Two nights and one day out of a village of about three hundred people. The gate is counted on the first and the fifteenth and I am not going to be standing at this gate on the first of next month, and the day I am away is not a counting day, and there is nobody else who counts, and if I do not stand at this gate then on that day about three hundred people in this village are not counted by anybody, and that has not happened in eleven years and it is a day.**"

"**And I am not going to be thanked for saying that, and if anybody in this yard thanks me I will tell them to stop, and I would rather nobody wrote it down than have it written down as a thing I was brave about. Write the day down. It is about three hundred people and it is not about me.**"

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything, and entered that about four of the nineteen people in that yard had said the word *right* out loud in about two minutes and that one of them had said *she should not have to go* and that the woman of about thirty-eight had turned round and looked at whoever said it and had not answered, and entered that the man of about seventy-four with the stick had not said anything at all after his arithmetic and had put his stick across his knees again.

And then the man of about thirty-one of Silling came down off the track into that yard at about the fifth hour of the afternoon, having come over the shoulder because the cart road does not go past this village, and he said his standing sentences and then he said one thing, and the fen entered that he said it to the count and not to the yard.

"**A man of about forty-four on the cart road told me on the road on my way over the shoulder that his own five lines are the same five every week because the five are the ones who cannot be elsewhere. He said it in his own yard in front of his own crew and he did not say it about any of you and I am carrying it here because I am carrying paper up this hill and I put it down where it came from.**"

"**And I am going to say the half of it that is mine and not his, and then I am going to go back up that hill. He is talking about a crew of five on a ditch. I am talking about a village of about three hundred people that has had nine mornings in eleven years out of about nine hundred and ninety, and I am not going to say the nine hundred and ninety again because I have said it once this afternoon and I have not got a second one.**"

And the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden said the only thing she said in that yard after the count was read out, and it was about five words, and the fen entered that it was about five words and that he wrote them down in his own book that night and did not write down the face she said them with.

"**Then write nine on it.**"

And then, on the thirtieth of this month at about the eighth hour of the evening, at nine hundred yards off a seed house, the reader of this body put the whole of it into his own book in the words of the person who had been in the yard and read it back to that person, and did not ask for the rest of it, and entered the reason, which is that a woman of about thirty-eight who has kept a count of her own for eleven years in her own hand on the back of a count she keeps for a gate has handed that count to a man she has met six times and that is enough, and that asking for the rest of it would be asking her to stand in a road and be a woman who handed over a count.

> Entered by the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden, in her own hand, at about the sixth hour of the afternoon of the twenty-eighth of this month, in Marden's own yard, in about nineteen people, on the standing of a count, and not by the clerk, who is at this holding and is five days west by the cart road, and not by the engineer of record, who was on the cart road: **A COUNT OF THE MORNINGS, kept eleven years, in my hand, at this gate, of the mornings on which anybody at all stood at the headland above this village at the second hour of the morning for this village's turn. NINE. Checked two ways and the two agree: the gate count on the first and the fifteenth, being a count of adults above fifteen, kept eleven years, out of a hundred and thirty-two counts of fifty-two there are one hundred and twenty-three mornings on which the men of this village who could walk to that headland were in this village, and on one hundred and fourteen of those there was no such man out on the ground; and the field, which does not lie, because a field that has had water in it in the second hour of a morning has a line on it you can see from this gate at noon, and I have walked to the head of that field at noon on the first and the fifteenth for eleven years and I have got the same number both ways. SIX OF THE NINE ARE THE SAME MAN and I am not going to tell this yard which man and I am not going to be asked twice, and the reason is that a name at a gate is a thing you put in a book and a name in a rota is a thing a man says with his own body at the second hour of a morning for two hours. A MAN OF ABOUT SEVENTY-FOUR WITH A STICK SAID NINE HUNDRED AND NINETY OUT LOUD IN THIS YARD and I stopped him and gave the reason first, which is that nine hundred and ninety is a number about a schedule and nine is a number about a field and the difference between them is the whole of what this village is. I AM GOING TO THE ROOM AT FENNEMERE, at the cost of two nights and one day out of a village of about three hundred people, and the gate is counted on the first and the fifteenth and there is nobody else who counts, and if I do not stand at this gate then on that day about three hundred people in this village are not counted by anybody, and that has not happened in eleven years and it is a day. I am not going to be thanked for saying that and anybody who thanks me can be told to stop. The fourth line of that sheet came round at the second hour of this morning and there was nobody at the headland and the fields waited and the standing says nobody is to be blamed for a field that waited. Nobody thanked anybody and nobody said anything.**
