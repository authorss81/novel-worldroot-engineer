# Chapter 200

## Four Columns And No Headings

The twenty-sixth of this month was a Friday and two things came up the fen road in the same bag, and the second of them was the reason the day is going to turn out the way it does.

The first was a return, four lines, in a hand that is not the hand this book has been written in for four years, and it came from the high ground on the east side of the Four Elbows, and it is correct.

> **To the body keeping the book at Lantern Reach, from the households of Marden.**
>
> **One.** We have had the proposal of equal turns and we have read it twice.
>
> **Two.** Equal turns divide one node by four and our count and the count at Havergate are not the same size, and a quarter of one thousand five hundred hundredweight a day is three hundred and seventy-five hundredweight a day, and three hundred and seventy-five a day does not grow what three hundred people have to grow, and this is arithmetic and not a complaint.
>
> **Three.** We propose that the four of us be counted, and that the node be divided in proportion to the count, and we enclose the counting paper used by the four of us and we enclose it because we would not ask anybody to divide a node on a figure they had not seen.
>
> **Four.** The counting paper is the second of its kind. The first is lost. We are sorry for it and we are not going to make a new one, because a new one would be a fifth count and four counts is enough.

The reader of this body read all four lines out in the yard of about eleven people, and the person who keeps the seed house said her standing first, and the clerk wrote them down and read them back, and nobody improved anything.

Then the man of about fifty opened the second thing, which was a sheet of ruled paper folded in three, and put it flat on the table in the yard, and there were four columns on it and eleven ruled lines and **there was no heading row.**

The four numbers were in the fourth line down, one under each column, and they were a hundred and forty-four, ninety-six, forty-eight, and two hundred and ten.

Nobody said anything for about half a minute and the fen entered the half minute.

"**Say the four numbers again, slowly, and say which column each one is in, because I have been looking at this for a minute and I do not know which of these is a village.**"

The man of about fifty said them again with the columns, and the woman of about forty-four of Havergate answered for two of them without being asked and gave a reason each time, and the man of about thirty-one of Silling answered for the fourth, and nobody answered for the third.

"**The first is us. A hundred and forty-four doors with a fire in them, and I have counted them myself twice and I am not going to give you the count of persons because I have not counted persons since the flood.**"

"**The second is Low Wether. Ninety-six hearths. A hearth is a fire, and a fire is a family, and a family is not a house, and I have told them that for eleven years and they still put it in the sheet, and I am not going to be the one who says their number is wrong.**"

"**The fourth is ours. Two hundred and ten, taken at a gate in a month two months back, and that is the oldest of the four and it is the only one of the four that anybody took by counting heads.**"

And the third column, and the forty-eight, and nobody in that yard could say what it was.

The man of about thirty-one said one thing about it, and he said it carefully, and he said it to the sheet and not to the man who had written it.

"**Forty-eight is a number I have seen in three other people's hands in eleven years and it is never a count of people. It is a count of something that was wanted.**"

The reader of this body did not write that in the book and did not repeat it, and the fen entered that he did not write it in the book, and entered the reason, which is the reason the whole of that yard turns on it.

"**I am not going to put that sentence in a book. It is a man's guess about a column on a piece of paper, and it is a good guess, and a good guess that goes in a book is a figure with a man attached to it and about four years from now somebody is going to read it and act on it. So the sheet does not say what forty-eight is and I am not going to say what forty-eight is, and if I say it in this yard somebody will write it down in about nine minutes.**"

"**That is the rule this holding has lived on for four years and I have never once had to pay for it until a Friday afternoon.**"

Then he did the sum, and he did it twice, and the woman who does the sums did it with him and the two of them and the man of about fifty all got the same three numbers.

"**On this sheet as it stands, and I am going to say the word stands on it, because it is the important word in that yard today and nobody here is going to understand that until the end of it.**"

"**Of the four numbers, two of them are households. A hundred and forty-four is a hundred and forty-four households. Ninety-six is ninety-six of something that counts a family and a family is the thing a household is a word for. Those two are two hundred and forty between them.**"

"**Two hundred and ten is persons, and a person is not a household, and a quarter of a node cannot be worked out in persons, and if you work it out in persons you are not dividing water, you are counting heads and calling it a share.**"

"**And forty-eight is nothing anybody in this yard can say, and it is therefore nothing, and a rota of four turns with nothing in one of the columns is a rota of three.**"

"**So: Havergate is a hundred and forty-four out of two hundred and forty, which is three fifths. Three fifths of one thousand five hundred hundredweight a day is nine hundred hundredweight a day. Low Wether is ninety-six out of two hundred and forty, which is two fifths, and two fifths of one thousand five hundred is six hundred hundredweight a day. Nine hundred and six hundred is one thousand five hundred, exactly, and the two of us and the woman who does the sums have that figure three times and it has not moved.**"

"**And Marden gets nothing, and Silling gets nothing, and their share is not a small share. Their share is the whole difference between a household and a number.**"

The woman of about forty-four said the thing that stopped him, and she said it standing up, and she was right.

"**Then you have done to Marden this morning exactly what a piece of paper in a bag did to Havergate on Sunday. You have decided what our number is from outside our own gate, and you have decided it in a yard, and you have decided it in a way that makes Marden nothing, and I want you to hear how that sounds and I want you to hear it as a person and not as a sum.**"

Nobody in that yard said a word and the fen entered that nobody said a word and entered the length of it.

"**That is correct and it took you eleven seconds and I have been trying to do it for four years.**"

He wrote to Marden that afternoon, on the fen's own paper, at about the fifth hour, and the fen read it out before it went and the man of about fifty read it back and the hour went on it and it went up the cart road on the Saturday post, and it is eleven words long.

> **The third column. What is the forty-eight. Not what it ought to be. What it is.**

And then, at about the seventh hour, because he could not sit down, he took the lamp and the rule and walked the nine hundred yards to the well house door, and there were about nine people at the front of the seed house who watched him go and did not go with him, and the fen entered that they did not go with him and that he did not ask them to.

The door is a plank door bolted to a frame in the ground, and it is nine hundred yards off, and it has been read on the first and the thirtieth of every month for four years, and it has a column on it that gets a figure put in it, and every figure in that column is the same figure, which is the number of readings, because that is what it is for, and a number of readings is not a number of anything else.

There are **seven columns** on that door.

The seventh has a heading above it. It has said READING over it since the door was made, and the chalk figure in it is thirty-seven, and the chalk is the chalk the fen keeps in the seed house in a tin.

The other six are blank. They have been blank since the door was made four years ago, they are blank now, and they have no headings, and no figure has ever been put in one of them, and no clerk has ever been asked to put one in, and no examiner has ever stood at this door and asked what the other six are for.

He stood there with the lamp for about ten minutes and read all seven headings, or rather read the one heading and looked at the other six places where a heading ought to be.

Then he walked the nine hundred yards back and told it to a yard of about nine people in the ordinary voice without sitting down.

"**This holding has spent four years writing down every single time it did not know something, and the count of those is a number I could put in a book, and this holding has had six empty columns on its own well house door for four years and has never once written down what any of them is for.**"

"**A column with no heading is not a small fault. A small fault is a bad figure in a column with a heading, because a man can go and look. A column with no heading is a fault that cannot be found by looking, and I have found one of those on Friday on a piece of paper that came out of a bag, and I found it by multiplying, and I have not got the faintest idea how many more of them there are.**"

"**And I am not going to write the six headings in tonight. I am going to write the day and the hour and the fact, and the six headings are going to sit there blank in front of about nine people until somebody who knows what a well house door is for comes and says what is supposed to go in them, and if nobody ever comes then six columns of a door stay blank for the rest of my life, and that is a truer entry than anything I could put in them.**"

The woman of about forty-four said one sentence into that and she was not thanked.

"**Then you have found one today by accident and there is a man on the high ground who counted forty-eight households in a bad year and told nobody, and both of those are the same kind of thing, and the one on the high ground is going to be embarrassed and you are going to find out in about a week.**"

"**Yes. And the reason I am not going to help him is that a man who is embarrassed about a number is a man who has just learned what a column is for, and if I arrive up there and tell him what his own number means I take that off him in about four seconds and it does not come back.**"

> Entered at about the ninth hour of the evening of the twenty-sixth of this month, in the seed house yard, by the clerk, on the standing of a figure and a fault: **a four-line return came up the fen road from the households of Marden, on the high ground, correct, refusing equal turns and proposing that the four bodies be counted and that the node be divided in proportion to the count, and enclosing the counting paper. The counting paper is the second of its kind, the first is lost, and it has four columns and no heading row. The four numbers are a hundred and forty-four, ninety-six, forty-eight and two hundred and ten. The first is dwellings with a fire, Havergate. The second is hearths, Low Wether, and a hearth is a family and not a house. The fourth is persons counted at a gate in a month two months back, Silling. The third is not stated by anybody and the reader of this body refused to state it and entered the reason, that a good guess in a yard becomes a figure in a book inside nine minutes. On the two of the four that are households, being a hundred and forty-four and ninety-six and two hundred and forty between them, Havergate is three fifths and Low Wether is two fifths; three fifths of one thousand five hundred hundredweight a day is nine hundred hundredweight a day and two fifths is six hundred hundredweight a day, and nine hundred and six hundred is one thousand five hundred exactly, and the figure was had three times by three people and did not move. Marden and Silling are owed nothing on that sheet and the fen said so in the yard. Brenna Oake said out loud that the reader of this body had done to Marden what the Assembly's sheet had done to Havergate and that it took eleven seconds, and the fen entered that she was right. Eleven words went up the cart road to Marden on the Saturday post asking what the forty-eight is and not what it ought to be, and the hour went on it and it was read back before it went. At about the seventh hour the reader of this body walked the nine hundred yards to the well house door and entered that the door carries seven columns, that the seventh has a heading over it and has read READING since the door was made and stands at thirty-seven, and that the other six are blank, have no headings, and never have had a figure or a heading or a question put to them in four years. The six are not to be written in by this holding. Nobody thanked anybody.**

The man of about fifty asked for his initial to be put against nothing and it went in, and the count of that request is forty-eight this season, and the fen entered the count.

And that night the reader of this body wrote under the day's work in his own hand, dated the twenty-sixth of this month, and it is the forty-ninth note and the notes ask for nothing and wait for a year, and a year and a day went by in the twelfth month with nothing coming of any of them.

*Four bodies of households, one node, about six hundred persons, and a sheet with four columns and no heading row, and on that sheet two of the four numbers are households and two of the four are not, and the arithmetic on it gives a mill town of six hundred people nine hundred hundredweight a day, a village of about four hundred people six hundred hundredweight a day, and about five hundred people between them nothing at all, and the sum is exact and the exactness is the finding and not the answer. A share is a number. A number is a charter. Four villages have gone eleven years without one and have now got one on a sheet because one of them asked for a proportion, and the proportion was worked out by a man five days west of them who was asked for no paper and offered one.*

*And this holding has six blank columns on its own door and has had them for four years, and a door is not a sheet of paper and a well house is not a node, and I am putting both of them in one note on purpose, because a body that writes down a fault in other people's paper in a yard and does not write down a fault in its own yard is a body that has learned to be right, and a body that has learned to be right is a body that has stopped being a body.*

*Eleven words went up the road to a village on the high ground asking one question. The answer is not in this book and this holding is not going to write and ask again.*
