# Chapter 205

## Two Hundred And Ten

The second of this month was a Thursday and the counting paper came down the cart road with a woman on the cart, and the woman got off at the gate and said her name and her position standing up before anybody asked her for either.

"**Hesta Lyle, of about fifty-one. I keep the four books for the four bodies of households at the Four Elbows and I am the only person who does, and that is not because anybody appointed me, it is because there was nobody else, and I have not asked for help and I am not going to. I am not of this holding and I am not a member of anything that meets.**"

She had the sheet folded in three in her coat and she took it out in the yard and put it on the table and did not open it, and the reader of this body asked her one question, and he gave the reason for the question before he asked it, which is the fourth time in nine days and the count is entered.

"**I am not going to ask you to trust what four people said in this yard about a column with no heading on it. I am going to ask you for the papers the four of you wrote the numbers on, all four, in your own hands, and I am going to write the headings on this sheet out loud in this yard from those papers and not from anybody's memory including mine.**"

"**And the reason is that a heading is not an opinion. A heading is a name somebody put on a thing at the time they counted it, and if I write a heading from memory I have written my own opinion on your sheet in your own yard and about four years from now one of you will say so and be right.**"

Hesta Lyle gave him the four papers and she stood in that yard and did not read them herself, because the reader of this body asked her not to and because she had already worked out that she would say them out loud in the wrong order.

He wrote nine headings and figures onto the sheet, and the man of about fifty wrote them down from him in the book, and he read each one back and she checked it against her own paper, and the whole of the nine was done in about twenty minutes and nobody argued about any of it.

> **THE HEADING ROW, as supplied from the four papers and in the four villages' own words, and no word of it is this holding's:**
> **DWELLINGS WITH A FIRE AND A DOOR. HEARTHS. HOUSEHOLDS THAT TOOK SEED IN THE YEAR BEFORE LAST. PERSONS COUNTED AT A GATE.**
> **A hundred and forty-four. Ninety-six. Forty-eight. Two hundred and ten.**

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything.

Then he went down the sheet to the seven ruled lines under the counting, which had been blank on the sheet as it came down the road, and which are the lines where the four of them put the last whole week's figure of water that came out of the node and into their own ground.

"**These seven lines are empty and they should not be, and the four of you have been running a node for eleven years with an empty line under your own arithmetic, and I have got the four numbers and I am going to write them down in front of you, and if one of them is wrong I am going to find out in this yard and not in a letter.**"

He wrote the first four and the woman who does the sums checked them, and the man of about fifty read them back, and the fifth was the number in Havergate's own mill book.

> **HAVERGATE. Nine hundred and forty hundredweight a day, out of the four bodies' own count of the mill's draw over the last whole week before the frost.**
> **LOW WETHER. Six hundred and ten hundredweight a day.**
> **MARDEN. Not entered. The line is blank and it has been blank for eleven years.**
> **SILLING. Not entered. The line is blank and it has been blank for eleven years.**

The reader of this body stopped there and did not go on, and the fen entered that he stopped there, and Hesta Lyle looked at the two figures for about as long as it takes a person to add four numbers.

"**Nine hundred and forty and six hundred and ten. That is one thousand five hundred and fifty.**"

"**That node delivers about one thousand five hundred hundredweight a day and it has delivered about one thousand five hundred hundredweight a day for as long as anybody has been counting, and I told you the figure on the first day I was in that yard and I did not derive it, I read it off a board in a shed, and the board has a column on it and the column has no heading.**"

"**Which means one thousand five hundred and fifty is fifty more than the whole of it, and somebody in this yard has had a hand in that fifty for a week or more, and I am going to say now that I have been in this yard nine days and I have done a sum twice in front of about eleven people and got nine hundred and six hundred both times, and nine hundred and six hundred is what two of your own books say after you take the overstatement out of them.**"

"**I did not derive it from the headings. I derived it from a column with no heading and I said out loud in this yard that I could not find out what forty-eight was and I was not going to guess at it. And a thing I was not willing to guess at turned out to be the correct answer, and I want that in the book in those words and I want the clerk to read it back in those words.**"

Then the clerk did the sum out loud, twice, with the four papers open on the table, and the second time he had the turn sheets as well, which record the number of pails that went on the boards each morning, and the sum came out the same both times and the two of them agreed.

> **THE OVERSTATEMENT. Havergate's own mill book overstates the draw by forty hundredweight a day. Low Wether's own book overstates it by ten hundredweight a day. Corrected: nine hundred and six hundred hundredweight a day, and nine hundred and six hundred is one thousand five hundred exactly, and the balance of nothing goes to Marden and Silling, whose lines have been blank for eleven years.**
> **FIFTY HUNDREDWEIGHT A DAY IS THE WHOLE OF WHAT MARDEN AND SILLING HAVE BEEN LIVING WITH SINCE SOMEBODY WROTE A FIGURE DOWN.**

Hesta Lyle checked both of the arithmetic corrections herself, and the fen had asked her to, and she said one thing about them out loud in the yard with the four papers still on the table, and she said it to the reader of this body and not to the yard.

"**I have kept those four books for nineteen years. I did not know the two figures were overstated. I have checked both of them and both of them are right and I have had them in my own hand for a whole week and I did not see it, and I am telling you that in front of these people because in about four years the only thing anybody is going to want to know is whether a man corrected a village's book on purpose and a woman checked it, and the answer to that is that I checked it and I am saying so in a yard.**"

"**And the other thing. Both of your corrections put your own sum in the right. I want that said out loud too, because it is the thing nobody is going to say for you, and if I do not say it now then in four years somebody will say that you wrote a figure on a village's sheet to make your own argument come out, and I will have to say that I checked it, and I checked it and it does not matter what you wanted because it is the same figure.**"

The reader of this body said one sentence and it went in the book and the clerk read it back.

"**Then it is the second time in six days that somebody has said the thing I was not going to say about myself, and the first one was a woman of forty-four on a Friday, and I am not going to improve on either of them, and I am not going to be thanked for being corrected, because being corrected is not a favour to me, it is what the yard is for.**"

> Entered at about the eleventh hour of the morning of the second of this month, in the seed house yard, by the clerk, on the standing of a document: **the counting paper came down the cart road with Hesta Lyle, of about fifty-one, who keeps the four books for the four bodies of households at the Four Elbows and is the only person who does, and who is not of this holding and is not a member of anything. The reader of this body asked for the four papers the numbers were written on, and for the headings to be written from those papers and not from memory, and gave the reason first, which is that a heading is not an opinion and a heading written from memory is a man's opinion on a village's sheet in a village's yard. Nine headings and figures went onto the sheet and were read back and checked against the four papers and nothing was argued about. The headings, in the four villages' own words, are DWELLINGS WITH A FIRE AND A DOOR, HEARTHS, HOUSEHOLDS THAT TOOK SEED IN THE YEAR BEFORE LAST, and PERSONS COUNTED AT A GATE, over one hundred and forty-four, ninety-six, forty-eight and two hundred and ten. The seven ruled lines below the counting were blank on the sheet as it came down the road. Havergate's own mill book overstates its draw by forty hundredweight a day and Low Wether's own book overstates it by ten hundredweight a day, and the two were corrected out loud in a yard, and corrected they are nine hundred hundredweight a day and six hundred hundredweight a day, and nine hundred and six hundred is one thousand five hundred exactly, and Marden's line and Silling's line have been blank for eleven years, and the balance of nothing is theirs. Fifty hundredweight a day is the whole of what Marden and Silling have been living with since somebody wrote a figure down. The reader of this body said out loud that he did not derive nine hundred and six hundred from the headings, that he derived it from a column with no heading, that he said in the yard he could not find out what forty-eight was and was not going to guess at it, and that a thing he was not willing to guess at turned out to be the correct answer. Hesta Lyle checked both arithmetic corrections, said that she had had the two figures in her own hand for a week and had not seen it, said that she was saying so in a yard because in about four years the only question anybody will want answered is whether a man corrected a village's book on purpose, and said that both of the corrections put his own sum in the right and that this is the thing nobody is going to say for him. The reader of this body entered that this is the second time in nine days that somebody has said a thing he was not going to say about himself. Nobody thanked anybody.**

Then the party loaded, at about the seventh hour of the same afternoon, in the rain, and the man of about fifty had a list and the list is in the book with the hour on it, and nobody argued with the list either.

> **THE FIVE, going east on the cart road on the third of this month, entered by the man of about fifty and read back before the cart was loaded:**
> **The engineer of record, who is not on the roll for the district in which the node stands and knows it.**
> **The man of about fifty, who holds the book and has not left this holding in nine days.**
> **The woman who does the sums, who has never been east of Fennmere.**
> **The man of the north row with the cough, who is about sixty-eight, who offered before anybody asked him, and who is entered because he offered and not because anybody wanted him.**
> **Coll Renner, licensed, on the roll for the Tarrow district, who is not on the roll for the district in which the node stands, and who came anyway, at his own cost, and said so in a yard.**
> **The cost is five days of cart and about nine days of road and back, and this holding has four pounds and fivepence in the box, and the cart is hired and is not ours, and two days of one household's plowing do not get done, and the fen entered the four costs separately and did not add them.**

And at about the ninth hour of the evening of the second, in a yard of about eleven people, the reader of this body put a question into this holding's own book, and the clerk was not asked to write it and did not write it, and the reader of this body wrote it himself in his own hand with the hour on it and read it back.

"**A question this book has carried for nine days and asked once and been answered never, and I am putting it in the book because a month turned and the book is where things go. Nobody is going to answer it. I am not going to answer it. It names no office and no machine and no person and no year.**"

"**A thing that can be shared is a thing somebody has to give up, and nobody in this book has yet said what a village gives up when its turn comes round.**"

"**That is the whole of it. It has been available to a room in this holding and not taken twice, and the count of the times this body has asked it is one, and the two are two counts and neither has been added to the other. And the standing count of the questions available to a room in this holding and not taken goes off seven on to eight, because a question went into the book and nobody took it, and it is not going to be added to the count of that one and that one is not going to be added to it.**"

Nobody said anything. Hesta Lyle was at the wall and she was not of this holding and the fen entered that she heard it and did not answer it and did not say afterwards that she would have, and entered the count of people in that yard who did not answer it as eleven and did not add the figure to anything.

*Entered by the engineer of record, in his own hand, at about the ninth hour of the evening of the second of this month, in the seed house yard, on the standing of a question, and not by the clerk:* **a thing that can be shared is a thing somebody has to give up, and nobody in this book has yet said what a village gives up when its turn comes round. Asked once. Available to a room in this holding and not taken twice. Nobody answered it. The standing count of the questions available to a room in this holding and not taken goes from seven to eight, and the two counts are two counts and neither has been added to the other, and the fen entered that nobody in this book has ever added one of them to another.**

And the man of the north row with the cough, who was in that yard, said one sentence on his way to the cart shed and did not stop to say it.

"**Neither have they. And I am going to be in a yard on the ninth of this month with a question of my own and it is a worse one than yours, and I am not going to tell you what it is on the road, because you would try to answer it.**"
