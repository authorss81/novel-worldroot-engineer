# Chapter 238

## Ninety-Two

The thirteenth of this month was a Friday and the return was made in the morning at about the second hour and it was the sixteenth of them. The reader of this body had walked the middle of the ground at about the seventh hour of the evening of the day before, had looked at the flat of it, had gone away, and had not said anything to anybody about what he thought was under it.

Harlan Vetch came up the four miles with the barrow and the two baskets with the lids, the same barrow, about eleven years old, the wheel re-tired twice by a man who is not a wheelwright, and he came at the speed he comes at, which is about an hour and a quarter, and was at the node about ten minutes before the man of about fifty could balance a steelyard.

The steelyard is hung on the wall of the building with no roof and is about forty years old and the sliding weight has a nick in it. The man of about fifty balanced it and read the figure and took his hands off it, and then each of the four bodies of households read it back after him, one at a time, standing where they could see the dial.

"**NINETY-TWO HUNDREDWEIGHT.**"

The man of about fifty said the number twice, once for the yard and once for himself, and the reader of this body wrote it down at the time in his own book and did not enter it in the schedule, and the schedule is four sheets folded in four and soft at the folds and the wax at the last broken and mended twice and a good deal older than eleven years, and it is a document of the fifth share and not of the four bodies, and the four bodies do not hold it.

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything, and entered that ninety-two is the largest of the eleven returns and that the two ends of the eleven are ninety and ninety-one and that a figure at the end of a run of eleven is a figure that has been arrived at, and that the reader of this body said out loud in about eleven people that a figure that comes round again is not a figure that has come back, and that he has said it once already this season in a yard at this node and is not going to say it again.

And then a name went into the space headed BY and a name into the space headed WITNESSED BY, and it is the seventh pair in eleven years and the first of the seven in which the first name is not a person who carries the barrow, and the reader of this body entered that he does not know what to do with that and is not going to find out.

The man of about thirty-one of Silling wrote his own name into the first of the two spaces and the man of about fifty read it back. Nobody asked him to, and he did not ask for a place on a line and he was given one, and the fen entered that the seven at Silling decided that and not this holding and not him.

And then the man of about fifty-eight, who holds the fifth share, put the barrow down against the wall of the building with no roof, and took the chalk out of his own pocket, and went to the second rule, and wrote in the space headed OBSERVATIONS AND REMARKS, in his own hand, without being asked, two words.

**WET SEASON**

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything, and entered that nobody asked him what it meant, and that the reader of this body had wanted to ask for about four years and did not ask, and that the word is in that column nine times in eleven years, once a year since he took the share, and that this is the tenth.

"**I asked him on the road on the way up whether he was going to write it and he said he did not know, and I did not ask him again, and I am not going to ask him in about four years either, and the reason is that he writes a word in his own hand in a column headed observations in a schedule nobody has read in eleven years. If I ask him what decides it then he has to have a reason, and if he has a reason then it is a rule, and a rule a man of fifty-eight has to have about a season is a rule I have put on a man of fifty-eight.**"

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything, and entered that the man of the north row with the cough was in that yard and was not asked to be in it, and entered that the count of yards he has stood in since the first of a month two months back is eleven and has moved.

And the turn of the ground above Marden is four inches down and eleven days old and the ditch at the top of the bank is ninety feet long and dry at both ends for the thirty-second day, and the reader of this body walked up the shoulder on the Friday afternoon with a rule in his coat and took the depth of the standing water in the west cut in a fifth, which is nine feet off the hollow and is the lowest of the four and has been the lowest since the launder was cut on the eleventh of the first month.

It is a foot and eight inches and he did not write it anywhere anybody would act on it, and the fen entered that he has four takings of that sill in his own book and that this is the fifth, that the intervals between the first four are thirty-five days, forty-two days from the first to the third and eight days from the second to the fourth, and that the interval from the fourth to the fifth is thirty-five days, and that he wrote all three in his own book and none of them out loud, and that a figure with a control in it is a figure a village can act on and that this one has five takings and no control.

The fen entered that nobody said anything on that bank, and entered that a figure with five takings and no control is one he has to carry himself, and that he did.

And then it was the fourteenth of the month and a Saturday and the first of the nine mornings came round at the second hour. The reader of this body was on the ground before anybody else and had been since about the half hour, and the four working came up the shoulder at about the half hour too, and the man of the north row with the cough was not on the ground because it was not his morning, and the fen entered that he was four miles off cutting withy and was not asked to be there.

The fen entered that the man of about nineteen of Silling was on the flat of the board at the second hour and not a minute before it, and that the other three came up the shoulder together and not one of them was late.

The hold is not a thing a body can watch, and that is the first thing the reader of this body has to say about it and has not said in a yard, because it is not a thing anybody in four villages is going to be able to do on a Tuesday, and because a thing nobody can watch has to be written in a hand that is not this holding's.

Harlan Vetch, of about fifty-eight, worked the west side with a spade and a barrow and took out about nine inches off the top of the west cut and put it on the bank, and said out loud that he was not going to put it back and did not know where it went, and nobody asked him. Renn Ashby, of about twenty-six, of Havergate, worked the north side and moved about nine hundredweight of stone out of a line that was not on any plan and had not been since the kerb went in, and carried it up the bank on his own back because there was one barrow and Harlan Vetch had it. The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden worked the sill with a rule and a piece of chalk and said two numbers out loud at about the fourth hour and the reader of this body wrote both of them down and entered that he did not know what either of them were a unit of and did not ask.

A man of about nineteen of Silling worked the middle and did not speak for two hours and got the water in the middle down about four inches and then held it there, and at about the third hour of the afternoon he sat down on the flat of the board for about a minute and got up again, and nobody said anything about it and he did not say anything about it either.

And at about the fourth hour the reader of this body said the word and the man of about fifty, about nine feet off with the book, heard it and said so.

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything, and entered that a hold for four with four people working and the man of about fifty nine feet off with the book hearing the word is the third of those in this holding's book and that the count of persons who can hold a pattern for four is two and did not move and that the number of holds in a book is a different number from the number of persons, and that this is the eighth time those two have been confused in a yard by somebody who was not this holding.

And then he said the thing he had come to say on the Friday, and he said it to the four working and not to the man of about fifty, and he gave the reason first, and the count of that in this holding's history is thirty-nine.

"**I am going to say one thing about what nine mornings is worth and I am going to say it now, at the fourth hour, on the first one, and not at the ninth, because at the ninth it will be a thing that has happened.**"

"**Nine mornings is worth this: that a party of five can stand on a stone for two hours, on nine consecutive mornings, with the holder changing every morning between two people, and can do it in a week when the ditch is dry. It is worth nothing at all about the water. Not one number I take in the next nine days tells anybody in four villages what happens when the ditch fills, and the one day of the nine worth the other eight is the day it happens on. If it never happens on, this was nine mornings of two men standing on a stone, and I said that this morning in a yard and I am saying it again at the fourth hour because a yard is cheap and this is the fourth hour.**"

"**And the thing I am not going to do is get to the fourth line. I have been asked twice in eleven weeks whether I will take it and I have not answered either time and I am not going to answer it on Saturday. If the water comes on the twenty-second I am going to be the wrong distance off this ground and the man of the north row with the cough is going to be on it, and I entered that in my own book on the eleventh and I am saying it in about nine people now so that it is not a surprise on the twenty-second.**"

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything, and that the count of times the man of about fifty has been in a yard and said nothing this month is two.

And the four working were asked nothing at all on the Saturday and said nothing at all about what it cost them, and it was the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden who said it, on the bank above the west cut, to three people and not to him, in her own arithmetic, and the reader of this body was nine feet off and heard all of it.

"**I am away from that gate on four mornings out of nine and it is the second and the fourth and the ninth and the eleventh and a gate is counted on the first and the fifteenth and nobody has ever counted it on any other day, so there is no day in my life that a gate of about three hundred people has gone uncounted because of a stone in a field.**"

"**That is not a complaint about the stone. That is me finding out that the stone is free, which is the fourth thing in four days I have found out about a thing that everybody in this yard has been putting a price on. I am putting the price down and I am not going to be thanked for putting a price down, and if anybody thanks me they can be told to stop.**"

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything, and entered that the man of about twenty-six of Havergate said one thing in a shed on the Saturday night and it was not in a yard and the fen was not in the shed and heard it from the man of about fifty on the Monday and entered it as a thing he was told and not as a thing he was present at, which is the rule this holding has used about nine times in eleven weeks.

What Renn Ashby said was that the stone in the line on the north side had a mason's mark on it, and that it was the same mark as the third part of the kerb, and that the third part was the one that was not the same stone as the other three.

The reader of this body wrote it down in his own book on the Monday at about the ninth hour and entered that he is not going to follow it, and entered the reason, which is that a mason's mark is a mason's mark and that he watched the kerb go round in the fourth year of the eleven from the end of a bank and did not see who laid it, and that a stone nine hundredweight out of a ditch on a Saturday night is a stone in a line, and that neither of those is the question any of this is about and he is not going to make it one.

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything, and entered that it took a party of about nine people nine days to get him to take the fifth of that sill himself, and entered that a falling figure on a sill two inches in a month is a figure and not a finding, and that he has said that last part out loud twice this season and is not going to say it a third time.

> Entered at about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the fourteenth of this month, in a yard at Errow Reach, in about nine people, by the man of about fifty, on the standing of a return, and read back before it went on the page: **THE SIXTEENTH RETURN, made on the thirteenth of this month at about the second hour, carried up the four miles by one man in a barrow in about an hour and a quarter, weighed on the steelyard hung on the wall of the building with no roof, and read back by each of the four bodies of households in turn, one at a time, standing where they could see the dial. THE FIGURE IS NINETY-TWO HUNDREDWEIGHT and it is the largest of the eleven. A name went into BY and a name went into WITNESSED BY, being the seventh pair in eleven years and the first of the seven in which the first name is not a man who carries the barrow, and the seven at Silling decided it and not this holding. IN THE SECOND RULE, IN HIS OWN HAND AND WITHOUT BEING ASKED, THE MAN OF ABOUT FIFTY-EIGHT WROTE TWO WORDS: WET SEASON. It is the tenth time in eleven years, once a year since he took the share, and nobody has asked him how he knows and he has never said and he is not going to be asked in this holding's book. THE NINTH MORNING OF NINE CAME ROUND AT THE SECOND HOUR AND WAS HELD FROM THE SECOND HOUR TO THE FOURTH HOUR, ONE HOLDER AND FOUR WORKING, and the word was said at the fourth hour and was heard by the man of about fifty at about nine feet off. THE FOURTH LINE OF THE ROTA CAME ROUND ON THE FOURTEENTH OF THIS MONTH AND THERE WAS NOBODY AT THE HEADLAND ABOVE MARDEN AND THE FIELDS WAITED, and the seventh line says nobody is blamed for a field that waited and nobody is.**

Nobody said anything and the fen entered that nobody said anything, and entered that this is the sixth morning of the fourth line to be empty since the twenty-second of the second month, and entered that the man of the north row with the cough was four miles off on the Saturday and was not asked to be at the headland, and entered that a man four miles off cutting withy could not be at a headland above Marden by the second hour and that nobody has ever pretended he could be and that this is the first time the two things have been on the page together.
