# Chapter 81

## The Road

They walked it on the Sunday, the three of them, because the carting was not decided and a road is a thing you walk before you pay anybody to drive over it.

Marek Vale, Jonas Byre and Ilsa Kern left the gate at about the seventh hour with a bar, a line, a spade and a bucket, and they were on the March road at the eleventh mile by the tenth hour, and Ilsa Kern marked the place with a stone on a stake and did not explain it and did not have to.

The fen road for eleven miles is the best road in this district and everybody in it says so and everybody in it is tired of saying so. The March road is a different animal. It is a line of about forty farms with a good surface on eleven miles of it, and this fen was on the bad stretch, and the bad stretch is four miles long, and half of it is standing water in the ditches and the other half is a bad half mile where the ditches have gone and the water has come up out of the ground and gone across the road and made a field of it.

"Forty years of the same ditch and nobody has ever cut the same ditch twice," Ilsa Kern said, in the bad half mile, standing in it up to the shin. "It is not the road that is bad. The road is on top of a road that has been moved twice, and every time it was moved because a man took out a bigger stone, and the water always comes back to where it was when there were no stones in it at all."

Then the ground, at about a mile short of the low dry, and it was the thing in the road.

It was about forty yards long. The peat had gone out of the top of it on both sides and the sides had slumped and there was standing water in the middle to about the depth of a boot, and the bottom of it was gravel where the peat had been and gravel that has had a foot of water stood on for a fortnight does not hold a wheel. A cart came over it empty on a plank and got across in about two minutes, and a loaded cart would go over it on a plank and a chain and a great deal of care, and the ground under it was soft to about the depth of a man's forearm and there is no stone within a mile and no stone within three miles of the low dry either, because the low dry is a low dry.

They stood on the far side of it for a long time. Jonas Byre put his bar in and it went in to the shoulder in two places and came up with black water in the slot.

Then they walked the rest of it, to the low dry and past it, because Marek wanted to see what was on the other side of the low dry, and what was on the other side of the low dry was the second break, a quarter of a mile on, and the second break was the bad one. The bad one is not forty yards. The bad one is sand, and it is a quarter of a mile of running sand with a soft rise at the far end of it, and there is a man in this district who has got a cart across the bad one with a team and a plank and he says he would not do it again, and he is not a man who says things.

"Ivo Tarrant's men would have dug the low quarter ditch in a night," said Jonas Byre, at the second break, with his hand on the sand. "Four of them, a barrow of tools, three nights, no charge, no names, and the condition that it could never be got back. And we refused them on five correct grounds and one of the five was that a nameless book makes the Fennmere copy a copy of a rumor, and the low quarter still has four wells off in it and forty-one houses on a bed this holding killed."

"I know," said Marek.

"I am not saying it to be hard on you. I am saying it at this sand because a man standing at a break is entitled to say the thing he is thinking about, and I have been thinking about it since the twenty-seventh of a month three months back and there is a ditch nine miles from here that four men would have dug for nothing and did not dig because we said no, and we said no correctly. I want it in the book that I said it correctly and I want it in the book that I have thought about it every day."

"It is in the book," said Dunmar Fitch, in the fen, on the Sunday night, and read it back, and Jonas Byre initialled it, and the reader was the man of about fifty, who had not been to the road and who read it correctly and said afterward that he wished he had been, and nobody told him why he had not been, and the reason was entered by Lissa Vale in one line and not read aloud to anybody, because she said a thing a person did not say out loud was not a thing anybody had to hear.

---

They took the carts out on the Monday, the twenty-fourth, and hiring them was the first money this holding has spent on itself in a season and it was spent on getting somebody else's seed off this holding's ground.

Two carts out of Fennmere, five shillings the day each, the carters' own man with each of them at two shillings a day, which is fourteen shillings a day, and the carters wanted the day's money on the morning, which is the practice and is not dishonesty.

Lissa Vale counted the seed house tin out on the step in front of two people before the money left it. Thirteen shillings and a shilling that came in on the fourteenth of this month from the one copy of the printing sold in the yard. Fourteen shillings. A day's carts. And the man of about fifty read the fourteen shillings out and initialled it and said, afterward, that he had been the loudest man in this yard about money since the twelfth of a month two months back and had been right about this once, and that it would not become a habit.

The levy went round the same morning, at four times what it was.

"It is entered," said Anneke Roos, "that Jonas Byre said on the fifth of this month, in this yard, that the levy would come round at four times what it was because the men would now know there was money somewhere, and that he said he was sorry to say it and meant it, and that it is in the book, and that it has happened, and that we are asking for it anyway, and I would like both halves of that in the same paragraph."

Eighteen pounds, which is four times four pounds five, and the levy is four times what it was because a woman from Marrowfield put four shillings into this building's tin in front of seven hands on the seventh of last month, and a boy went down this road on the fourteenth with a book and a shilling, and both of those things are in the district.

Thirteen pounds four came in. Four pounds sixteen did not, and the clerk entered that it was not received and was not remitted and was not carried as a debt against anybody, because a body cannot make a debt of a man who has chosen not to pay it and then call it a debt in its own book without becoming something else.

At the break, the carters' man had a chain off on the near side and it took him an hour to mend with a cold rivet and a hammer he had brought, and it was a mender of implements out of Fennmere called Wil Tam who mended it, because Tam keeps a bothy at the crossing and the carters go to him, and he charged two shillings for the cold rivet and would not take anything at all for a tire he found split where the tire had met the plank, and said why.

"Because you have not had a tire off me before and I would like there to be a first one that cost you nothing," he said. "Next one is two and I will tell you so at the time."

Then they put a load on.

They put on what the carts would have carried if the carts were going, which was four of the fen's own bean crates and four of the March's, lashed the way Nia Vale lashed crates on a hand barrow in the spring, which is a thing three people in this yard can still do and which none of them had wanted to think about for a season.

The plank went down. They got a loaded cart over the first break in about eleven minutes with two men on the plank at the front wheels and a chain to a mule from the mill, and it stood, and it stood well, and it could have been done again in an hour.

It could not be done again in an hour, and that was the sentence that decided the twenty-fourth, and it was said by the carters' man, who is a man who crosses bad ground for a living and who had been doing it for nineteen years and who had no interest in anybody's book.

"I can put one over," he said. "I have done it and I would do it again tomorrow. I cannot put a second one over this week in the same place, because the place I have just used is a hole now and it will be a worse hole on Monday, and if I bring a second cart over it I will be standing in it. And I cannot bring that cart back with the load on it, and that is the part your engineer has not asked me about. You can go over loaded. You cannot come back loaded. Ever. Not this month and not in the spring."

"Say that again," said Marek.

"You can go over loaded. You cannot come back loaded. That is the ground and not me. The ground under that plank is peat and gravel with a foot of water stood on it, and a loaded cart going over it presses the water down and it comes up on the far side, and a loaded cart coming back onto it puts the wheels in the hole it made on the way out. You would come back in the same hole, one yard further on, and you would be in up to the shafts and I would be standing there telling you about it."

The decision was made in about four minutes and it was not a close one and Marek Vale made it himself and did not vote on it, because it was his arithmetic and the fen's rule is that a man's own arithmetic is his and the room's.

"The loaded carts do not go," he said. "Not today, and not in this month. One of them may go across and come back empty at the end of the month, and the other one may stay at the gate where it costs nothing, and I am not hiring a cart a day to stand in a yard. And I am not going to send four crates of the fen's own bean and four of the March's over that ground to a place where nobody has been asked and nobody has answered and there is no form, because a crate that cannot be brought back cannot be accounted for, and a route that only goes one way is not a route."

The carts came back empty, both of them, and the day's money was fourteen shillings and the chain was two, and a tire was nothing, and the whole of that was sixteen shillings, and the sixteen shillings bought nothing at all, and every one of the sixteen is in the book with the hour.

The four crates of the March's had been lashed for the better part of an hour and then unlashd in a quarter of one by a man who said, and entered, that there was no point in a crate that has been lashed and not sent because a lashed crate sweats and a sweated crate loses about a pound in a fortnight, which is a hundred and eight pounds of the March's rye a year, which is a number, and the number is nobody's, and it was in the book because Jonas Byre asked for it and because Tova Reed said that a trade that cannot say what a lashing costs is a trade that will be told.

And Jonas Byre said the thing that is the whole of the twenty-fourth, standing in the yard at dusk with his bar over his shoulder, to a man who had already gone inside.

"Mr Vale. I want to say a thing and I want it in the book in my words and I want it read back and I want the reader to be somebody who was not there. We have paid sixteen shillings today to move a thing we have not got, over a road we do not own, to a place we have never seen, and the road is out at two places and one of them is a hole and the other is sand, and I would like it entered that I said the first of it on the seventeenth of this month and I have said it twice now and that I am not going to say it a third time because two is enough and three would be a man making a case."

"A bridge with no return," said Marek, "is a road with crates on it."

Jonas Byre looked at him for a second.

"Write that down," he said, "because I did not say it and you did, and I would rather it was in your mouth than mine, and I am not going to argue with a sentence I could not have improved on."

Underneath the schedule, dated the twenty-fourth, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the fifteenth note under section nine of the charter schedule: eleven miles of fen road and four bad miles of March road walked and measured on the twenty-third by three men; the first break, forty yards of gone ground, put in and taken out; the second break, a quarter mile of running sand with a soft rise; the carters' man, nineteen years at it, who can put one loaded cart over the first break and cannot put one back; Wil Tam, of the bothy at the crossing, two shillings for a cold rivet and nothing for a tire; a levy at four times what it was, thirteen pounds four received and four pounds sixteen not received and not remitted; two carts at five shillings a day and two carters' men at two, fourteen shillings a day; sixteen shillings spent and nothing moved; and a bridge with no return is a road with crates on it.

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*
