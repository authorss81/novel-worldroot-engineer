# Chapter 85

## No Holder

Gael Senn did the examination at the seventh hour on the first of the month, in the same words she has used since the first of a month two months back, and there was no charter to make it a condition, and she did it anyway, and nobody asked her to.

The leak was five-eighths of an inch. Twice measured, two rules, and the second rule the one that reads smaller on that corner. Pump on. Into the housing and not into the pit, and the pit four feet of iron water and not up a quarter of an inch. She wrote all of that in the words she uses and read it back.

The beat was the twelfth. Nine in the quarter hour with one run of four, at the rail, cause not established, licensed person present none, and nothing written underneath it, which is her decision and which is hers and not the thing's.

The bed was at three and a half degrees, twice, and a soil iron cannot read the tenth of a degree.

The return line said what it has said for three months: nothing put into the return main. None available. Specification four hundred hundredweight a month. Shortfall three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight, seventy-nine pounds sixteen at four shillings, carried forward and not discharged.

And then the thing nobody in that yard had ever had to do, which took her about a minute and a half and which nobody helped her with.

She signed for herself.

"Under a charter, Miss Senn," said Dunmar Fitch, "the examiner signs and the holder countersigns, and the counter is the district's."

"I know," said Gael Senn. "There is no charter. There is no examiner's appointment, because there is no charter to appoint under, and there is no form for an examination that nobody is required to hold, and there is nobody in this district who is going to come up this road on the first of the month to be told *nothing found* by a woman of twenty-three with a soil iron and a rule." She put the pen down. "So it is entered as a thing a person did at the seventh hour of the first of this month, in the ordinary words, in the ordinary book, and nobody has countersigned it and nobody is entitled to and I would like the clerk to enter that I signed it for myself and why, and I would like it read back."

It went in, and the reader was Jonas Byre, and it is the first entry in this holding's book that carries a signature with nothing behind it at all.

The report went up on the cart at the ninth hour, nine lines, to the sub-office and to Cray, and there was no condition requiring it and no form for it and no addressee with standing to receive it, and Dunmar Fitch entered that it went because Gael Senn wrote it and not because anybody asked her to, and the ninth line still said that nobody in the yard could say what the root had changed, and the cart took it, and the root did not change on the first of a month because anybody looked at it.

And then the thing that the whole of the first of the month had been about, and nobody had said it aloud until the second.

"The examination at which the third condition is tested," said Marek Vale, "is the first examination held after the term ends. The term ended at midnight on the twenty-ninth of last month. This morning was the first examination. It was held. It was not described."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"Where is the form," said Anneke Roos. "Somebody has got the form. It is in a drawer in a district office nine miles from here with a line of type on it that says the third condition is not to be described before the examination at which it is tested, and somebody in this district has to have that form in front of them, and if they have it then they could have read it out, and if they read it out then we would know what it is, and we do not know what it is, and the examination has been and gone."

"There is no form," said Gael Senn. "There is a form. It is the form that came up this road with a man and a case on the first of last month, and it is the district's, and I wrote what it said on it and read it back. It is not a form for testing the condition. It is a form with a line of type about when the condition is tested. And that form is in a file nine miles from here, and there is no second sheet, and there is no book, and the appointment of an examiner to be told a thing is a thing the district does under a charter, and there is no charter, and so the district has nobody to tell."

"So it is untested," said Dunmar Fitch, "and it is unowned, and it is unnamed, and the test date has been and gone, and the reason it has been and gone is that it was never ours to keep, and I want that entered in that order and not in the other one."

It went in in that order: that the third of the three conditions in clause three was not described at the first examination held after the end of the term; that the examination was held at the seventh hour on the first of this month by a person who signed for herself; that there was no officer of the district present and none was required to be; that the reason is that there is no charter and there is nothing to appoint an examiner under; and that the condition is therefore untested, unowned and unnamed, and that no person in Tarrow Fen has any idea what it is and has not guessed and will not.

"May I say the rest of it," said Marek Vale, "and it is my rest of it and it is not yours."

"Say it."

"We are not going to write and ask," he said. "Not today and not this month. There is a rule in this holding's own book that a second form produces nothing on purpose, because a body that asks twice has told a district office that asking works, and the first form was answered in four days and the second one would not be answered at all, and we would learn nothing and the office would learn that a body with no charter can still be got to move. So it sits there. And what sits there is a condition of the Crown that was going to be tested on whoever held this node on a day, and the day has been and gone, and nobody was hurt, and that is not a mercy. That is a hole in a form that a person who finds it in four years is going to be astonished by, and I would rather it were a hole we knew about."

"It is a hole we know about," said Dunmar Fitch, "and I have entered the knowing, and I am not going to enter the four years, because a body that enters what it is afraid of has started keeping records for somebody else."

---

The notice came up the fen road on the second, a Tuesday, on the office's paper, and it was four lines and correct.

*Water-supply bylaw, second part. To the holder of a node, or to any person in occupation of the ground. The node at Lantern Reach is not shown to be maintained. The district may at any time make arrangements for its maintenance, and may do so without notice to any person not in occupation of the ground.*

That is the language of the eighteenth of a month four months back, again, and fifteen weeks on, and it is the same language and not one word of it has changed, and nobody in the yard was a villain about it, and nobody said anything for about a minute, and then the man of about seventy said the thing that made the rest of it ordinary.

"Put it on the door," he said. "And put it on the same nails as the schedule, and put it on the left, because that is where a notice goes, and if it is going on that door at all then it is going on the left where a man can find it without reading past the schedule."

Lissa Vale read it out, and Dunmar Fitch entered it, and a person read it back, and it went on the well house door on the left of the schedule, and the two of them are on that door together and they are the only two things on it.

Nobody in the yard said the district had done it wrong. The node was not shown to be maintained. It was not. There was no charter, and there was no appointment, and the compost line had read *nothing put in, none available* for three months, and the return main was twenty-two rods of eight inch against a specification of forty-one of twelve, and nobody was going to come and look at any of it, and the district had said so in four lines and was entitled to.

"Can they shut the head," said Jonas Byre. "Not today. They can any time they like and they do not have to tell us they are going to."

"They can," said Tova Reed. "There is a form for it somewhere in that office and it is a good form and the man who brings it will be polite. And if they bring it, then the seed house has no water and the bed is at three and a half degrees and the tin under the ledger is a hundred and two days at risk, and that is what four lines of correct paper does to a body with no charter, and nobody in that office will enjoy it and it will not be their fault and they will be able to say so in writing." She squared the notice to the edge of the step with her own hand, which she does to paper and not to posts. "So write down that we have worked out what the four lines are for, and that we are not going to pretend we have not, and that the answer to it is compost and money and neither of which we have, and that is all the answer there is and I would rather it was on the page now than be a surprise at the first hard frost."

The pump kept running. Nothing was sealed. The head stayed as it had been for eleven weeks. And the leak went on being measured at the seventh hour, twice, with two rules, by a woman who has no appointment to do it, and the fen did not use the expiry to stop measuring it, and the entry says why.

And then, at about the eleventh hour, two men with a cart stopped on the road, and this is the whole of the second, and it is the first time in eleven weeks that anybody has stopped on that road for a reason that was not a question about a bill.

They were a chalk cart out of Fennmere, and the carter had been past the gate four times in a season and had never once seen anybody stand still at it, and he said so, and then he said the other thing.

"There is a sheet of paper nailed up over that door," he said, "and I have been going past it for a month and there was never anybody reading out loud, and this morning there was a woman reading out loud and she was reading a column that is empty."

"It is not empty, sir," said Lissa Vale, from the step. "It is empty and it has been read sixteen times and the number is written under the form. Would you like the number?"

"Yes," said the carter.

So she read him the number, and the number of the readings of the return column, and he stood at the gate and listened to the whole of it, and his mate stood at the cart and did not come over, and the second man asked one question, which was what a return column was, and Jonas Byre told him, and told him about the bay, and told him about the number cut into the board over the bay, and the carter looked at the board and then at the schedule and then at the notice on the left of the door, and neither man said anything about the notice.

"Has anybody come from the office to hear it," said the carter at the end.

"No," said Marek Vale.

"Then who is it for," said the carter.

And that is the question the fen has to answer on the third of this month, and the answer is the whole of what a body with no charter has, and nobody in this district has ever put it in a book.

"It is for us," said Tova Reed, at the counting-house end of the yard. "That is the honest answer and it is a bad one. We are the only two ends of this route in this district that can be party to each other, because we have nothing. A charter is the only thing that can be served, and refused, and enforced, and we have not got one, and she has not got one, and there is no form in this district that would let either of us be made to answer for anything. So the whole of this arrangement stands on the fact that nothing about it can be enforced. That is not a foundation. It is the absence of a floor. But it is what we have, and it is the only kind of promise in this arrangement that she can walk away from without a reason and without a notice, and that is the reason I did not strike out clause five on Saturday."

Lissa Vale said that the returning column had been read empty sixteen times and that a carter had asked for the number, and that the district had sent nobody, and that both of those were now in the book, and that she did not know which of them was the answer the fen had wanted and had spent a season finding out whether a book anybody may read is a thing anybody reads.

"So the fen is going to keep reading it out," she said, "on the first and on the middle and on the last, to anybody who comes, and the number of the readings goes in, and the day nobody comes the number still goes up, and I would like somebody to write down that that is the decision and not the mood, because a mood is worth nothing and costs nothing and I have been the reader of this book long enough to know the difference."

"Enter it as a decision," said Dunmar Fitch, "and put the hour on it, and put the two strangers on it with the hour, because a carter and a chalk wagon are the only people who have ever asked this holding a question about a column and they are not of this body and they do not vote and they were here."
