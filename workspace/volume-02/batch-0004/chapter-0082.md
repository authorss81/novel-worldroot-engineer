# Chapter 82

## The First Crate

The declared capacity went into the schedule on the twenty-fifth, in the trade's words, and the trade's words are not the fen's, and Marek Vale made that plain in the yard before he wrote a word of it.

"The fen is not going to name it," he said. "Mr Renner named it at a gate on the fifteenth and I put his sentence in this holding's book with the hour on it and it is his. If the fen writes its own sentence on the same thing, then in four counties there will be two sentences and a printer will take whichever one pays better. So the title of the form stands as it stands, and the capacity is written in the trade's words, which is: a seed-route bridge carries a stated load on a stated day from one keeper to another keeper, and it has a return, and the return is entered separately and is not part of the load."

He wrote the declared figure under it, on the morning of the twenty-fifth, in the fifth column, in the yard, and the man of about fifty read it aloud before it was initialled because the rule of the twentieth of last month says a person reads an entry and a person is anybody who comes.

"Capacity, declared," he said. "Thirty crates a month out and thirty a month back. Being a cart a day on the working days of a month. The return is entered in the seventh column and is not part of this figure."

That went in at the ninth hour on the twenty-fifth and it is on the well house door now, and it was true when it was written.

The bridge's works went up on the same day, and they are the whole of what a seed-route bridge is made of at this end. Eleven willow sets driven along the north ditch of the seed house ground, with a fiber bed twenty-two yards between them, cut out of the ditch-head scour and laid the way a fiber bed is laid. Four new posts and a re-lashed roof on the compost bay. A board on two stakes at the north gate with the schedule nailed to it and a second board under it, empty, for a copy, and the second board is not nailed to anything yet, because there is no copy at the other end and the fen would not nail an empty board up and call it a place a person can arrive at.

The withy for the eleven sets came off the south embankment windbreak, and the withy for the four posts came off the same, and Ilsa Kern cut all of it, and somebody said the word *windbreak* in the yard on the twenty-fifth and nobody said anything after it.

---

Coll Renner came up the fen road on the twenty-sixth, a Wednesday, at about the tenth hour, with his own clip in his hand and no case, and he came to see a set of willow and not a person, and he said so at the gate.

"I am not here about the form and I am not here about the route and I have read the two hundred pages twice since the fourteenth and I want to tell you that the last page is the best page of the two hundred and I have said that to three people who did not print it," he said. "I am here because your engineer wrote to me on Friday and asked me to be in this yard on the twenty-sixth at the tenth hour, and he did not say why, and I have not asked, and I have spent two days not asking, which is the hardest thing I have done since I came up this road in the spring with a bead in a fist-sized parcel."

The use-log line was written before anybody touched anything, on the page, in Lissa Vale's hand, and read back, and Marek read it back after her. It was the twelfth line. The twenty-sixth, the tenth hour and forty minutes, the north ditch of the seed house ground, the lashing of the eleventh willow set, the instrument laid against green withy, licensed man present and named and the roll named, the contact tested before the contact and the reading of the test written down first in Mr Renner's own hand, and nothing whatever about what came out.

Renner tested it with a plain wire gauge and wrote the reading of the test down before he let the instrument near anything, and then he fitted his own clip.

The purpose in the use-log line was *the return*. That was in the line, because Lissa Vale writes the purpose and the purpose is what the instrument is being asked and there is no other column for it.

It went, and it took nine seconds, and Marek Vale could not account for the nine any better than he had accounted for the eleven at the head of the node on the twelfth of this month, or the four on a cut face at Weir Row, and he said so out loud afterward, twice, and both times it went in the book.

The four inches along the inside of his forearm were hot before it was over and stayed hot for the rest of the day, and his hand from the wrist down was no use to him from the second hour, and Renner carried the case for him down the seed house steps because he could not get the latch with two fingers.

It said three things, the way it says three things, and not in a sentence anybody would want.

> A return is a second keeper, not a second cart.
>
> The first keeper gives. The second keeper sends something back.
>
> If the second end sends nothing, the first end is a road.

Renner read them twice and shut the case, and did not read them out loud.

Marek asked him why not, and Renner gave the reason without any argument in it.

"Because I read three lines out loud in a yard in this room a season ago and had a foreman standing at the end of the table who cannot read," he said. "You had it entered that I did it in front of six people and that I had thought about it for two days. I have thought about it for a season. The lines are in your book or they are in nobody's, and I have made up my mind that I would rather be the second, and that I do not want to be a man who says a thing twice in a place where a man I employ is standing, because the third time I say it in a yard it will be because I want somebody to agree with me."

Nothing was written down. Marek asked Lissa Vale whether it could be, and she gave him the reason in one sentence and it was the reason she had given twice before, and Renner said that he had heard her give it twice and that he now said it to himself about a copper collar.

Then he did the licensed man's part, which is to say what a man with a rule can check about any of it, and what he said was that none of it could be checked, that he was not going to enter it, that the use log records a contact and an hour and the roll and nothing else, and that if anybody in this fen ever wanted to know whether the eleventh willow set was worth a contact on the twenty-sixth, the answer in the book was the twelfth line and the answer in the book was the twelfth line whatever the contact said.

---

They measured the capacity on the afternoon of the twenty-sixth, at the well house door, in public, on the district's own discipline, which is the rule this holding has been using since a test at the head on the twentieth of last month and which is the only thing it has ever done that the district also does: the load goes in before the reading.

Lissa Vale wrote the load in the fifth column first, in her own hand, and read it back to the yard.

"Capacity, declared, the twenty-fifth, the ninth hour: thirty crates a month out and thirty a month back, being a cart a day on the working days of a month. The return is entered in the seventh column and is not part of this figure."

Then she ruled a line under it and wrote in the fifth column: *load entered before the reading. The following was read at the well house door on the twenty-sixth, in the yard, in daylight, and is the whole of the measurement.* And then the yard read it out, one item at a time, and Dunmar Fitch wrote it and a person read it back after him, and the person was the man of about fifty, and he read all of it twice.

"One. The first break will take one loaded cart in a week and will not take a second in the same place inside a week, and it will not take a loaded cart back at any time, and this is the ground and not a man. Two. A day's cart and two carters' men is fourteen shillings. Three. A levy at four times what it was is eighteen pounds and it came in at thirteen pounds four. Four. The seventh column is empty. Five. The bed is at three and a half degrees and nothing has gone into the return main for two months and the specification is four hundred hundredweight a month and the shortfall is three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight. Six. The return main is twenty-two rods of eight inch against a specification of forty-one of twelve. Seven. There is one cart available in this holding, borrowed, for one day a month, on the days when the seed house is not doing something else, which it is always doing."

Then Marek said the reading and the reading was six, and Lissa Vale wrote it in, and the man of about fifty read it back and initialled it, and it is on the well house door in the fen's own hand where anybody may stand and look at it.

"Capacity, measured, the twenty-sixth: six crates out in a month and none back. Six is a fifth of thirty. A fifth of what was declared is what a cart that goes once and cannot come back and a seventh column that is empty will carry, and it is entered in the same hand and on the same sheet and under the figure it corrects, and no person has to go looking for it."

Somebody in the yard laughed, and it was not a good laugh and it was not at anybody, and Ilsa Kern said the thing that made the yard stop.

"They put the small figure under the big one on the door," she said, "and they read it out. In this district. In eleven weeks, at this door, a schedule of damage of sixty pounds ten went up with a fourteen-day limit on it, and a form with a box in it went to the low quarter, and every single thing this district has ever put on a door has been a thing that was correct and not a thing that was honest. And that one is on the door, and it says out loud that it is a fifth of what it promised, and anybody can walk up to it and count the crates themselves."

And Jonas Byre stood at the back and said nothing for a quarter of an hour, and the fen waited, because he had objected on the fifteenth and again on the twenty-fourth and there was a thing coming, and it came at last and it was the thing nobody expected from him.

"That is the reason I have been waiting for," he said. "I have been waiting nine days for this. I have been telling that room since the fifteenth that a schedule is a promise anybody can read and that a promise anybody can read and cannot be kept is worse than no promise, and I said the people who read this door have been let down by a correct form before. And it is here now. It is on the door. It is a fifth of what it said it was, in the fen's own hand, and the reason is on the sheet, and the man who put it up is the man who wrote the big number four days ago and did not come down here to soften it when it turned out to be a fifth."

He took his hand off the bar.

"So I am going to close the book," he said, "and I cannot, and I would like somebody to say why not, because I have had it in my hand all afternoon and there is nothing to hold it with, and I would like it said in the yard by somebody who is not me."

Nobody said it for about a minute, and then the woman with the shilling said it, and she said it in one sentence, and it was her standing and nobody had ever tried to take it from her.

"Because you can only close a book by writing in it," she said, "and if you write in it you are the man who closed it, and the man who closed it is a name in a book anybody may read, and we spent a month finding that out."

Then Dunmar Fitch entered, with the hour: that a member of the body had said on the well house steps on the twenty-sixth that he would close the book, and had not, and had asked that the reason be entered, and that the reason was given by a person who was not a clerk, and that he took nothing else down.

And the return column was read empty on the evening of the twenty-sixth, at the well house door, to the man of about seventy, who had come to look and had stayed, and who asked for his initial to be put against the number forty-one, which is the number of days the bay had stood empty on the fifteenth, and who had counted it himself on the twenty-fourth and was right, and whose initial went against nothing, which is his standing, and which cost the holding nothing.

The number of the readings of the seventh column was written under the form on the same night, in the clerk's hand, and it is ten, and it is the first time a number has gone on that sheet since the title, and Dunmar Fitch wrote it and could not make it into a sentence and did not try, and Lissa Vale read the figure back to him and then read the words under it as well, because a number in a book is not a record of anything until somebody has said what it is a number of.

"Ten," she said. "Ten times this column has been read and it has been empty ten times, and it was empty on the seventeenth when the form was ruled and it is empty on the twenty-sixth, and nobody has put anything in it and nobody has blamed anybody for not putting anything in it. That is the tenth number of its kind and I would like the next one to be the day something goes in it, and I would like whoever is here that day to notice that it is a Tuesday."
