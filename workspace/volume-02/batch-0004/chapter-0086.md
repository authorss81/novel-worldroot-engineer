# Chapter 86

## The Return

The answer to the trust's question went in on the morning of the third, at the ninth hour, at the middle of the table, in Tova Reed's own hand, and it is the eleventh line against the trust's own interest, and it took eleven minutes and it took two days of the worst thinking she has done in eleven years of keeping a line in a tin in a drawer.

The answer was that this holding would hold the trust's seed for one season, and that it had been holding it since the ninth of a month three months back, and that it had not been asked until the twelfth of last month, and that the trust could not have got it away before the season turned in any case because the March road has been out at two places since the fourteenth of last month and one of them is a quarter mile of running sand.

And then she did the arithmetic, out loud, and the arithmetic got worse.

"The bed is at three and a half degrees," she said. "It has been losing about a tenth of a degree every nine or ten days since the first of a month three months back and a soil iron cannot read the tenth of a degree, so that is a figure I cannot defend and I am going to use it anyway because it is the number I have. The compost line has read nothing put in, none available, for three months. The return main is three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight short of four hundred a month, which is seventy-nine pounds sixteen at four shillings, and the specification is a Crown document and this body has no standing to promise it and is not going to."

"Six trays of the March's wet line and four of the dry line and a tin, in a bed this holding cannot feed, held on behalf of a counting house that cannot pay carriage, that cannot pay compost, and that has said in a letter nine hundred miles off that a fault of that kind cannot be corrected by paying anybody and can only be corrected by saying it out loud in a book that anybody may read."

She put her pencil down.

"So I have written the word *gift* in my own book and I have struck it out, and I would like to say why, and the reason is that a bed at three and a half degrees that this holding cannot feed, given to a body that has asked nothing and been asked nothing, is a favour, and a favour goes one way. And I have spent eleven years telling seed houses that a line kept in one book is a line that can be got at, and I sent the March's line to eleven grounds and wrote no list, and the last wrong I entered was mine and it was a large one, and the worst of that wrong was not that it was wrong. It was that it was a gift. Eleven households kept a dying line for two years and were never asked what they got back and never told, and I let that be the shape of the thing, and now I am proposing to do it again in a bed."

Nobody in that room had anything to say for about a minute, and the woman with the shilling had plenty and was given the floor because Dunmar Fitch gives the floor.

"You have got my children's seed in that bed," she said. "That is all I have got to say and I have said it. Four children and one of them has not had a proper winter in four years, and there is a woman's rye in that bed from a counting house nine hundred miles off that I have never seen, and the bed is going, and I do not know what comes out of it in the spring and neither do you, and if nothing comes out of it then next season there is nothing to sow, and that is not a thing I am frightened of, it is a thing I have watched happen to people. I am not asking you to take it out. I would not know where to put it. I am asking that it be in the book in my words and that nobody come at me afterwards and say that I did not raise it."

"It is in the book in your words and the hour on it," said Dunmar Fitch, "and it is read back, and you did not ask to be thanked and there is nothing in this yard that could thank you."

The man of about fifty said the thing that made the fourth of the month, and he said it standing up, and he was honest in a way that two people in the room found hard to sit through.

"I am the man who voted against keeping the book. I am going to say the honest thing about a return and I have thought about it for two days and I have got it wrong twice. I want to say first that I do not want it to be true. I want the seventh column to be full of compost and I want it to be full by the spring, and I have wanted that every day since the fifteenth of last month, and there is not one hundredweight of it coming from anywhere in this district, and if I stand here and make a beautiful speech about a return I am a man who has been given a good day. So I will say what is true and you may all decide what it is worth."

"Everything coming back down this road goes to Cray."

He said it flatly, and he looked at the second end of the schedule on the door and not at anybody, and the fen let it stand for about four seconds.

"I have read the second refusal. She is not going to be in that column and she told us why and she was right. So whatever goes in the seventh column this month is not for her, and if we put it there and the schedule gets read out at that door by somebody in a year, the woman at the other end of it will know exactly what it is for and so will any stranger who stops at the gate, and that is the fen being honest, and the fen being honest is a thing we have voted on, and I voted against it and I am voting for it again this morning and I would like that noted as well."

Jonas Byre had said it once already, on the twenty-fourth of last month, standing in the yard at dusk with a bar over his shoulder, and he said the second half of it now and did not pretend it was new.

"I said a bridge with no return is a road with crates on it, and Mr Vale said it and not me, and I want to say the other half of it and I have not said it. A road with crates on it is not a bad road. It is a road. People walk on it. It goes somewhere. It just does not come back." He put his hand flat on the table, which he has done twice. "And a body that has spent a season finding that out ought to be the last body in the world to invent a return to make the road look finished."

And Tova Reed sat down and picked the pencil up and said the sentence that is the whole of the third of this month.

"A return does not have to be compost," she said. "I have been in this room for two days treating compost as the only thing that can come back down a road, and it is not, and I have known that for eleven years and I have been using it to avoid the harder arithmetic.

"What comes back down a path is whatever goes along it and comes back. Not a kindness. Not a gift. Not a mood. A weight, at two ends, on a day, with a name against it." She took the sheet of her own and turned it round. "Here is the return, and it is the only one this holding has, and it is arithmetic and it is not in anybody's favour.

"The March's dry line was cut to eleven grounds in the eleventh month two seasons back and the eleventh of them is this fen, and there is no column anywhere for it, and it is in a tin in a counting house nine hundred miles off and this holding has the only other part of it that anybody knows of, in a tray, four inches high, in the seed house, sown before the sowing closed on the twenty-ninth of last month. That sowing was on this ground. That ground is this fen's. And when it is lifted in the autumn the seed off it is the March's line, grown in this fen, and it goes back down the March road to Cray in a crate with a sheet on it, weighed at this end and weighed at that end, and signed by a person at each end, and counted."

"That is a return," she said. "It goes both ways. It can be checked by a rule, which is the only kind that has ever been worth anything in this book. It is not a hundredweight of compost and it is not money and it costs this holding nothing that it was not already spending, and it is the first thing that has come back down anything in this holding in a season, and I would like the seventh column filled in with it in plain words and I would like the word *return* left off it, because *return* is a trade word and this is a sowing."

Lissa Vale read the column's entry back twice before it was initialled, and then she did the thing that is her rule and has been her rule twice this season, and she said it out loud for the third time in a season, in a room with eight people in it, and there has been no licensed man in that room since the sixth of a month two months back.

"There is nothing in that a man with a rule cannot look at twice and get the same answer," she said. "It can be weighed at two ends. It can be counted. It can be signed. It is the first line anybody has offered me in a season and I am going to put it in the book and I am not going to add anything to it, and I want that written down as the reason I added nothing."

Then the fen entered the two halves of it, and Dunmar Fitch read them both back, and the reader for the second was the man of about fifty.

The first: *the standing line is unchanged. Nothing put into the return main this month. None available. The shortfall three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight, seventy-nine pounds sixteen, carried forward and not discharged. The bed at three and a half degrees. The graft fed about a twentieth of what it was designed to be fed. The leak at five-eighths and not mended. The return column of the seed-route schedule filled on the third of this month with a sowing and weighed at two ends, and it is entered as a return and not as a hundredweight, and no person in this yard may call it a solution to the three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight, and I would like the clerk to give that its own line and not put it inside a sentence, because it is the only line in this book that nobody is to be able to miss.*

The second: *that the thing in the seventh column goes to Cray, and that the second end of the route is eleven households, keeper not named, and that the second end's column is empty and stays empty this season, and that on the twenty-eighth of last month a person at that end said the road is for somebody else and she was right, and that this holding has entered the fact of her refusal and not its terms.*

And then the yard went out to the bay, because that is what a yard does at the end of a morning like that, and both boards were on the north wall of the bay where anybody can read them from the gate: the big one, with RETURN cut in Jonas Byre's hand and the number under it, and the small one, with the same word in smaller letters and the same number on it, which Ilsa Kern cut in the same week and nailed up herself the same evening, against Marek Vale's standing that nobody was to be allowed to take a board down and put it back a day later.

The number was fifty-eight, and she counted it herself before anybody said it, and she had been counting it every day, and she said so, and then she said the thing that goes in the book under it.

"I have been standing at that bay since the eighteenth of a month two months back," she said, "and I have put one board up and then a second one under it, and neither of them is about compost, and both of them are about a road, and there is a column in a schedule on a door that has a line in it now for the first time in its life, and it is in a different hand from mine, and it is about a sowing, and I have cut fifty-eight days out of a board for it and I would like somebody to tell me that those two things are not the same thing and then I would like somebody else to tell me the truth, and the truth is that they are the same thing and I do not know what to do about it and I am going back to the ditch."

She went back to the ditch. Dunmar Fitch entered the two boards and the number and the hour and read it back, and the reader was the man of about seventy, who had come in for the morning and had stayed, and who asked for his initial to be put against nothing, which is his standing, and which cost the holding nothing.

The letter came up the fen road on the fourth, in the afternoon post, and it was from Marrowfield, and it was four lines, and Ottoline Fray had said in a yard on the seventh of a month two months back that she would send a line of her own in a month and did not know what she would do with it when it came, and she had written it, and it was not a sale and it was not a discount and it was not a bargain of any kind.

*Four lines and a sheet. The sheet is a cutting sheet off my own book and it is the first one I have ever written and I have had it eleven weeks and I did not know what to do with it and then I found out what to do with it, which was to send it to somebody who has a reader who reads hands and not names. The four lines are mine, out of a lot I grew off a cutting two seasons ago, and I do not know what they are, and I have put in the third column what I have been told the third column is for and I want to know whether I have got it right, because I have had four hundred lots read on a device and never once been told what a cutting sheet is supposed to say, and I am told by a man at Fennmere that there is no column for it and that this is a fault of my trade and not of my paper, and I have thought about that for a fortnight and I have decided that the fault of my trade can be put on my paper in my own hand and that is how a person starts, and I am not going to be helped and I am not going to be refused, because a person who is helped does not learn it and a person who is refused stops, and I am forty-one and I have four hundred smallholders and eleven years, and I would rather be corrected in a book than corrected nowhere.*

Lissa Vale read it out in the yard on the fourth, twice, and a person read it back after her, and it went in the book as received, and nobody in the fen answered it that day, and Dunmar Fitch entered that nobody had answered it and that it had not gone on the Thursday cart, and that a body with no charter and no standing has no method for a thing like this and that the absence of a method is entered as well as the letter.
