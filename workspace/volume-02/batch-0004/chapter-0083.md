# Chapter 83

## The Dry End

They decided it in eleven minutes on the morning of the twenty-seventh, and the reason for the decision was six weeks old and had been written in a book on the eleventh of a month two months back by a man with a bar in his hand.

Nobody from this holding goes and collects. A holding that is owed a survey cannot also be the body that goes and collects it, because the collecting is the part that makes it theirs. A steward who goes to the far end of a route she is party to cannot afterwards be a person who received anything there.

"Mr Vale is the engineer of record and he wrote the terms and he cannot go," said Anneke Roos.

"I am not going because of that," said Marek. "I am not going because if I stand on that ground and look at it I will have seen it, and everything this holding says about it for the rest of its life will be a thing a man who has been there said about a place, and the far end would never be able to make me put the book down."

"And I am not going," said Tova Reed, "because I am a party to six of the terms and I would be a person who received a thing at a place and did not write it down and cannot be cross-examined about it, and I have never wanted a document more in my life."

"Which leaves us," said Jonas Byre.

"Which leaves you, and Ilsa, and the man of about fifty," said Dunmar Fitch, "and it leaves Lissa here, and I am going to enter the reason for that separately because it is hers and not mine."

Lissa Vale said: "I am the reader. The reader reads. If I am thirteen miles off in a place with no charter and there is an entry in this book, then there is no person in it who is reading entries, and the whole of the twentieth of last month falls over, and I would rather not be the reason. I will read every one of the three of you out to the yard when you come back and I will read it twice."

The man of about seventy said he would go and was not asked, and Anneke Roos said no, and gave the reason that the cart takes three and he takes two and there are three men already.

They took the cart and the plank and a chain, and got over the first break at about the second hour of the afternoon, and went on to a mile beyond the low dry, and slept at the bothy of Wil Tam, mender of implements, who charged them two shillings for the floor and would not charge for the fire and who asked whether the second break had been walked, and was told it had, and said that a man who has walked the second break and come back is a man who has been somewhere most men in this trade have only heard of.

---

Wrenlow is eleven plots in a ring about two hundred yards across, on the low dry, and there is no gate because there has never been anything at Wrenlow worth closing.

There is a well at the low end and it was in. There is a building of two rooms and a stove and a table, and there is a fence of thorn and hazel round the whole of it that a body has cut and re-cut for as long as anybody here can remember, and eleven households live in the two rooms and in four of the plots, and each household has a plot, and the plots are about a sixteenth of an acre each and they are not good ground.

Wenna Roke was in the ninth plot with a hoe when they came in, and she is thirty-nine, and she put the hoe down and came out to the fence and did not come to the gate, because there is no gate.

"You are the fen," she said. "The one that printed itself."

"Yes," said Ilsa Kern.

"Then I will tell you what I have got to say to a fen and I will say all of it and I will not say it twice, and if you have come to ask me for a thing I do not have it and I am not going to be polite about it." She had the list in her hand. She had been carrying it while she hoed, which she said afterward was because the wind takes paper.

"It is that one sheet," she said. "It has got eleven lines on it and one is each household, and against each one it says what the household will sow this season and what it had last season, and the household that is me says the same twice. And it is the only list of this place that has ever been made, and I made it, and it is on a sheet of lining paper with a stone on it, and it has been the only record of this place since the year before I came here."

"And there is no column for where any of it came from," said Jonas Byre.

He said it in the voice he reads in, and he said it because he had been looking at it, and Wenna Roke turned round and looked at him properly for the first time.

"No," she said. "There is no column for where any of it came from, and there has never been one, and I have had eleven households come to me in nine years with a sack of something out of a cart, through a man who wanted to be helpful, and not one of them said where it was from, and I did not ask, because I am not a person to whom a man in a cart has to give an account of himself, and because on the day I do ask, that is the day the sacks stop coming."

"How long have you been keeping a line going," said the man of about fifty, who had not said anything for the whole of the walk and had said this.

"Two winters," said Wenna Roke. "A short one out of the ground under the north embankment nine miles east of here that nobody in this district will sow, and I keep it in all eleven plots because a line in one plot is a line that dies with a bad month, and a line in eleven plots is a line that can lose nine and keep two. And I have the list of the eleven plots and no column for where it came from, and I have never once known, and I am not going to know this year either, and that is the ordinary state of my life and I am not complaining about it."

Tova Reed was nine hundred miles off and was not in this room, and the room in Fennmere learned about the list on the twenty-eighth, and what Marek Vale said at the seed house table about it that night is in the book, and it is four lines long, and the fourth of them is: *it is not hers and I am not going to stand in this room and tell her it is mine, and I have been the other end of a return for eleven years and I have never once written down where anything came from either, and she has hit a hole that I have been living in.*

Then the two refusals, and the man of about fifty took out the terms, six clauses, and the second of them struck through, and offered it across the fence.

Wenna Roke did not take it.

"No," she said.

"May it be entered," said Jonas Byre.

"It may not," she said, and Ilsa Kern noticed that she had used the fen's own words, and had not said so, and had said them because she had been in this book once before or had been told about it, and the fen never found out which.

"I have never signed a paper in my life," said Wenna Roke. "Not one. Not for the district, not for anybody, not for a man who wanted to be helpful. And if I sign that one I am in it. And if I am in it then the day I want out of it there is no day, and there is nobody to say to, and I would rather be a woman who never signed anything, which is the only thing I have got, and I would rather have it than a copy of your record in my room that I cannot answer on and cannot get rid of."

"That is fair," said the man of about fifty.

"It is not fair, it is only true, and there is a difference and you will find it out." She put the list under her arm. "And I am not going to put anything in your seventh column either, and that is the second thing and it is the one you actually came for."

"We did not come for that," said Jonas Byre.

"You came for it in a cart," said Wenna Roke, "and you will not get it, and here is the reason and it is the reason your door is empty. You have written a column on your gate and you have not filled it in ten times and you have said so yourself in your own hand where anybody can read it, and that is the first true thing I have heard off a piece of paper in nine years, and I would like to say the rest of it to somebody who is not a fen. The thing that comes back down your road goes to a counting house nine hundred miles off. I have worked that out between here and your break and I have got it right, because the March road is the March road and the March's line is the March's line and a thing that goes back to Cray does not stop here on the way. So I am the end of a road, and the road is for somebody else, and I am not going to be the first thing in a column to make a fen's door look right."

Nobody in the yard of that place said anything for a while, and the wind came off the low dry and took the paper out of the man's of-about-fifty's hand and he folded it and put it in his coat, and it was the most useful thing anybody did all day.

And then Wenna Roke said the thing that decided the whole of the rest of the season, and she said it standing at her own fence, and it was about eight words long.

"You may write the crates on your list," she said. "Leave my name off it. Whoever is standing here when it comes, they can put a mark on it and I do not mind a mark. I have been a name on a piece of paper twice in my life and both times it was another person's hand on it, and I am not going to start."

The second end of the schedule is *eleven households, keeper not named*, and it has been that since the seventeenth of this month, and she did not have to be told, and Ilsa Kern has said in this book that she does not know whether that was luck or whether the fen wrote the six weeks early for a reason it could not put in a book.

---

They came back on the twenty-eighth, at about the fourth hour, over the first break empty, and read out three times in the yard, and the third reading was by the man of about fifty, who had not been able to say one word to a woman for two days and who read the whole of it including the two refusals and including the terms, and Lissa Vale read the second refusal back a second time because it was the ninth line of something and the ninth line is read twice.

The fen entered the first refusal as the fact of a refusal and not its terms, and the second refusal the same way, and the clerk's hand has the hour on both, and under the first of them, in the engineer's own hand, because the engineer's hand was the one that was at the gate and the fen's standing is that these lines are in that hand and not in anybody else's:

*this entry records the fact of a refusal and not its terms, and the terms are not held by this body.*

And then there was the thing, and the thing was a mistake, and it is entered as a mistake, and it cost Tova Reed a day and a half and four hundred miles of her thinking and it is the only thing in this batch she has got wrong.

She had a second copy of the terms made on the morning of the twenty-seventh. Four sheets, copied by a boy at Fennmere, the second clause struck through and the strike copied as a strike, and she gave it to Jonas Byre at the gate at the eighth hour and told him it was for the woman, and told him it was unasked for, and said that if it came back unopened he was to burn it and enter that it had been burned and not return it, which is a sentence she has used before and has never once been glad of using.

It came back.

It came back into the yard on the twenty-eighth in the middle of the reading out, in the inside pocket of Jonas Byre's coat, folded twice, and Jonas Byre said he had put it in his coat at the fence and the woman had not taken it and had said two sentences, and he could give the two sentences, and he was asked to and he gave them.

"She said, don't send me anything with my name at the top of it," he said. "And she said, if you want to keep a thing going, come back with the thing and not with a paper."

Nobody made anything of that for about four minutes, and then Tova Reed said the whole thing herself, standing up off the bench, in front of the yard, without being asked to and in about ninety seconds.

"I sent it," she said. "I knew better. I have spent a season being the person in this room who does not put a document in front of a body that cannot examine one, and on Wednesday morning I folded four sheets and gave them to a man to carry thirteen miles, and I did it because I was afraid that the reason she refused was the clause and not the fen, and I wanted the reason to be tested, and I tested it on a woman instead of on myself. I have entered it against the trust's interest for the tenth time and this is not against the trust's interest, this is against mine, and I would like it entered as a fault and not as a piece of good practice."

Anneke Roos said, from the doorway, that she had been asked on the Wednesday to check the paper before it went and had not been, and that she had assumed a steward checked her own paper, and that she is going to say the rest of that sentence to herself out loud in a book at some point and not today.

The four sheets went into the fen's own book as the fen's own, dated the twenty-seventh, with the strike on clause two copied as a strike, and the fact of the return entered, and the two sentences Jonas Byre was given entered in his words with the hour, and the terms are in this holding's book and in no other book in the world and are held by nobody at the far end.
