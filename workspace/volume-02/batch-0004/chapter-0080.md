# Chapter 80

## The Terms Of A Bridge

Marek Vale drafted the terms of the seed-route on the twenty-first, alone, at the north end of the seed house bench, over the better part of a day, and the draft was good. He said so himself in the yard that evening, and he said it in a way that made the man of about fifty put down a bar he had been holding, because Marek Vale does not say a thing about his own work unless he means it.

"It is the best piece of drafting I have ever done and I have been angry about it for about an hour," he said. "Six clauses. Every one of them is answerable. Every one of them can be carried out by a man with a rule. Three of them I would put in front of a district and I think two of those three would survive the district. I have written it in the plainest English I have and there is not one word in it that means two things, and I have read it four times and I have found one thing wrong with it and I am not going to fix the one thing."

"Tell us the one thing," said Anneke Roos.

"The one thing is clause two and I am not going to fix it until somebody who has a better reason than mine has had a go at it, and I have got a feeling the somebody is going to be sitting at that end of the table and is not going to like it."

Clause one: what is carried. A written schedule of crates, crate by crate, with the number of the crate, the lot and the keeper of it in the keeper's own words, the weight, the hour it left the first end, the hour it arrived at the second end, and a signature at each end against the hour.

Clause two: the record. A copy of this record is kept at each end of the route. Each end answers on it, in the ordinary way, within the month.

Clause three: the return. The return is entered in the seventh column. Where the seventh column is empty, the route goes and nothing comes back, and both ends may say so in the ordinary way and neither has to excuse it.

Clause four: the book. One book. It is not kept in two places. Either end may ask to have any entry in it read aloud, the reader is a person, a person is anybody who comes, and the reading is before the initial and not after.

Clause five: stopping. Either end may stop the route on a day's notice by writing it in the book. No reason is required. None is to be entered.

Clause six: the term. A season, and at the end of the season the route goes on by a new writing or it stops, and stopping needs no reason, no paper, and no notice, and a route that stops is not a route that has failed.

Anneke Roos read all six clauses twice at the seed house table on the evening of the twenty-first and said that she had found nothing to object to in five of them, and that she wanted it entered that she had said that in a room with the engineer of record sitting at it, and that she had been looking for a fault in all six and had not found one, and that she was suspicious of a document that cannot be faulted and would rather say so at the time than afterwards.

Then she said, from the doorway, that clause five was the best sentence anybody had written in this building in eleven weeks, and that she wanted that entered too, with her name against it, and that she was not going to be thanked for it by either of them and that she would be annoyed if she were.

---

The reading was on the twenty-second, in the yard, in daylight, standing, with the book shut under Dunmar Fitch's hands and the form on the well house door behind them and the empty bay at the end of the yard with its number cut into the board.

Marek read all six clauses out in his own hand, in the flat voice he uses for a figure he has checked twice, and he read the drafting notes under them as well, because he had written the notes and the notes were the best of it, and the last note said: *this document is written so that a person who cannot read it can have it read to them and be certain that what they heard is what is in it. That is the whole of the work. The rest is arithmetic.*

Nobody had anything to say until the woman with the shilling asked what a copy of the record would be worth to the far end, and Marek said he did not know and had not thought about it, and she said that was the first honest answer he had given anybody in a fortnight and she would like it in the book.

And then Tova Reed took her case off the table, unlocked it in front of everybody the way she does, took out her pencil, and struck clause two out.

She did it in one line, from the first word to the last, and she did not scratch it out, and she put her hand flat on the page above where it had been.

"Clause two is gone," she said. "And I am going to say why in about two hundred words and I am going to say it to Mr Vale and not to the room, because he is the only person here who is going to be asked to carry it and because everybody else in this yard would agree with me in about nine words and I want him to have to argue."

She did not sit down.

"A copy of the record is kept at each end. Each end answers on it. That is two sentences and they are both ordinary and either of them would be in any document I have ever signed, and I have signed forty of them at a counting house and I would sign it again in a morning."

"It makes her a party. Not a witness. Not a person we have written to. A party. A body that keeps a copy of a record and answers on it has taken a step into a document and once a body is in a document it can be examined about the document, by anybody who has the document, and there is nothing in clause two that says the examination has to be fair, or has to be at a counter, or has to be on a form, or has to be put to her in a room with a line of type and not a discretion in it."

"And there is no holding number for her ground. Perrin Osse turned that form round on the counter on Thursday morning and put her finger on the third box and said the box is six digits and her ground has none, because ground this district carries in schedule is ground nobody holds. So a Crown form about her cannot be completed. It can only be sent with a hole in it. And a hole in a form is a thing a clerk has to answer for, and in nine years in this office the answer will be the same answer, and the answer will be a letter to a body that has a number, asking it about a place that has not got one. And the body that has the number is this holding. So the far end is not on the hook for a document. This holding is. And the whole of the document would be the document this holding makes a party to on her behalf, in her name, without asking her, about ground she did not choose to write to us about."

She put the pencil down.

"I was in a room on the first of this month with a clerk who had a line of type and a form and who read it to me and would not explain it, and I was not allowed to say anything back, and I went out and stood at a gate for ten minutes. That is the whole of what I know about it and it was enough. And I am not going to write a clause that puts a woman who kept seed for eleven households into a room like that because she is the only other keeper on this route, and because I would like an answer on the second clause when the seventh column is empty in the middle of the winter, and I would like the answer to be in a book she can walk away from."

The yard was very quiet, and the pump ran, and the lamp above the seed-house door was burning on its living bulb.

Marek Vale did not argue for a while, and when he did he argued the engineer's argument, which is the one he has, and he gave it cleanly and without heat, and he did not pretend it was a better one than hers.

"You are right about the standing and I cannot argue standing with you and I am not going to try," he said. "I am going to argue the load. Here is the risk and it is mine and I would like it in the book in these words.

"In clause three the seventh column says what comes back down the path. There are two ends. This end is a cooperative of eight people with an empty compost bay and a bed at three and a half degrees and a hundredweight a month that comes off forty households who do not have it. That end can say no to anything, and it will, because that is what a column is for, and it has said no three times in a season.

"That end has eleven households and no charter and no standing and no document and a person who has told us in a letter three lines long that the road is out at two places. If clause two goes, she cannot be asked for a crate that does not arrive. She cannot be asked to hold a copy. She cannot be asked to answer. Nothing about that arrangement can be made into an instrument, and if in the middle of the winter this holding puts something in the seventh column that it has not got, she will be the first to know it, and she will find out from the road, and she has no document, and there is nobody she can go to, and this holding will be right."

He stopped, and then he said the part that is the whole of the twenty-second.

"Neither of us can answer that for the other. You have got standing and I have got the load and they are two different things and I have watched you be right about standing for eleven weeks and I have watched me be right about the load in a pit on a Saturday, and there is no form in this district that takes both. So it goes in the book as it is, with both our names on the page and neither of us softening anything, and I am not going to move an inch on the risk and I am not asking you to move an inch on the standing, and if somebody reads this page in four years I would like it to say that two competent people looked at the same clause and each of them was right and neither of them was enough."

"Before it is entered. There is a second thing and I have been sitting on it for an hour and I am going to put it down because if I do not put it down this afternoon somebody will find it in a year and it will look as though I had hidden it. Clause two is the clause you have struck. Clause four is the one you have not struck, and clause four says one book, not kept in two places, and either end may ask to have any entry read aloud."

"That is the clause I care about most and it is the one I would not give up, and it is also the clause that puts her in a document exactly as much as clause two does. She is named in it as an end. She may ask to have a thing read. And on the day somebody wants to know what this holding has been saying about a settlement with no charter, the book that says it is the one book, and clause four is what makes her a party to every line in it, and clause four is in your hand as much as clause two is in mine."

"I know," said Tova Reed. "I have known it since the fourth of a month three months back and I have not said it because it does not have an answer and I am not going to invent one in front of you. Clause four is the price of this document and clause two was the attempt not to pay it. I struck two because two was a lie about what we were doing and I would not write a lie, and I am keeping four because four is the truth and I would rather be examinable than quiet. Put both of those in the book with the hour, and put them in that order, and put my name under the second one and not under the first."

"It goes in," said Dunmar Fitch.

"Enter it exactly like that," said Anneke Roos, "and I will initial it as an opinion, and I will initial it fast, and I would like the clause struck out to be entered as struck out with the hour, and not as amended."

"It is entered as struck out with the hour," said Tova Reed.

The terms went out with no enforceable term at the far end at all, and that fact was entered against this holding's own interest, in daylight, at the well house door, with the hour on it, and the clerk read it back and the reader was Jonas Byre, who read the whole of the six clauses with the second one struck through, and the strike, and the entry about the strike, and then read the twenty-second's paragraph about the load, twice, because he said the first time he had not taken in that anybody was going to write a thing down that made the engineer of record look like a man who had been outargued, and the second time he took it in, and initialled both.

What went in the book, in the clerk's hand and in her own, is this: *that the terms of a seed-route carry no enforceable term at the far end, and that the fen knows it, and has entered it against itself, and that no person in this yard has been thanked for it and that the fen's book has no column for being thanked and could not use one.*

Nobody thanked her. She gave a book of two hundred pages out of another body's money. She entered the largest wrong of her career against that body's own interest in three books and a printing and a road nine hundred miles long. She refused a lease on her own record in a yard at the ninth hour in the morning. She asked the man beside her to be the one who said the sentence about her own wrong out loud in front of seven hands, and he said it, and it is in the book, and he voted against her majority and put his own name against his own vote. And on Saturday she struck out the only clause in the document that would have protected her, in front of a yard, in daylight, on the grounds that a person with no standing must not be made a party to a paper, and she is right, and there is no clause in a charter or a fifth sheet or a book about a woman being thanked.
