# Chapter 78

## The Schedule

The form was ruled by hand on Monday the seventeenth, on a sheet of the seed house's own account paper, by Dunmar Fitch, with a pen and a straight edge and a great deal of swearing about the width of the last column, and it has no number on it, and that was the first argument anybody had about it.

"A form with a number is a form somebody has made a class of," said Anneke Roos, from the doorway, on the morning of the seventeenth, before there was anything on the table but the ruling. "Every form in this district with a number on it is a form somebody can go and get a second one of. If this one has a number on it, then in a year somebody in a printing office can print a hundred of them and put this holding's name at the top of all of them, and we will have built a form for other people. If it has no number on it, it is a piece of paper with rules on it, and a piece of paper with rules on it can be copied by anybody who has seen it, and a copy has to be copied honestly or it is a forgery and not a document."

"It is going to be copied by somebody inside a month," said Tova Reed. "A book that can be read can be copied. That is the argument for it."

"Then it is copied with no number on it," said Anneke Roos, "and the copy has no number on it either, and anybody who puts a number on a copy has put a number on it themselves and may have it wherever they like. Put the title at the top and no number anywhere and let it be a piece of paper that this holding made and that anybody may write out again."

At the top of the sheet, in Tova Reed's hand, because she wrote it:

*Seed-route schedule, from the seed house gate at Lantern Reach to the far end. One book. Kept on the well house door. Every entry read aloud before it is initialled. The reader is a person. A person is anybody who comes.*

Under the title, and before the rules, the head of the sheet. It was not Coll Renner's sentence, and Marek said so out loud, and Anneke Roos made him say it twice.

"His words go on the form," said Marek. "He named it in a yard. It is the only sentence anybody in this district has used about this and it is correct and if I write it in my own hand it is a licensed man's professional opinion in a book anybody may copy, and there is no form in this district for a licensed man's professional opinion in a book anybody may copy. He said it once, at a gate, and the gate is not a document. So it goes in as his and it stays his, and it is on the page of this holding's book with the hour on it, and it does not go on the form."

"It is a downgrade and I would like it minuted as a downgrade," said Dunmar Fitch, and entered it, and read it back, and the reader was Ilsa Kern, who had come in from the ditch and had not washed.

Then the rules, and there were seven columns, and the yard came out to look at them because a thing with rules on it that is going on a door is a thing everybody in a fen will read within a day whether it is nailed up or not.

The first column was the day. The second was the crate, numbered. The third was the lot and the keeper of it, in the keeper's own words. The fourth was the weight. The fifth was the first end: the hour it left this gate, and a signature. The sixth was the second end: the hour it arrived, and a signature. The seventh was the return.

And under the seventh column, on the same sheet, Dunmar Fitch ruled one more line and wrote on it: *the return column was read empty on the day this form was ruled, and it is read aloud every day it is read, and the number of times it has been read empty is kept under the form in the clerk's hand, and the clerk does not know yet how many there will be.*

"There is no name in the sixth column," said Jonas Byre.

"There is no name in the sixth column because nobody has told us one," said Tova Reed. "A letter on the fourteenth of this month from a person who gave her name and no house, and eleven households, and a road out at two places. That is what is at the far end of this form. If anybody in this yard writes a name in that column today, they will have written it out of a guess, and I will not have it on a piece of paper that goes on a door."

"It has got eleven households in it," said Jonas Byre.

"It has got eleven households in it and no house and no name and no ground number, because the district has no box for the ground and said so in a letter of four lines, and I am not writing a name into a column to fill a column. Write *eleven households, keeper not named* and let the column be short."

It was nailed up on the well house door on the afternoon of the seventeenth at about the fourth hour, with four nails of a size that had been chosen by Jonas Byre, who does not trust nails, and it is still on the door.

And the first person to read a seed-route schedule aloud to a body of this kind was Jonas Byre, and he objected, and the objection was correct, and it is in the book in his own words with his name against it.

He read the title and the seven columns and the line under the seventh, and then he read the seventh column again by itself, and then he put the sheet down on the step and said the thing.

"I am going to object to the seventh column and I am going to do it properly, in the yard, in front of anybody who wants to hear it, and I want it read back before it is initialled and I want the reader to be the woman with the shilling, because she is the one person in this yard this is about.

"The seventh column is a debt," he said. "You have written a form that anybody may copy, and you have nailed it on a door, and on that door there is a column headed *the return*, and in five weeks or a month or a year somebody is going to come to that door and read that column and see whether anything is in it. And there is going to be nothing in it, because there has been nothing in it for two months and there is nothing in it this afternoon. And I am the man who put that word on the board over the bay, so I know exactly what I have done and I would like it in the book that I did it and why.

"I have been let down by a promise four times in my life and three of them were written down. Not by men. By weather and by a mill and by a road. But they were written down, and I turned up, and there was nothing in the column. And I am not standing here to say this yard is doing that to anybody. I am saying that the people who can read this form are people who have been to this door before, at the gate, on the twenty-eighth of a month three months back, when a schedule of damage of sixty pounds ten was pinned up on it with a fourteen-day limit and no appeal anywhere, and that form was correct too. Every form this holding has ever put on that door has been correct."

He picked the sheet up off the step and looked at it.

"I am not asking for the column taken off," he said. "I am asking for it left empty, out loud, in front of people, and for the number of times it has been read empty to be written down where the column is, so that a man standing at this door in the third month of next year reads *it has been read empty ninety times* and understands that this is not a promise that has been broken. It is a promise that was never made. That is all I am asking and I will read this form every day of my life if it is worth reading."

Nobody argued with him, because he was right, and because the fen had spent a season learning that a true thing that costs money is not a crime and that a category that is true cannot be caught at anything.

The rule went on the sheet that evening, under the seventh column, in the clerk's hand: *the return column is read aloud. It is empty. The number of the readings is kept under this form. A column that has never been filled is not a broken promise. It is a line anybody may write on.*

The woman with the shilling read it back, and read it twice, and initialled it, and said one sentence in the yard afterward, which was her standing and which nobody had ever tried to take from her.

"I have four children," she said, "and one of them has not had a proper winter in four years, and I am not asking anybody for anything, and I want it in the book that I asked who was going to fill that column and nobody told me and nobody lied to me either, and that I would rather it was nailed up empty than not nailed up at all, and I do not know how those two things go together and I have thought about it for an hour and I have not got it."

Ilsa Kern had her own objection and she made it at the bay, on the evening of the seventeenth, with her hand on the board and the thirty-nine under the word.

"I want the board taken off," she said. "Not the form. The board. I cut the word RETURN and I cut the number under it and I have been looking at it every day since and I did not know until this afternoon that a board nailed over a door is a thing people read before they read the door. That number is going to be a hundred, and then it is going to be three hundred, and there is going to be a man in a county somewhere who knows that a bay in a fen has stood empty three hundred days, and that is not a fact about compost, it is a fact about a bay. Keep the form. Take my board down. I have cut a second board and it says the same thing in smaller letters and it is not nailed to anything."

"It is going up," said Marek, "and it is going to carry whatever number it has got on the morning somebody puts a crate under it, and nobody is going to be allowed to take it down and put it back a day later, and I would like the clerk to enter that I have said so in the yard in front of five people."

The man of about fifty did not object, and he said why, and the reason was the worst one anybody said that week.

He said it on the second, the eighteenth, at the eighth hour, standing at the back of the yard with his hands behind him, after two days of listening to everybody else.

"I do not object because of anything about the form," he said. "I have read the form and I can read a form, most of it, and I have put my initial against half of this holding's book since the fourth of a month three months back and I have not asked to be thanked for that and I am not going to be now. I do not object, and I am going to tell you the reason, and I want it in the book in my words and I want it read back, and I want it entered that I am a member of this body and I have a vote and I have not used it this morning and I would like somebody to ask me why and I would like the answer to be the same one.

"A hundred and four days," he said. "A hundred and four days this yard has been a thing that other people can read. Nobody came. Twenty-six days once, and nobody came, and that was written down and it was not a punishment, and I was pleased about it, and I have thought about being pleased about it every day since and I have not liked it. And then a woman read our whole book out to five households and to a buyer with a pencil, and a man off the Marrowfield road stopped on the road and made our clerk say out loud that he had looked nine days for a reason and not found one, and that was the first time since the fourth of a month three months back that anybody came to this door for nothing. And now a woman has walked eleven days and paid a shilling, and that is the second.

"So a schedule is a thing you can arrive at," he said, "and a book is a thing you can be read out of, and there is a difference and it is not a small one, and this yard has spent a season being a book and has never once been a place anybody had to come to. I am not objecting to the form. I am putting my hand on it, and the worst of it is that I cannot tell you who it is for, and I have not asked, and I have not asked because I do not want to be told, because if it is for the eleven households then I have been talking about a reader since the book was opened when what I wanted was a person, and if it is for this fen then we have just built a door out of a thing that is already nailed to a table, and I would like somebody else to work out which of those two it is and to tell me, because I have got a hand on a piece of paper and I do not know what I am holding."

Lissa Vale read that back to him, slowly, and he initialled it, and Dunmar Fitch entered it with the hour and read it back again, and the reader for that second reading was the man of about seventy, who had come to the door to look and had stayed, and who asked for his initial to be put against nothing, which is his standing, and which cost the holding nothing.

Then the fen did the thing that was the whole of the eighteenth, which was to go out to the empty bay with the form's rule in one hand and read the seventh column out loud in an empty yard, to nobody, twice, and enter that it had been read empty twice.

Underneath the schedule, dated the eighteenth, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the fourteenth note under section nine of the charter schedule: a form ruled by hand with seven columns and no number on it, nailed on the well house door on the seventeenth of this month; a return column that has been read empty and the number of the readings kept under it; a licensed man's sentence entered as his and not put on the form because there is no form in this district for a licensed man's opinion in a book anybody may copy; an objection by a member of the body that a column that has never been filled is not a broken promise but a line anybody may write on; and eleven days.

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*
