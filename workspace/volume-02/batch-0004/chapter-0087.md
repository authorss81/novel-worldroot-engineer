# Chapter 87

## The Convoy

The convoy was written up on the fifth, at the seed house table, crate by crate, and it is in the book whole, and it took Lissa Vale most of the morning and Dunmar Fitch the rest of the day.

Twelve crates. Numbered one to twelve in the second column. The third column is the lot and its keeper in the keeper's own words, because a form that puts a keeper in a box of its own is a form that chooses the keeper.

One to six: *the seed house's own bean of Lantern Reach, out of the seed house's own saving, lot of the season before last, from the low strip above the toe ditch.*

Seven to twelve: *the March's line, Dryfoot Trust, out of the tin, sown in this seed house before the sowing closed, the eleventh of eleven cuttings of the eleventh month two seasons back.*

That is what is in the third column for seven to twelve, and it is the keeper's own words, and the words are *the March's line*, and a line is not a lot.

"I am not going to correct it," Tova Reed said, when Anneke Roos asked. "It is the truth and it is the keeper's own words and the column is the keeper's words. If I write *a crate of four inches of young rye in soil* then a person in a year cannot tell from the book whether that was a line or a lot, and the only thing that makes the difference is that one of them can be sown twice and the other cannot. So the column says line, and if that is wrong on the face of the page then the page is showing a thing that is wrong, and that is what a page is for."

Each crate carried a weight, and the weights were put in by hand on a scales in the seed house door, and six of them came to about eleven pounds and six of them came to about twenty-nine, because six of them are soil.

And the order on the list is date order, and the list was ruled in date order before anybody knew what would be on it, and the fen's own saving was ready on the day the form was ruled and the March's line was not, and the fen did not reorder it, and gave the reason in the book in Tova Reed's words and Marek's together.

"A body that has spent a season refusing to choose for other people does not get to choose which line reaches a settlement because of the date on a list," Marek said. "The road chose. The ground chose. The list chose and the list is a piece of paper and it is what anybody may copy."

"I would have reordered it," said Tova Reed, and nobody argued with her about that and nobody said she was wrong, and it went in that way.

Two carts at five shillings a day and two carters' men at two shillings a day. Fourteen shillings a day, and the second cart is paid for whether it goes or not, and the plank and the chain at the crossing are two, and the whole of Saturday was sixteen shillings, which is the same sixteen as Monday of a week before, and this time something moved.

One loaded cart crossed the first break, on a plank, with a chain to a mule from the mill, and two men on the plank at the front wheels, in about eleven minutes, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon.

The second cart did not go. It stood in the seed house yard from the eighth hour of the morning until the seventh hour in the evening with its shafts down and its man beside it, and it cost five shillings and a man's two, and the reason is a sentence a carter's man said on the twenty-fourth of last month and which has not been out of this holding's book since: he can put one loaded cart over the first break and will not put a second in the same place inside a week, and the ground under the plank is peat and gravel with a foot of water stood on it, and a loaded cart going over presses the water down and it comes up on the far side.

The man who stood beside that cart all day was about sixty and had been crossing bad ground for thirty-one years, and he did not go home when it was clear the cart was not going, and Marek Vale asked him why, and he said it in one sentence, and it went in the book.

"Because you paid for me to be here," he said, "and if I go home I have not been here, and the next time you want a cart over that break you will remember whether I was standing in your yard on a Saturday or whether I was at home, and I would like to be a man you remember that way. It is five shillings. I have stood in worse yards for less."

The schedule's route line, at the top of the form, reads: first end, the first break, the low dry, the second break, and beyond. The road holds at the first break and does not hold at the second, and the second break is a quarter mile of running sand with a soft rise, and that is why the route ends at the low dry and does not go on to the March road, and it is the first fact about a seed-route bridge anybody in this fen has ever written down, and it is on a door, and anybody who stops at that door can read that the road to the far end of this route is the bad half of the last two miles of it.

---

Marek Vale went with the cart, and nobody in the yard agreed with him about it and the clerk entered the reason in three lines.

"Nobody from this holding goes and collects," he said. "I know it and I am not going. I am not collecting a survey. I am not receiving anything. I am taking six crates of this holding's own bean to a place it has never been, over a road it has walked, and I am going to stand in front of a woman and say what is in the crates, and what is in the crates is this holding's own name, and part of that name is wrong, and I am going to say the wrong part out loud to the person who is going to sow it. That is not collecting. That is the opposite of collecting."

"Enter it," said Dunmar Fitch, "and put the hour on it and put the rule he quoted in beside it, so that a person in four years can see that the rule was applied and the reason did not reach it."

Ilsa Kern and Jonas Byre went with him, and Lissa Vale stayed, and the man of about fifty stayed, and the man of about seventy stayed, and the seed house was four people and a lamp and the schedule nailed on a door and the fieldbook on the middle of the table where anybody may ask to have a page of it read, and Lissa Vale said she would read the seventh column out to any person who came, on the first, the middle and the last, whether anybody came or not.

The convoy came over the first break at the fourth hour and was on the low dry at about the seventh, and Wrenlow is at the low end of eleven plots and there is no gate.

The far end of the bridge is eleven plots, a well, two rooms, and a woman of thirty-nine standing at a fence of thorn and hazel with her sleeves rolled, and a crate was put down on the ground inside the fence, and Marek Vale was the first thing through the far end of a seed-route bridge, and the fact of his being the first thing through it is entered with the hour, and it is the only time in this batch that a fact is entered about a man for being the first.

He had a piece of lining paper in his coat, folded twice, and he took it out and gave it to her, and it was a certificate, and it is the only certificate in this batch that anybody asked for.

"It is about a crate," he said. "It is not a form. There is no form on it. I wrote it this morning and I want you to know before I give it to you that it says something about this seed that is not true, and that the not-true part is on purpose, and that I would rather you heard it from me than off a piece of paper."

She took it and looked at it for a while, and then she said the thing that made the whole of the sixth of the month.

"I cannot read it," she said.

He said nothing.

"Not the words," she said. "I have never been handed a form in my life. Not a certificate and not a bill and not a note from a man with a cart. I know the words I use and I know the numbers, and everything else is a mark on a page, and I have been looking at this for about a minute and I do not know one of the marks on it and I am not going to pretend I have read it and I am not going to put it down and say I have."

"Then I will read it to you," Marek Vale said, "and then I am going to say it again without the paper, because a paper you cannot read is worth nothing to you and I would like to have said it in a way that you have."

And he read it, and it is on lining paper with no printed head on it, in his own hand, forty-one words:

*Six crates. The seed house's own bean of Lantern Reach. The ground is this fen, the low strip above the toe ditch. The name the seed house has printed for this bean was copied off a Crown list in a year it does not name, and it does not know where the bean came from. Eleven years kept. It will do on poor dry ground. Weighed at this end at the gate.*

And then he said it again, standing in front of her at a fence of thorn and hazel, without the paper, in a plain voice, and it took about ninety seconds, and he was wrong about his own bean in front of the person it was wrong about, and there is no other person in this volume who is more qualified to hear it.

She listened to all of it, and she asked one question, and it was not about the bean.

"The name you do not know," she said. "Then whose name is on the tin you are going to sow it out of?"

"Mine," said Marek Vale. "Mine, and a printer's, and a man in an office in a county I have never been to, and none of them are the seed. I have got it in a printed book in four counties and on the front page of that book it says the seed house copied the name off a Crown list in a year it does not name, and it is on the front page because a woman in this fen decided in a yard that it should be, and she was not thanked for it and neither am I."

Wenna Roke thought about that for about as long as it takes to turn a barrow.

"Then take the name off the tin," she said. "Not off the book. Off the tin. Write on the tin what it is. A thing in a sack with the true name on it is worth more to me than a sack with a good name on it that a woman nine miles away has spent eleven years not being able to check." She looked at the six crates. "And I will keep your paper. I am not going to keep it because I can read it. I am going to keep it because a man came eleven miles with a thing he did not have to tell me and told me anyway, and there is one sheet of paper in this settlement and it is a list of eleven households, and a list is not a record of anybody's bean, and now there will be two sheets. That is all. Two sheets and a stone on each."

So the fen entered, on the sixth, at the well house door, with the hour, and it is the first entry in this holding's book that is about something a person at the far end of a route was asked to do:

*That a settlement of eleven households with no charter, no standing and no document was asked to keep one sheet of lining paper with forty-one words on it and no form, and kept it, and put a stone on it, and the fen has entered the fact that it was asked, and does not know and has not asked whether it was a kindness, and has entered that the not-knowing is in the book as well as the asking, and that the two are the same size.*

The copies went up the fen road on the seventh and one of them was nailed to the second board at the north gate, which had been cut on the seventeenth of last month and had never been nailed to anything because there had been no copy for it, and one of them was nailed to a post inside a fence of thorn and hazel on the low dry with the second end column on it, unsigned, and it says *eleven households, keeper not named*, and nobody put a name on it, and the schedule's fifth and sixth columns are empty at that end and stay empty.

Six crates are in eleven plots. Six crates are in a barn on this fen, under four inches of green in soil, in six trays, in a bed at three and a half degrees, and the sowing closed on the twenty-ninth of last month and the ground is not going to be turned again until spring, and the difference between the six that traveled and the six that did not is a date on a piece of paper, and everybody in this yard knows it, and one of them has entered the knowing, and the knowing is not in capitals.

The entry about the two sheets and the stone was read aloud in the yard on the seventh, twice, and a person read it back after Lissa Vale, and the person was the man of about fifty, who had voted against the book eight days ago and who read it in a slow careful voice and then said one sentence, which Dunmar Fitch entered and which is the last thing that man said in this batch.

"She has got a stone on a piece of paper she cannot read," he said, "and that is worth more than the six crates, and I am not going to pretend I know what it is worth, and I would like the fen to be careful about how pleased it is."
