# Chapter 79

## A Place The Map Does Not Have

The district's letter came up the fen road on the morning of the nineteenth, on the office's paper and not Hilary Skeath's, and it was four lines long, and it was signed by a clerk whose name was entered in the book with the hour and read back, and not one word of it was wrong.

*Water-supply bylaw, second part. To the holder of a node at Lantern Reach, or to a body having correspondence with that holder. The district is required to record correspondences concerning ground within the district. Does the above body have any holding, interest, correspondence or claim in or to a place within this district which the district is unable to name upon its own form? If so, state the place and state the nature of the interest. Four lines. This office has no discretion and asks the question because the question has arisen.*

Four lines, and a fifth that was not a line of the question, which was a sentence at the bottom in the same hand saying that if the body had no such place the answer would be received in the ordinary course and no further form would be sent.

Tova Reed read it twice at the seed house table before she said anything, and then she said the thing out loud, and she said it to the room and not to the letter, and she did not soften it and she asked for it to be entered before anybody could answer it.

"I sent them a holder," she said. "On the fourteenth of this month, on the Thursday cart, with a page and a half of my own on the second sheet. I told a district office in writing that this holding is a holder of a line that a Crown map of nine years ago says has no holder, and I said it in a return, and I sent it because I was not going to send a Crown sheet and be silent. And they have gone looking for what the holder is holding."

She put her hand flat on the letter.

"That is what has happened and it is correct and I would like it entered in that order, the correct part first, and I would like the hour on it, and I would like a person to read it back."

Dunmar Fitch entered it and read it back and Marek initialled it, and the reader was Jonas Byre, who said that he had not understood the whole of it and had understood the shape of it, which the fen has learned is the correct use of a reader.

Then Marek did the thing that the fen has done four times this season, which is to find out why a Crown office has done something, out loud, before answering it.

"They are asking because of the counter-mark," he said. "Not because of the letter that went down this road on the fourteenth, and not because of anything in this yard. On the fourteenth of this month a body with a charter sent a Crown office a piece of paper saying that the body is a holder of a line on ground the district maintains. The office cannot leave that on a shelf. It is in a file, and a file is read at the next renewal, a variation or an objection, and there is no renewal coming because the charter is going, and so the office has gone looking for the holder in the only direction available, which is outward. They have a holder and they do not know what the holding is of. So they have asked the only party they can ask."

"Is that fair to the woman," said the man of about fifty.

"It is fair in the way the district is fair about everything," said Marek, "which is to say it is correct and it does not know her and it is not asking about her. It is asking about us. Whatever the district finds, it will find through this holding, because this holding is the only body in this district that has ever admitted to holding anything."

Nobody wrote that down, and Marek noticed that it had not been written down and did not ask for it, and Lissa Vale wrote it down anyway, on her own sheet, and entered that she had, and the hour, and the reason she gave was that it was the kind of sentence that gets said once and never again and she had been in this room thirty-one years.

---

They went down the fen road on the twentieth to the sub-office at Fennmere, the four of them, because a body that has a form to answer does not answer it from a table, and because Anneke Roos said so and was right.

Perrin Osse was at the counter, where she has been for eleven years, and she read the fen's four-line letter twice and then did something that had not happened at that counter in the whole of this season, which was to laugh, once, and then stop.

"Sit down," she said. "All four of you, because this takes longer than it looks and you are not going to be standing up for it. Do you know why I sent a letter?"

"Because your form would not take it," said Marek.

"Because my form would not take it," said Perrin Osse, "and that is the whole of the answer and I have been waiting nineteen days for somebody to come up this road so that I could say it at a counter instead of into a bag."

She took a single printed sheet out of a drawer and turned it round and put it on the counter, and it was a good form in the way the district's forms are good, and it had four boxes, and the third box was headed with a printed head and a printed head of six digits under it.

"That is the district holding number," she said. "Six digits. Every ground in this district that anybody in this office can be asked about has one, because a ground with a number is a ground somebody holds, and this office's business is holdings. And your letter tells me there is a place in this district that the office has correspondence about, and I have written *the place* in the first box and I cannot write anything in the third, because the place you are describing is on ground this district carries in schedule. Ground the district carries in schedule is ground with no holding number, and ground with no holding number cannot be written on this form, and a form with a box that cannot be filled in is not a form, it is a piece of paper with a hole in it, and I would have had to send it back to my own office with a note saying the applicant could not comply, and there is no line on this form for that either."

She put her finger on the fourth box.

"The fourth box asks the body to set out the nature of the interest and the ground and the works. That I can fill in, and I would have filled it in myself, and I would have written *see correspondence* and sent it, and the file would have had a hole in it and the hole would have been in the Crown's hand and not in yours. So I sent a letter instead, and I have sent letters for eleven years and this is the first one I have ever sent to a body that has never given me any trouble, and I would like that noticed, and I have written it in the register with the hour and you may read the register because you may read anything in this office, and you may not take a copy of anything, and the reason for that is not secrecy, it is that a copy in a body is a copy a clerk can be asked for, and I have read about your door."

That last sentence went into the fen's book in Perrin Osse's own words, with the hour, because she said it out loud and Lissa Vale was in the room and read it back in the room.

"I would like to ask you a question," said Marek, "and it is not about the form."

"You may ask."

"Would the district have asked anybody else?" Marek said. "If this holding had not sent the second sheet on the fourteenth of this month, and if the district had not had a holder in its file, would this letter have been sent at all, or would a woman with no house have gone on writing letters until somebody answered her?"

Perrin Osse thought about it for longer than a clerk is supposed to think about it.

"No," she said. "She would not have had a letter. She would have had a form with a hole in it, if anybody had thought to send her one, and there is no address to send it to and no box on it that takes her. There are eleven households on ground this district carries in schedule and no district holding number and no box on any form in this office, and that is not a secret and it is not a fault and it is a true thing about the paper. You sent me a holder. I have gone looking for what the holder is holding, and I would rather do that than leave a form in a tray with a hole in it, and I would rather a body answered me badly than that the hole sat in my drawer and got older."

She squared the form on the counter with the flat of her hand, which is the gesture of a woman who has kept a register for eleven years.

"Now. What are you going to write in the second box."

---

They drafted the answer in the yard of the sub-office with the wind coming off the low quarter, and it took two hours and it is the only document in this batch that the fen had to get wrong twice before it got right, and the wrongness of the first attempt is entered because Anneke Roos would not have it out of the book.

The first attempt was four lines long and it described a settlement. It said eleven households on ground the district carries in schedule, kept by a person who keeps seed, on ground of about nine hundred acres by a walker's estimate, with a well at the low end of it that is in some years and is not in others.

Anneke Roos read it and said no to the whole of it in four words, and then took six to explain.

"That is a survey of a place we have never been to, made out of a woman's letter of three lines and a number off a trade article, and there is nothing in it that a man with a rule can check, and a line in a book that cannot be checked is a line somebody can use. Nine hundred acres is a number and I do not know where it came from. The well that is in some years is not in this holding's book at all, and I do not know who wrote it to anybody."

"It is a fact," said Marek.

"It is a fact that somebody said it," said Anneke Roos, "and we have got her name and no house and no district holding number and no ground number, and the district is not asking us what a place is like. The district is asking us what our interest in it is. Answer the question that was asked. The rest of it is us writing about a woman who walked eleven days, in our own words, on a form that will go into a Crown file, and in four years there will be a man in an office reading that a fen said a place had nine hundred acres, and we will not be here, and she will, and that is the whole of what is wrong with it."

The second attempt was what went.

*That this body has a correspondence. The correspondence is a letter received on the afternoon post of the fourteenth of this month, on foot, by a person who gave her name and did not give a house, stating that she keeps seed for eleven households on ground the district carries in schedule, and offering to pay a shilling a copy of this holding's printed record. A copy and a shilling of this holding's money went down the fen road on the same afternoon. This body has not been to the place. This body has made no survey of it and holds no map of it. The nature of this body's interest is this: it is a correspondence, and nothing else. This body has a schedule for a route it has not walked, and no interest in the ground. The name of the person is in this holding's book and this holding will supply it to the district in the ordinary course and not on this form, for the reason that a name in a book anybody may read is a name anybody may use, and that reason is the holding's own and was given out loud in a yard on the eleventh of a month of last month and entered.*

Lissa Vale read it out twice at the counter and Perrin Osse read it a third time and initialled the copy she made, and the copy went into the file, and the fen was not to have a copy, and Perrin Osse said so before anybody asked, because she had learned in a season what a body that is surprised gets like.

And then the form came, on the twenty-first of a month in a season nobody named, by the Thursday cart, and it was form S/10, one sheet, and it had the four boxes on it, and the third one was headed with a printed head and six digits under it, and it was the same form Perrin Osse had turned round on the counter two days before.

Marek read it at the well house door, and then he took a piece of the seed house's own account paper and wrote in the six-digit box, in his own hand, the words he had used eleven days before at this door on a form of a different shape for a different office about a different piece of ground, and he did not tidy them, and he read them aloud first.

*This holding has never been asked to, and it would not be a map if it were.*

Then the second line, which was not on the form and went under the box on the same sheet, because a box of six digits is not a place to put a sentence in and the sentence was the answer.

*And that is the second time this holding has written a sentence of that kind to an office, and a sentence used twice is not a sentence any more, it is a practice, and a practice is a thing an office can make a form of, and this holding has noticed that and would like the noticing put in its own book.*

The form went back on the Thursday cart with the printing's second copy in it, and Dunmar Fitch entered the sending and the hour and the words and read it back, and the reader was the woman with the shilling, who read both lines correctly and said that the second line was the first sensible thing she had heard anybody in this fen say about an office, and did not say why, and nobody asked her.
