# Chapter 77

## Fifteen Days

Coll Renner came up the fen road on the fifteenth, a Saturday, at about the fourth hour in the afternoon, on the date he had given them in a yard on the eighth of last month, and he had nothing, and the nothing was the whole of his errand and he said so at the gate before anybody had asked him for it.

"That is the report," he said. "I said the fifteenth and I have come on the fifteenth. I would have come on the fifteenth anyway. A carter came into my yard on Friday with a load of chalk and told me a boy had gone down this road on the fourteenth with a book and a letter and a shilling in his hand, and he asked me about a road I have never been on, and I told him I did not know it, and he said he did not either, and that is the whole conversation. I have been turning it over since Friday and I did not like it."

"About the walking," said Marek.

"About the walking," said Renner.

He put his case down on the step of the well house, which is where he has put it four times this season, and stood with his hand flat on the lid.

"You have bought a thing," he said. "That is what has happened. You have paid a shilling to a woman you have never met for a book about your own eleven years, and she is going to send you a shilling back if she can raise it, and if she cannot she is going to send you nothing, and both of those are honest, and neither of them is a trade. In eleven years I have never seen a seed house sell a record to a place it could not find. So I have come up here to say one thing about what you appear to be doing, and I would like it said before anybody in this yard has decided what it is, because after you have decided it you will all hear your own decision in my words and think I agreed with it."

Nobody in the yard had decided anything. That was the plain fact and it took Lissa Vale about a minute to establish, standing in the seed house door, because the fen had four things on the middle of the table and none of them was this.

"Go on," said Marek.

Renner took his hand off the case.

"A local conduit takes water from one place to another place," he said. "That is all a conduit is and I have built about forty of them and thirty-one of them are still going, which is better than anything else in this district. A conduit has one end and it has a head and it has a run, and nobody has ever asked me for the far end of a conduit, because water finds the low place. Water is the easy half of this trade. Water goes down and does not need anybody's permission on the way and does not send anything back and is not a person.

"A seed-route bridge takes seed from one keeper to another keeper, and it has two ends and a return, and the return is the part nobody ever wants to pay for."

The yard was quiet. Ilsa Kern was on the north ditch with a bar and had stopped, which she does not do.

"You do not name a bridge for the distance," Renner said. "There is no such thing in this trade as a long bridge or a short bridge. You name it for the load. You put on the paper what it carries and how much of it and on what day, and you leave the miles off, because a man who has written down the miles has not yet decided what the thing is for, and he will keep writing the miles until somebody forces a number on him, and the number will be wrong. There is one hundredweight at this yard and four hundred hundredweight specified and three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight short, and that is seventy-nine pounds sixteen at four shillings, and I have read it in your book in four places, and the first thing a bridge will ask you for is the one thing you have never once been able to buy."

He stopped, and looked at the compost bay, which is a bay of beaten earth and boards at the north end of the seed house yard, eleven feet by seven, with a roof of oiled sacking on four posts, and which had been put there by Jonas Byre and the man of about fifty in the third week of last month because Anneke Roos had said in a yard that a body ought to have a place to put the thing that comes back down a route, and it has had nothing in it every day since the day it was made, and there is a board over the front of it with the word RETURN cut into it in Jonas Byre's own hand and a number under the word, and the number is the number of days the bay has stood there and not the number of days anything has stood in it, and there has never been a difference.

"Thirty-nine," said Renner, reading it from the step. "That is your figure and I would like it to be a figure you can put on a form."

---

The room did what the room does, and the room on the fifteenth and the sixteenth of a month that nobody named did four things.

The first was arithmetic, and Tova Reed did it standing at the counting-house end of the table with her case shut and her key on her belt, and she did not open the case, and she had a sheet of her own in front of her because she had been keeping one since the twelfth of this month.

"Read the return line," she said.

Lissa Vale read it. She read everything, and the rule of the twentieth of last month says a person reads an entry before it is initialled and the reader is a person and a person is anybody who comes, and the person that afternoon was the man of about fifty, who had come in off the embankment and had not said anything for an hour and a half.

"Return main, the fifteenth," she said. "Nothing put in. None available. Specification four hundred hundredweight a month. Shortfall three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight, seventy-nine pounds sixteen at four shillings. Carried forward and not discharged. Second month running."

"Thank you," said Dunmar Fitch, and entered it, with the hour, and read it back, and the man of about fifty initialed what he had heard.

"That is the whole of what is coming back down this route," said Tova Reed, "and it is nothing, and it has been nothing for two months, and Mr Renner has told us in his own trade that a bridge with an empty return is a road, and I would like somebody who is not a licensed man and not a steward to say that sentence in his own words so that it is in the book as somebody's and not as mine."

Nobody said it, and she did not press it, and she wrote *the sentence was said at the gate by C. Renner, licensed, of Fennmere, in the trade's words, and is entered as his and not as a finding* — and then struck *and is entered as his and not as a finding* out again with a line through it, because Lissa Vale said, without turning round from the press, that a man's sentence put in a book is a finding about the man and not a finding about the road, and that she would not initial a struck-out line that had not been struck out by the person who wrote it, and that Tova Reed struck it and initialled the striking, which is the third time in a season.

The second thing was the question, and it was put in the yard by Jonas Byre, standing beside the empty bay with his back to it as though he could get out of its way.

"Who is the other keeper," he said.

Nobody answered him, and he waited about ten seconds, which he had never done before, and then he said it again.

"That is the whole of it and I have said it twice so that it is in the book twice," he said. "You have got a book of two hundred pages that anybody may read and a woman eleven days on foot who wants to buy a copy of it at a shilling, and out of that a man who builds things has made a bridge in the yard in one sentence without being asked. And I have been trying since Wednesday to work out what the far end of it is, and I cannot, because nobody in this yard has ever been there and I cannot find out from anything in this building, and if I write *the far end* on a piece of paper and nail it on that door, then this holding has written down the name of a place it does not know and it will have answered for it."

The man of about fifty said, from the step, "It is a woman with eleven households."

"It is a person with eleven households," said Jonas Byre. "I said it on the fourteenth, in this yard, and the clerk entered that she gave a name and no house, and that is all we have. I am not standing here saying she cannot be trusted. I am standing here saying that if we put a name on a piece of paper we will have made her into something we know, and the one thing we have actually got is that we do not."

The third thing was the compost line again, and it was Ilsa Kern who put her hand on the back of the empty bay and said the only thing she said that day, which she said to Jonas Byre and not to the room, and which Dunmar Fitch entered because she said it in the yard where the rule is.

"Six feet deep would be about nine tons of it if it were full," she said. "It has been three feet deep of nothing for thirty-nine days. I have cut withy for the bed on the north ditch and I can cut withy for the edge of that bay and I cannot dig compost, and there is no flock and there is no second flock coming and the spent bark ran out at the middle of a month three months back. So the board over that bay is the truest thing in this yard and I would like somebody to say so in the book while I am standing here, and I would like it to be read out, because in four months somebody is going to take that board off and put a crate under it and there is no reason on this earth for them to know it was ever empty."

The fourth thing was on the Sunday, and it was the whole of the sixteenth, and it was Tova Reed's, and she gave it standing at the counting-house end with the sheet of her own on the table and her ruler squared to the edge of it.

"The question the trust asked us on the twelfth of this month," she said, "was whether this holding would hold the trust's seed for one season. I have carried that question for four days and I have not put it in front of a room, and I am putting it in front of a room now, and I am going to do the arithmetic out loud before I say the rest of it, because I have learned in this room that a feeling about an offer is worth nothing and costs the holding nothing, and a number is a different animal.

"The trust's seed is in this seed house. It has been in this seed house since the ninth of a month two months back. Four crates and a tin, and the tin is in the press under the ledger and it has been in the press since the nineteenth of a month three months back and it is eighty-six days at risk on this afternoon and it will be a hundred and ten on the ninth of next month, and I have undertaken that the seed house will not move it and the trust has undertaken that the trust will not ask it to, and neither of us has broken that and neither of us will.

"The bed is at three and a half degrees," she said. "A soil iron cannot read the tenth of a degree and I have read it twice a week since the first of a month. The compost line has read nothing put in, none available, for two months. The return main is short three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight a month and has been since the day a licensed man wrote it on a page in her own hand. So the arithmetic of holding somebody else's seed on that bed is this: a bed at three and a half degrees, fed nothing, with a seed house that has already lost a flock and a mill floor and a bin of sweepings and a third of a ton of spent bark, and the seed of the trust is in it, and the trust is nine hundred miles off and cannot get here to see it and cannot move a crate until the season turns.

"Now here is the part I want written down," she said, "and I want it in the room before I say it. A bridge takes seed from one keeper to another keeper and has two ends and a return. This holding is one end. The far end is a person with eleven households on ground the district says it maintains and does not. If the seed goes, the far end is a keeper. If nothing comes back, the far end is a door, and a door is not a keeper, and a warehouse is not a keeper, and I know that because I have been the other end of a return for eleven years and for most of those years I was a person with a tin in a drawer, and a tin in a drawer is not an end of anything."

She put her hand on her own sheet.

"So the answer to the trust's question is yes, this holding will hold the trust's seed for a season, and it will hold it on a bed at three and a half degrees with nothing going into it, and I would like that answered, and the arithmetic read back before it is initialled, and the word *warehouse* kept, because somebody in this room is going to need it in about six weeks and I would rather it were in the book in my own hand now with the hour on it than worked out later by somebody with an interest in being right."

Marek asked the question, because it was his arithmetic and it was his ground.

"How much of the trust's material is on that bed at one time," he said.

"Six trays of the wet line and four of the dry," said Tova Reed, "and the tin, and the tin is not a tray and the tin is the only thing on this fen that is worth anything to anybody at the other end, and the tin is the reason the answer is yes and the tin is also the reason the answer is frightening, because if the bed goes over three and a half and stays there, there is no second season for the tin, and I have said that out loud in a seed house and I would like it to stay out loud in a book."

"Ten trays," said Marek. "The bed holds twenty-one and it is losing a tenth of a degree every nine or ten days, and the fen's own sowing is twenty-one trays, and the fen's own sowing is due on the seed in about eleven days, and the two cannot both be in that bed. So the answer to the trust's question is yes, and the arithmetic of it is that somebody in this holding has to say out loud that ten trays of somebody else's rye are in the bed, and that is not a number, it is a decision about whose seed is on the ground, and it is not a decision this holding has ever had to take and it is not a decision I can take by myself, and I am not going to take it by myself."

The man of about fifty said it was the fourth of a month three months back that Lissa Vale had put the whole of the fen's ledger on the middle of the table and said anybody might ask to have a page of it read, and that this holding had spent a season learning that a book anybody may read is worth what a reader is prepared to do about it, and that a woman had just done about eleven pages of it on foot and that was a reader preparing to do something about it.

Then the two of them went back to the ditch, and the compost bay stood empty under its board with thirty-nine cut into the wood, and the pump ran, and the lamp above the seed-house table was burning on its living bulb because somebody has to feed it tonight and will feed it tomorrow and the bulb does not care who.
