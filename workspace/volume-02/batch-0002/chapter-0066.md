# Chapter 66

## What The Book Is For

On the nineteenth Dunmar Fitch read the whole of it out, and he did it the way he does everything, without hurrying and without leaving anything out.

He did it once, in public, in the yard, in daylight, from the middle of the table, with the fieldbook and the use log beside him and the press open behind him with the March's tin under the ledger, sixty days at risk and unmoved, a thing that had never happened in this holding. Five households came when a thing was read out, the way five always came, and two strangers on the road stopped to listen, and nobody told them to go. Gael Senn stood at the end of the table with the standing log open to check him. Anneke Roos stood with her coat on. Marek stood on the well house step.

He read the standing pressure log from the first page to the last entry, with the hours on every line, including the morning's line: the leak at five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, two rules, at the seventh hour, pump on, into the housing and not into the pit, and the second rule the one that read the smaller figure.

He read the ninth logging of the beat where it stood, eleven in the quarter hour, two runs of four and one of three, felt at the rail and not at the coping stone, cause not established, licensed person present none, and nothing underneath it by the examiner's decision with her name on the decision and not the thing's. And then he read the tenth, entered that morning in the same hand, eleven again, cause not established, and nothing under that either.

He read the use log, all six lines of it, in Lissa Vale's voice after him, and he gave the dates as they stood on the page: the tenth, two months back, entered on the eleventh with the lateness declared; the twentieth, the same month, the certificate and the seal; the fourth of last month, a refusal to open it, with the reason written against it; the seventeenth, four in the morning, on the back of the page, in her own hand; the thirtieth of last month, a bead, a clip, a license and a witness; and the twelfth of this month, the instrument put on the head at the eleventh hour with a licensed man's name on it and nothing else at all.

He read the seed annex, with its eight lines against the trust's interest, Tova Reed reading the eighth after him without flinching. He read the bed at three and a half degrees and the compost standing at nothing put in, none available, and he read the tin in the press and the days it had been at risk, and he did not explain any of it and nobody asked him to.

He read the three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight shortfall where it stood in the licensed hand, seventy-nine pounds sixteen at four shillings, carried forward. He read the three acres as a decision and not as a charge. He read the nine section-nine margin notes, one after another, with their closing sentence, and the sixth and the eighth without it, and with Marek's own reason for leaving those two bare. He read the certified copy in a Fennmere file where it stood in Perrin Osse's hand, and the copy going to Cray where it stood in Tova Reed's packing list. He read the three figures a well contractor had put on the table on the twelfth and the district clerk's own note of a refusal made at a gatepost on the tenth. It took him the better part of an hour. Nobody left.

Halfway through, at the entry about the third levy, the man on the road put his hand up, the way a man does at a meeting and not at a gate, and said: "You are reading that in a yard."

"I am," said Dunmar Fitch.

"Then say it again," said the man, "and say it to me. Sixteen houses, two papers, the same four pounds five, the same fourteen names. I have got a brother-in-law in that number and I have heard the story from both ends and I have never heard anybody say out loud that nobody can find the reason for it."

"There is no reason I could find," said Dunmar Fitch. "That is not a thing I have looked for and not found, in the sense of a thing I went after. I went after it. Marek went after it. We have been at it since the twenty-fourth of last month and there is no explanation in this building for a sum that came back the same twice, and I entered it as an observation and not with a cause on it, because a cause I cannot support is a worse thing in a book than a blank."

The man on the road stood with his hand still up for a moment and then took it down and said, "That is the first time anybody has said that to me," and did not come in and did not ask for anything else, and went up the road.

The other stranger, who had not said anything at all in the first hour, spoke once, in the middle of the return line. "There is a name on the notes you read at the beginning. The engineer. I have heard it before this morning and I have not heard one of you say it out loud."

"There is not going to be," said Dunmar Fitch.

He did not say whether that was a comfort. Nobody asked him, and he wrote nothing down about it, on the grounds that a thing said by a stranger on a road is not a record of anything until somebody has heard it twice.

When he came to the end of it he closed the book and set both hands flat on the cover and said the sentence that turned the morning.

"A book that anybody may read is worth exactly what a reader is prepared to do about it," he said, "and this holding has spent a season building the first and has not found the second."

Nobody answered. The pump ran. The yard was quiet. The two men from the road had gone up it by then, which was two more people than had ever heard the whole of it. The lamp in the seed house burned on its living bulb, fed that morning with water and sweepings, warm and dim.

Marek looked at the five households, and at the two men on the road who were most of the way up it by then, and thought about the fourth of last month, thirty-five hands in a room of forty, and how pleased they had all been. He thought about the twenty-first of last month, a hearing officer adopting their promise as a condition at their own request. He thought about the eighth of last month, the clean record gone, Lissa Vale saying she was not sorry. None of them had understood what they had done. Dunmar Fitch had said it on the twenty-third and Anneke Roos had written her initial against it. It was still true on the nineteenth, only more so, because now the whole book had been read aloud once in daylight to two men who had stopped on a road to listen to it.

After the reading Dunmar Fitch took the book back into the seed house with Gael Senn and checked every line he had read against the page, the way he always checked a reading, and Gael initialled the checking. Tova Reed packed the copy for Cray with the eighth line in it and the four days of lateness above the eighth line, crate by crate on the list, the way the consignment had been packed on the twentieth of last month with item and crate and witness. Lissa Vale fed the lamp bulb for the night, water and a scoop of matter, the way the bulb has to be fed by somebody every night or it dies. The ordinary work closed round the extraordinary reading the way water closes round a stone.

Anneke Roos said the thing that has to be said, from the edge of the crowd, in the voice she used when she was not going to say it again.

"We published a book," she said. "On the fourth of last month, we opened it as a private act, thirty-five hands in a room of forty. On the twenty-first of last month the district adopted it as a condition at our own request, on a fifth sheet, and put it where any member, any stranger and any clerk may read it. It cannot be repealed. A body cannot consent to the removal of its own record, because the removal is the consent. That is clause two on the fourth sheet, and I initialled it against a sentence about the book being evidence, and I would like it read back that the sentence I initialled has turned out to be a sentence about the book being a thing that cannot be got back. That is the mechanism that was put there to protect the book against the district, and it is now protecting it against us, and it works perfectly, and I would like nobody in this yard to be disappointed by it this morning."

"There is one thing that does not decide it either, and I am going to say it now so that it is in somebody's mouth before somebody asks me for it," said Tova Reed, from the counting-house end, without getting up. "The seed annex is a condition of this charter and the Dryfoot Trust is a named party to it, and its fourth term sends a copy of everything to Cray. Every entry this holding makes is a thing the trust is entitled to have. If a stranger may come and ask to have an entry read, and I hand him a sheet, and that sheet goes on to a counter in Cray in a stack with a name on it, then the vote we took on the fourth of last month, is a vote about who may stand in a yard and not about who may hold a copy. I am not asking anybody to read that vote differently. I am saying that it is a bigger door than the one we looked at when we built it, and that the trust has not asked me a single question about any of it, and that I am not going to go to Cray to warn them, because a steward who warns people about the size of their own rights is a steward who has decided the rights are too big."

Anneke Roos wrote that down in her own book, which she had not done for a thing another person had said since the fourteenth of last month, and she wrote it as an opinion and not as a minute, and Dunmar Fitch did not ask to see it.

"Then what is the book for," said the man of about fifty, loudly, from the back. "If it convicts us and cannot be taken back. I have been the loudest in this yard about the beat and about the third levy that came to the same sum as the one before it with the same fourteen names on it, and both of those are in that book, and every household in this district can walk up that road and read them, and a man with forty sheep does not have to have been in a yard to find out what we have been doing to our own water."

He had a right to it and Dunmar Fitch gave him the floor for it, which was the rule, and then he waited.

"It is for anybody who comes," Dunmar Fitch said. "That is all it was ever for. Not for us. For anybody."

"That is what I am objecting to," said the man of about fifty.

"Then you are objecting to the right thing," said Marek, from the well house step, "and I want to say two things and then I will sit down. My three acres are in that book. Not as a loss to the holding, which they are not, and not as a claim, which nobody will pay. As a decision. I decided to work three acres on my own account for three seasons instead of putting them in the shared field, and the decision is the thing that put the water where it went, and it is written down in a book that anybody may read, and it is the only honest line in the whole hour. And the second thing is that a thing only the six of us read is not a record. It is a diary. A diary can be lost and a record cannot, and this holding spent a season arguing for the second and got it, and I am not going to be the man who asks for the first back because the reading is uncomfortable."

The body voted on the twentieth, in the morning, and it was the first thing anybody had put to a vote in this holding since the fourth of last month, and the whole of the yard knew both of those facts by the second hour of the afternoon. It was short, and it was taken standing, in daylight, with the book still shut under Dunmar Fitch's hands. Five hands went up from the households present. Two of the five had not voted on the fourth of last month, and one of them said so out loud before putting a hand up, and Dunmar entered the fact and the hour, because a vote that hides who was in the room is a vote that gets read wrong later.

The two strangers did not vote, because they were not of the body, and Dunmar entered that they did not vote, because a book that anybody might read had to show who had decided and who had only heard. The man of about fifty had not put a hand up either, and said why before the count was read, which was that he had made an objection in that yard an hour ago and was not going to take it back by answering a vote about the same thing, and Dunmar entered that as well, with the hour, because a man who will not vote and will not say why is a man a later reader cannot account for. From this day forward every entry would be read aloud before it was initialled, and the reader would be a person and not a room, and a person would be anybody who came. Dunmar Fitch wrote it with the hour on the twentieth, and Anneke Roos initialled it as an opinion, and Gael Senn read it back before anybody else had read it. Marek stood on the step and watched the hands go up and thought about the thirty-five hands on the fourth of last month, and how a smaller vote in daylight could weigh more than a larger one in a lamp-lit room, because the smaller one knew what it cost.

It was not a repeal. It could not be a repeal, and the reason is on the fifth sheet and cannot be argued with. The district had adopted the book as a condition and a body cannot consent to the removal of its own record. It was what a body does when it cannot unwrite a page and will not stop writing.

Underneath the vote Dunmar Fitch wrote the tenth note under section nine of the charter schedule, dated the twentieth, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled: the whole of the book read aloud once in daylight on the nineteenth to five households and two strangers, the standing log and the use log and the annex and the beat and the leak and the shortfall and the nine earlier notes and the certified copy and the copy to Cray all in one hearing, the sentence that a readable book is worth what a reader will do about it, and the vote that every entry be read aloud by a person and not a room. And Dunmar Fitch wrote one more line under the note, in his own hand and with the hour, because a chain of notes that quietly drops a link is worse than no chain: the closing sentence stands on eight of the ten notes and is off the sixth and the eighth, and both of those two were records and not arguments, and both of them say so underneath in his own hand.

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*

And the first person to come would be a stranger, because that is what the vote had been for, and the fen knew inside a week who it would be, and it would be a seed buyer out of a town with no fen in it, because this holding had put a buyer's question in its own book and a buyer's house reads books. She would be owed an answer by the fourteenth. The fourteenth was six days gone.
