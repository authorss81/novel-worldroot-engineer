# Chapter 66

## What The Book Is For

On the eighteenth Dunmar Fitch read the whole of it out, and he did it the way he does everything, without hurrying and without leaving anything out.

He did it once, in public, in the yard, in daylight, from the middle of the table, with the fieldbook and the use log beside him and the press open behind him with the March's tin under the ledger — a thing that had never happened in this holding. Five households came when a thing was read out, the way five always came, and two strangers on the road stopped to listen, and nobody told them to go. Gael Senn stood at the end of the table with the standing log open to check him. Anneke Roos stood with her coat on. Marek stood on the well house step.

He read the standing pressure log, from the first page to the last entry, with the hours on every line, including the morning's line: the leak at five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, two rules, at the seventh hour, pump on, into the housing and not into the pit. He read the ninth logging of the pulse where it stood — eleven in the quarter hour on the first, two runs of four and one of three, felt at the rail and not at the coping stone, cause not established — and then he read the tenth where it stood beside it, logged on the eighteenth in the same hand, eleven again, cause not established, nothing under it by the examiner's decision. He read the use log, all four lines, with the lateness declared on the first, in Lissa Vale's voice after him, line by line. He read the seed annex, with the eight lines against the trust's interest, Tova Reed reading the eighth after him without flinching. He read the pressure pulse where it stood in the log — cause not established, licensed person present none, nothing written underneath it by the examiner's decision with her name on the decision — and he did not explain it and nobody asked him to.

He read the three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight shortfall where it stood in the licensed hand. He read the three acres as a decision and not as a charge. He read the eight section-nine notes, one after another, with their closing sentence, and the eighth without it, with Marek's reason. He read the certified copy in a Fennmere file where it stood in Perrin Osse's hand, and the copy going to Cray where it stood in Tova Reed's packing list. It took him the better part of an hour. Nobody left.

When he came to the end of it he closed the book and set both hands flat on the cover and said the sentence that turned the batch.

"A book that anybody may read is worth exactly what a reader is prepared to do about it," he said, "and this holding has spent a season building the first and has not found the second."

Nobody answered. The pump ran. The yard was quiet. A stranger on the road shifted his weight and stayed. The lamp in the seed house burned on its living bulb, fed that morning with water and sweepings, warm and dim.

Marek looked at the five households and the two strangers and thought about the fourth of a month, thirty-five hands in a room of forty, and how pleased they had all been. He thought about the twenty-first of a month, a hearing officer adopting their promise as a condition at their own request. He thought about the eighth of a month, the clean record gone, Lissa Vale saying she was not sorry. None of them had understood what they had done. Dunmar Fitch had said it on the twenty-third and Anneke Roos had written her initial against it. It was still true on the eighteenth, only more so, because now the whole book had been read aloud once in daylight.

After the reading Dunmar Fitch took the book back into the seed house with Gael Senn and checked every line he had read against the page, the way he always checked a reading, and Gael initialled the checking. Tova Reed packed the copy for Cray with the eighth line in it, crate by crate on the list, the way the consignment had been packed on the twentieth of a month with item and crate and witness. Lissa Vale fed the lamp bulb for the night, water and a scoop of matter, the way the bulb had to be fed by somebody every night or it died. The ordinary work closed round the extraordinary reading the way water closes round a stone.

"We published a book," said Anneke Roos at last, from the edge of the crowd. "On the fourth of a month we opened it as a private act, thirty-five hands in a room of forty. On the twenty-first of a month the district adopted it as a condition at our own request, on a fifth sheet, and put it where any member, any stranger and any clerk may read it. It cannot be repealed. A body cannot consent to the removal of its own record, because the removal is the consent. That is clause two on the fourth sheet. That mechanism was put there to protect the book against the district, and it now protects it against us, and it works. That is Chapter 50's mechanism being used against us by a mechanism that works."

"Then what is the book for," said the man of about fifty, loudly, from the back. "If it convicts us and cannot be taken back."

Dunmar Fitch looked at him for a long time, and then he looked at the two strangers, and then he answered.

"It is for anybody who comes," he said. "That is all it was ever for. Not for us. For anybody."

The body voted, and the vote was the batch's last real act. It was short, and it was taken standing, in daylight, with the book still shut under Dunmar Fitch's hands. Five hands went up from the households present, and the two strangers did not vote, because they were not of the body, and Dunmar entered that they did not vote, because a book that anybody might read had to show who had decided and who had only heard. From this day forward every entry would be read aloud before it was initialled, and the reader would be a person and not a room, and a person would be anybody who came. Dunmar Fitch wrote it with the hour on the nineteenth, and Anneke Roos initialled it as an opinion, and Gael Senn read it back before anybody else had read it. Marek stood on the step and watched the hands go up and thought about the thirty-five hands on the fourth of a month, and how a smaller vote in daylight could weigh more than a larger one in a lamp-lit room, because the smaller one knew what it cost.

It was not a repeal. It could not be a repeal for the volume's reasons — the district had adopted the book as a condition and a body cannot consent to the removal of its own record. It was what a body does when it cannot unwrite a page and will not stop writing.

Underneath the vote Dunmar Fitch wrote the tenth note under section nine of the charter schedule, dated the nineteenth, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled: the whole of the book read aloud once in daylight to five households and two strangers, the standing log and the use log and the annex and the pulse and the leak and the shortfall and the eight notes and the certified copy and the copy to Cray all in one hearing, the sentence that a readable book is worth what a reader will do, and the vote that every entry be read aloud by a person and not a room.

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*

The first person to come was a stranger, and it was a seed buyer, and she was owed an answer by the fourteenth.
