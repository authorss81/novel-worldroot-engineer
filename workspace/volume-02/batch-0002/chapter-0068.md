# Chapter 68

## The First Reason

The yard agreed on the twenty-third, in the morning, without a vote and without a speech.

Kellan Rusk's request had stood minuted since the thirteenth: to be asked at the next examination, in front of a district officer, to say the thing he had said on a bench in the mud, with the figure in his hand. Anneke Roos had said the yard did not agree to things like that in a room in an afternoon. Ten days had passed. The next monthly examination was due, and the district had been written to in the ordinary way, and the district had answered in the ordinary way.

It went into the schedule. Kellan would come. The district would send an officer.

The officer who came on the twenty-fourth was not Hester Carrow. Her authority had run to the end of the year and she had a tray with three other districts in it, and Tarrow had been given to somebody else for the winter. The woman who came up the fen road on a Sunday morning was about thirty-five, with a satchel and a form case and no lamp, and she said her name once and did not repeat it.

The figure was in Kellan's hand. The three figures were on the table. The standing pressure log was open in the middle of the seed-house table, beside the fieldbook and the use log. The press stood open behind them with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved, sixty days at risk on the twenty-fourth.

"State your business," said the officer.

Kellan stood. He had the district's monthly return folded in his coat, with the district's mark on it, and the fen's book open to the page of the eleventh where the three figures stood with the hour on them.

"I am asked to say what I said on the seventeenth of a month on a bench," he said. "The name in the box is an office and not a man. Schedule clerk, fen drains. The post has existed longer than I have. The man in it has changed four times that I know of. He does not come out. He signs the paper. The paper goes to a store. The store gives out the kit."

The officer held up her hand.

"Under your charter," she said, "your return to the district is a document. I will hear the figure when I have seen the document. Produce it."

Kellan produced it. He laid the return on the table, unfolded, with the district's mark uppermost. The officer read it twice, front and back, and then she looked up.

"It does not contain the figure," she said.

"No," said Kellan.

The room was very quiet. Gael Senn had her pen in her hand. Dunmar Fitch had his book open. Marek stood at the end of the table with his marked forearm out of sight under his sleeve.

"I have been putting the figure in this holding's book and not in my own," said Kellan. "Eighty a day. Three hundred at my house against eleven hundred claimed and seven hundred and twenty taken. It is in their book on the eleventh with the hour on it. It is not in my return. That is a breach of a condition of my own charter, disclosed by me, in this room, on the day I asked to be asked."

Nobody rescued him. Nobody forgave him. Nobody apologized to him. Lissa Vale did not look away from him, and that was the most anybody gave him. Gael Senn kept her pen steady and wrote every word, because the book anybody might read had to hold a breach in the same hand as a figure.

The officer wrote the breach down in his own words, with the hour, and read it back, and Kellan initialled it with a hand that shook and then was steady. Dunmar Fitch entered the reading of the return beside it, with the hour, and the three figures from the eleventh beside that, so that a later officer opening the book would find the breach and the figure on the same page and know which was which.

"The figure stands," said the officer. "A person who discloses a breach of his own condition can still be believed about the thing that is not the breach. The breach is on the record. The figure is on the record. Both are in books I can check. That is the rule for a monitored charter, and it is the same rule that held for a sealed envelope in this district once before: what a man says about his own fault does not unmake what he says about the ground."

She turned to Marek, with the file still open on the table and the three figures and the standing log and the fieldbook and the use log all in front of her, and the press open behind with the tin under the ledger, sixty days at risk on the twenty-fourth, unmoved since the nineteenth of a month. The morning light stood flat on the table. Kellan stood at the end with his breached return in his hand and his figure in the fen's book, not rescued and not forgiven, waiting to see what the holding would do with what he had given it.

"Engineer of record," she said. "Is there anything this holding wants the district to require of it."

Marek had waited a season for that question. He had declined to perform the full-draw load test on the first of this month, for the first time in a season when it could have been, because he had written the book against himself so that it could be used and he was not going to hand the district the one number that would help him. Gael Senn had entered it in the second wording, in her own hand, under her name, in a book the district might read: the test did not happen, and if the district preferred a figure she was sorry and there was no figure. He had stood beside that entry for twenty-three days, from the first to the twenty-fourth, and it had cost him sleep.

He asked it now, aloud, in front of a district officer, the question nobody in the yard had asked in a season, which was what the fen wanted the district to ask him. His voice did not shake, which surprised him.

"Will the district require the full-draw load test that the engineer of record declined to perform," he said, "and will the district order it. I offer to perform it myself on the district's order and to take the paper for it. I will bring the boards and the dial and the hands. The district will bring the order and the witness. The figure will be the district's before it is mine."

The officer looked at him for a long time, and then at Gael Senn, who stood with her pen ready, twenty-three years old and the examiner of this node since the twenty-first of a month, who had written a load test that was not performed into a book the district might read and had not celebrated and had not been thanked.

"Will the district require the full-draw load test that the engineer of record declined to perform," he said, "and will the district order it. I offer to perform it myself on the district's order and to take the paper for it."

The officer looked at him for a long time. Then she wrote four lines on a district form, with the hour, and read them back.

"Yes," she said. "The district requires it. The test will be performed on the district's order, at the next examination, with a licensed engineer present. The paper will be the district's. It costs this holding nothing to be ordered. It gives this holding the only thing it has wanted since the twenty-first of a month, which is a figure that is true in front of somebody who can check it."

Gael Senn wrote that down with the hour and read it back, and the officer initialled it.

Then the officer closed the file, all but the string, and said the last thing, the way officers say a thing that is not a decision and will become one.

"The district will want the map next," she said. "A Crown seed-route map of this district is nine years old and there is a legend mark on it that this holding can now name."

Nobody in that room said the word. Tova Reed did not touch her case. Marek did not look at the map sheet on the table. Kellan Rusk put his return back in his coat, with the breach in it, and went out into the yard.

That night Dunmar Fitch wrote it in the book, at the hour, in his own hand, and it was the first entry of the next movement and the longest single line in the volume so far.

It was the eleventh note under section nine of the charter schedule, dated the twenty-fourth, signed by nobody but Marek, and it read in one line: on the twenty-fourth the district ordered the full-draw load test this holding declined on the first and will take the paper for it, Kellan Rusk's breach stands disclosed in his own words with the figure standing beside it, and the district will want the map next because the Crown sheet is nine years old and carries a mark for a maintained line with no holder that this holding has learned to name.

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*
