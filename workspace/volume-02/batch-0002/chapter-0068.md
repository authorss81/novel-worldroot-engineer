# Chapter 68

## The First Reason

The yard agreed on the twenty-third, in the morning, without a vote and without a speech.

Kellan Rusk's request had stood minuted since the twelfth, the day he put three figures on the middle of this table with the district's return in his coat: to be asked at the next examination, in front of a district officer, to say the thing he had said on a bench in the mud, with the figure in his hand. Anneke Roos had said in the seed house that the yard did not agree to things like that in a room in an afternoon. Eleven days had passed. The district had been written to in the ordinary way, and the district had answered in the ordinary way, in four lines, that a return under a monitored charter is a document and that a document is produced before a figure is heard.

It went into the schedule. Kellan would come. The district would send an officer, on a Saturday, because districts hold sittings on Saturdays and had done so for thirty years and would say so if anybody asked.

The officer who came up the fen road on the morning of the twenty-fourth was not Hester Carrow. Her authority had run to the end of the year and she had a tray with three other districts in it, and Tarrow had been given to somebody else for the winter. The woman who came was about thirty-five, with a satchel and a form case and no lamp, and she gave her name once, at the gate, and did not give it again, and Dunmar Fitch entered it in his own book afterward with the hour, Hilary Skeath of the district water office for the Tarrow district, because a name said once at a gate is a name that belongs in a book and not in a memory, and because a name that a clerk has to ask an officer to repeat is a name that a clerk will get right.

The figure was in Kellan's hand. The three figures were on the table where they had been put on the twelfth, in the same ink, on the same page, with the hour on them. The standing pressure log was open in the middle of the seed-house table, beside the fieldbook and the use log. The press stood open behind them with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved, sixty-five days at risk on the twenty-fourth.

"State your business," said the officer.

Kellan stood. He had the district's monthly return folded in his coat, with the district's mark on it, and he did not put it on the table yet.

"I am asked to say what I said on a bench on the seventeenth, two months back, in the mud," he said. "The name in the box is an office and not a man. Schedule clerk, fen drains. The post has existed longer than I have. The man in it has changed four times that I know of. He does not come out. He signs the paper. The paper goes to a store. The store gives out the kit. I said two sentences nobody asked me for and I have not been able to put them anywhere for a season, and I am asking to be asked, and I have the figure in my hand and I want to give it to an officer and not to a book."

The officer held up her hand.

"Under your charter," she said, "your return to the district is a document. I will hear the figure when I have seen the document. Produce it."

Kellan produced it. He laid the return on the table, unfolded, with the district's mark uppermost. The officer read it twice, front and back, and then she looked up.

"It does not contain the figure," she said.

"No," said Kellan.

The room was very quiet. Gael Senn had her pen in her hand. Dunmar Fitch had his book open. Marek stood at the end of the table with his marked forearm out of sight under his sleeve.

"I have been putting the figure in this holding's book and not in my own," said Kellan. "Eighty a day, against eleven hundred claimed at the head and seven hundred and twenty taken by the ground, with three hundred a day drawn at my own pump house. It is in their book on the twelfth with the hour on it. It is not in my return, and my return is the document my charter says it is, and the charter does not permit a figure to be given to a third party unless it is in the return. That is a breach of a condition of my own charter, disclosed by me, in this room, on the day I asked to be asked, and I am disclosing it before anybody has found it and not after."

Nobody rescued him. Nobody forgave him. Nobody apologized to him. Lissa Vale did not look away from him, and that was the most anybody gave him. Gael Senn kept her pen steady and wrote every word, because a book anybody might read had to hold a breach in the same hand as a figure, and a book that held them in two places was a book a man could be found in either one.

The officer wrote the breach down in his own words, with the hour, and read it back, and Kellan initialled it with a hand that shook and then was steady. Dunmar Fitch entered the reading of the return beside it, with the hour, and the three figures from the twelfth beside that, so that a later officer opening the book would find the breach and the figure on the same page and know which was which.

"The figure stands," said the officer. "A person who discloses a breach of his own condition can still be believed about the thing that is not the breach. The breach is on the record. The figure is on the record. Both are in books I can check. That is the rule for a monitored charter, and it is the same rule that held for a sealed envelope in this district once before: what a man says about his own fault does not unmake what he says about the ground."

Then she did the thing that made the day worth a season, and she did it correctly, and nobody in that yard wanted it.

"I cannot act on the figure," she said. "It is in the book of a body that is not my holder, given to me by a man who is not my licensee, about water drawn through a bed my district maintains. I have no power to make it an account and I have no power to send a demand on it and I am not going to pretend I have. What I have is a file, and the file is the only place in this district where the draw of a private charter can be recorded without anybody's consent, and this morning I am putting eighty a day for six years into the district file on the strength of a licensed man's own disclosure, and it will be read the next time anything in this district comes up for a renewal or a variation or an objection."

Nobody said anything at all.

"That is what your open book did," she said, looking around the table and not at anybody. "Not to you. It is not a favor and it is not a trap and it was not a clever thing anybody did. You put a figure where a stranger could find it, and a stranger found it, and he brought it to a licensed man in a room with a witness, and he disclosed his own breach to get in front of me, and now it is in a file I cannot lose. If you had kept it in a drawer it would be in a drawer. I want that written down as a fact about a form and not as a fact about anybody's character."

Nobody wrote it down. Dunmar Fitch looked at his book for a while and then said that he would enter it, with the hour, in the words she had used, and she said that she would want to see it before it went, and he said that she could, and it stood, and it was the first time in a season that a district officer had asked to read a line in this holding's book before it was entered.

She turned to Marek, with the file still open on the table and the three figures and the standing log and the fieldbook and the use log all in front of her, and the press open behind with the tin under the ledger. The morning light stood flat on the table. Kellan stood at the end with his breached return in his hand and his figure in the fen's book, not rescued and not forgiven, waiting to see what the holding would do with what he had given it.

"Engineer of record," she said. "Is there anything this holding wants the district to require of it."

Marek had waited a season for that question. He had declined to perform the full-draw load test on the first of this month, for the first time in a season when it could have been, because he had written the book against himself so that it could be used and he was not going to hand the district the one number that would help him. Gael Senn had entered it in the second wording, in her own hand, under her name, in a book the district might read: the test did not happen, and if the district preferred a figure she was sorry and there was no figure. He had stood beside that entry for twenty-three days, from the first to the twenty-fourth, and it had cost him sleep.

He asked it now, aloud, in front of a district officer, the question nobody in the yard had asked in a season, which was what the fen wanted the district to ask him. His voice did not shake, which surprised him.

"Will the district require the full-draw load test that the engineer of record declined to perform," he said, "and will the district order it. I offer to perform it myself on the district's order and to take the paper for it. I will bring the boards and the dial and the hands and the sacking. The district will bring the order and the witness. The figure will be the district's before it is mine, and it will be in a book the district keeps and not in a book I keep, and I will not have the first copy."

The officer looked at him for a long time, and then at Gael Senn, who stood with her pen ready, twenty-three years old and the examiner of this node since the twenty-first of last month, who had written a load test that was not performed into a book the district might read and had not celebrated and had not been thanked.

"Yes," she said. "The district requires it. The test will be performed on the district's order, at the next examination, with a licensed engineer present, and the paper will be the district's. It costs this holding nothing to be ordered. It gives this holding the only thing it has wanted since the twenty-first of last month, which is a figure that is true in front of somebody who can check it. And I will say why I am saying yes without being asked, because you will spend a week wondering whether it was a favor. It is not a favor. There is a clause in this charter with a condition in it that neither the district nor this holding can test, and a node with an untestable condition in it is a node I have to defend at every renewal, and a number I can check is worth more to this holding than any objection I could raise."

Gael Senn wrote that down with the hour and read it back, and the officer initialled it.

Then the officer closed the file, all but the string, and said the last thing, the way officers say a thing that is not a decision and will become one.

"The district will want the map next," she said. "A Crown seed-route map of this district is nine years old and there is a legend mark on it that this holding can now name."

Nobody in that room said the word. Tova Reed did not touch her case. Marek did not look at the map sheet lying on the table where it had lain since the twenty-ninth of last month under a reader's own marginal line in her own hand. Kellan Rusk put his return back in his coat, with the breach in it, and went out into the yard without waiting to be spoken to.

That night Dunmar Fitch wrote it in the book, at the hour, in his own hand, and it was the eleventh note under section nine of the charter schedule and the longest single line this holding has ever entered.

The charter expires on the twenty-ninth of the month after next. That is thirty-five days from this morning, and it had been in two books since the first of the month, and nobody in that yard had said it out loud in three weeks, and the reason nobody had said it was that on the first of the month a man of about seventy had put his hand flat on a well house door and asked for his initial on a feeling, and Dunmar Fitch had written down that a feeling about an expiry is worth nothing, and had been right, and it had cost the holding nothing and it was going to cost a great deal more now, and the only thing on the paper that was still a fact about the future was a line at the foot of a form in an office nine miles off.

It was dated the twenty-fourth, signed by nobody but Marek, and it read in one line: on the twenty-fourth, at the seventh hour, a district officer of the district water office heard a licensed man's figure and a breach of his own charter disclosed at the same time in the same room, the figure entered in the district file on his own disclosure and not on anybody's application, the breach entered in this holding's book in his own words beside the figure and not below it, a body voted in this yard on the twentieth that every entry be read aloud by a person and not by a room, and a line above all of it that has stood since the first of this month and now has a district order under it, which is that the district requires the full-draw load test this holding declined to perform and will take the paper for it, and then, last, that the district will want the map next, because the Crown sheet is nine years old and carries a mark for a maintained line with no local holder that this holding has learned to name, and because every Crown act in this matter so far has been correct and timely and structural and none of them has been an act by a man.

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*
