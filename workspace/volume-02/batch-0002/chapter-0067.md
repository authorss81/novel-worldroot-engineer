# Chapter 67

## The Answer

She came to the gate on the twentieth with a pencil and a notebook and no standing whatsoever, walking up the fen road in the middle of the morning with dust on her boots.

She was about forty, with a coat cut for a town and boots cut for a road, and she had come two hundred miles to buy an answer about a bean. The buyer two hundred miles off who had written to Cray about the Ashcombe oat was her house, and she said so in the first minute, without being asked. She stood at the posts the way Slee stood at the posts, and she asked to have the use log read to her, because the fifth sheet said anybody may, and the standing pressure log was open, and the vote of the nineteenth said every entry would be read aloud by a person.

"I am a stranger," she said. "I have no charter. I have no paper from any office. The fifth sheet says any member, any stranger and any clerk may read without notice. I am the stranger. I would like the book read. I have a pencil. I will write what I hear."

Lissa Vale came out to the gate because Lissa Vale was the one who had written that rule down, on the eleventh day of a month, in her own hand, with the lateness declared on the first line, and because the rule said the reader was a person. She looked at the woman for a long time and then she nodded.

"Come in," she said. "We read entries aloud before they are initialled now. The reader is a person. You are a person. Come to the table."

They sat at the middle of the seed-house table, with the fieldbook and the use log in the middle where anybody might look, and the press open behind them with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved. Dunmar Fitch sat at the end with his book open to witness. Gael Senn stood by with the standing log. Tova Reed sat at the counting-house end and said nothing unless she was asked.

Lissa Vale read. She read the whole of the fen's hydrology because the use log touched the head at every line — the tenth of a month entered on the eleventh with the lateness declared, the twentieth, the refusal of the fourth of this month with the reason, the seventeenth at four in the morning on the back of the page in her own hand — and with it the standing log's leak and pulse and shortfall, the tenth pulse logged on the eighteenth beside the ninth, cause not established on both, nothing under either, because the rule said anybody may ask and she was asked, and the vote said the reading came before the initialling. She read the three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight where it stood, seventy-nine pounds sixteen at four shillings, and the bed at three and a half degrees, and the tin under the ledger, fifty-nine days at risk on the twentieth, unmoved.

Nothing was withheld. Nothing was softened. The leak at five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, at the seventh hour. The pulse, cause not established, nothing under it. The three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight, seventy-nine pounds sixteen at four shillings, carried forward. The bed at three and a half degrees. The stranger wrote in pencil all the way through, stopping Lissa Vale twice to have a figure repeated — the eighty a day, the twenty-one hundredweight and a quarter on the fourteenth — and Lissa Vale repeated them, clearly, the way she repeated everything. Dunmar Fitch watched the pencil the way he watched every pencil, to see what was written and what was left out, and the stranger let him watch, holding the notebook open on the table.

At midday they broke for bread, because a reading that ran through the morning made people hungry. The stranger ate with them in the yard, sitting on the well house step with the notebook in her lap, asking Ilsa Kern about the toe ditch and Jonas Byre about the culvert and Gael Senn about the examination, writing none of it down because none of it was what she had come for. Ilsa told her the ditch was fourteen feet wide and four foot six deep and the stranger nodded as though committing it to memory anyway. Jonas told her he counted minutes out loud and wrote down whatever the hook brought out. Gael told her the return line recorded a thing that did not happen, in the same book, on the same page, in the same hand, with the hour. The stranger listened to all of it the way buyers listen, storing what might matter later.

On the twenty-first the stranger came back with two questions written out fair, in her notebook, in pencil, with the date at the top. The first was about the Ashcombe oat, and Lissa Vale gave her the four-line answer that had gone to the trust: the seed house did not know and had never known and had copied its name off a Crown list in a year it did not name. The stranger wrote that down too, word for word, and read it back.

The second was harder, and the stranger knew it, and she asked it standing up, with her notebook shut. The yard was quiet. The pump ran. Gael Senn had the standing log open and Dunmar Fitch had his pen ready, and neither of them wrote until the stranger spoke, because the vote said the reading came first and the writing second, and the stranger was the reader now.

"Your reason for being here is in a book two hundred miles from here," said Tova Reed, who had been sitting at the end of the table throughout without writing. "The sheet that says the source is disputed. You read it and you did not buy. You came here instead. And you are going to write to a printer about what you heard here."

"Yes," said the stranger. She did not look away. "I am going to write to a printer about it. I have to. My house buys on print. If the print is doubtful, I need a better print. Nobody in this yard can stop me and nobody in this yard wants to, and I know that, and I am saying it out loud so that it is in your book too."

Dunmar Fitch wrote that down, with the hour, and read it back, and the stranger initialled it as a true account of what she had said.

"That is the correct outcome," said Lissa Vale quietly, afterward, to Marek alone. "A woman came two hundred miles to buy an answer about a bean and we handed her a season's water. Being readable is a position and not a virtue. No one asked us to be a good citizen. We are not thanked for it and cannot be."

On the twenty-second, on her way out, with her notebook in her coat and her pencil behind her ear, she asked the question nobody in the yard had asked a stranger in six weeks — whether there was a use log for the fieldbook, a book that said who had held the instrument and when and why.

Lissa Vale looked at her for a moment, and then she answered in one word.

"No," she said.

The stranger wrote that down too.

She left on the morning of the twenty-third, on the post cart going down, with her notebook full and her pencil worn short. Dunmar Fitch entered her going, with the hour: stranger from a town with no fen, read the use log and the standing log on the twentieth and twenty-first, asked about the oat and about the fieldbook's book, answered in four lines and in one word, going to write to a printer. He read the entry back and she initialled it as a true account, standing at the gate with her bag on her shoulder.

Marek watched the cart go and thought about what the yard had handed her: a season's water for a bean's question, the correct outcome, no thanks in it, because no one had asked the holding to be a good citizen. Tova Reed stood beside him with her case locked, saying nothing. Lissa Vale went back inside to the table where the fieldbook and the use log lay in the middle, and straightened both, though neither needed straightening.
