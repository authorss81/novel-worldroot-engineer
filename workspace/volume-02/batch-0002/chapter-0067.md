# Chapter 67

## The Answer

She came to the gate on the twenty-first with a pencil and a notebook and no standing whatsoever, walking up the fen road in the middle of the morning with dust on her boots.

She was about forty, with a coat cut for a town and boots cut for a road, and she had come two hundred miles to buy an answer about a bean. The buyer two hundred miles off who had written to Cray about the Ashcombe oat was her house, and she said so in the first minute, without being asked. She stood at the posts the way Slee stood at the posts, and she asked to have the use log read to her, because the fifth sheet said anybody may, and the standing pressure log was open, and the body had voted on the twentieth that every entry would be read aloud by a person and not by a room.

"I am a stranger," she said. "I have no charter. I have no paper from any office. The fifth sheet says any member, any stranger and any clerk may read without notice, and on the twentieth your own body voted that the reader is a person and that a person is anybody who comes. I am the anybody who came. I would like the book read. I have a pencil. I will write what I hear and I will not write down what I do not hear, and I will tell you at the end if there is anything you did not say, because I am going to have to say so in a letter."

Lissa Vale came out to the gate because Lissa Vale was the one who had written that rule down, on the eleventh, two months back, in her own hand, on the first line of the use log, and the lateness was declared on that first line, and because the rule said the reader was a person. She looked at the woman for a long time and then she nodded.

"Come in," she said. "We read entries aloud before they are initialled now. The reader is a person. You are a person. Come to the table."

They sat at the middle of the seed-house table, with the fieldbook and the use log in the middle where anybody might look, and the press open behind them with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved, sixty-two days at risk on the twenty-first. Dunmar Fitch sat at the end with his book open to witness. Gael Senn stood by with the standing log. Tova Reed sat at the counting-house end and said nothing unless she was asked.

"I am going to read it," Lissa Vale said, before the stranger asked twice, "and I am going to read it myself, and that is not a courtesy. I wrote the line that says anybody may ask to have an entry read. A rule somebody else keeps is a rule that gets kept the way a form gets kept, and I have watched that happen to a woman in a market who is not in this building and is not discussed. If that line is worth anything it is worth being kept by the person whose hand it is in."

Lissa Vale read. She read the whole of the fen's hydrology because the use log touched the head at every line, and she gave the six dates as they stood on the page and not as the room would have said them. The tenth, two months back, entered on the eleventh with the lateness declared. The twentieth, the same month, the certificate and the seal. The fourth of last month, a refusal to open it, with the reason written against it. The seventeenth, four in the morning, on the back of the page, in her own hand, saying that a thing had come out of a press. The thirtieth of last month, a bead, a clip, a license and a witness. The twelfth of this month, the instrument on the head at the eleventh hour with a licensed man's name on it, the contact tested and the test written down first, and nothing else, which she read twice and would not embellish.

And with it the standing log's leak and beat and shortfall, the tenth logging of the beat standing beside the ninth, cause not established on both, nothing under either, because the rule said anybody may ask and she was asked, and the vote said the reading came before the initialling. She read the three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight where it stood, seventy-nine pounds sixteen at four shillings, carried forward, and the bed at three and a half degrees, and the tin under the ledger, unmoved.

Nothing was withheld. Nothing was softened. The leak at five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, at the seventh hour, the second rule the one that read smaller. The beat, cause not established, nothing under it. The three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight, carried forward. The bed at three and a half degrees. The stranger wrote in pencil all the way through, stopping Lissa Vale twice to have a figure repeated, the eighty a day and the twenty-one hundredweight and a quarter put in on the fourteenth of last month, and Lissa Vale repeated them clearly, the way she repeated everything. Dunmar Fitch watched the pencil the way he watched every pencil, to see what was written and what was left out, and the stranger let him watch, holding the notebook open on the table the whole time, which no one in that yard had thought to do and which she said she had done at four other counters that season.

At midday they broke for bread, because a reading that ran through the morning made people hungry. The stranger ate with them in the yard, sitting on the well house step with the notebook in her lap, asking Ilsa Kern about the toe ditch and Jonas Byre about the culvert and Gael Senn about the examination, writing none of it down because none of it was what she had come for. Ilsa told her the ditch was fourteen feet wide and four foot six deep. Jonas told her he counted minutes out loud and wrote down whatever the hook brought out and how big. Gael told her the return line records a thing that did not happen, in the same book, on the same page, in the same hand, with the hour. The stranger listened to all of it the way buyers listen, storing what might matter later.

Halfway through the morning she stopped Lissa Vale on the sixth of the ten margin notes and asked what the sentence at the foot of each one meant, because it was the same seven words over and over and she had begun to think it was a form of words a clerk wrote out because a form has a place for it.

"It is the engine of record's answer to his own note," said Lissa Vale. "He writes what he cannot make anybody do, and then he writes that it is worth nothing against a thing that can be undone. A cut can be refused. A measurement cannot."

"And it is the same on all ten."

"It is on eight of the ten," said Lissa Vale. "The two that do not carry it are the sixth and the eighth, and both of those were records and not arguments and both of them say so in the engineer's own hand underneath. I would rather you read that than have me say it."

The stranger read it. It took her a while and she read it twice, and then she said, out loud, to nobody, that whoever wrote the ninth note was arguing with himself in public, and that she had never in twenty-two years of buying seed seen a book do that, and that she was going to think about the ninth note for a while and was not going to put it in her letter. She said this last part to Lissa Vale and not to Dunmar Fitch, and Lissa Vale let it stand, and did not enter it anywhere, on the grounds that a reader declining to use a thing is not a record of anything except the reader.

On the twenty-second the stranger came back with two questions written out fair, in her notebook, in pencil, with the date at the top. The first was about the Ashcombe oat, and Lissa Vale gave her the four-line answer that had gone to the trust: the seed house did not know and had never known and had copied its own name off a Crown list in a year it does not name. The stranger wrote that down too, word for word, and read it back.

The second was harder, and the stranger knew it, and she asked it standing up, with her notebook shut. The yard was quiet. The pump ran. Gael Senn had the standing log open and Dunmar Fitch had his pen ready, and neither of them wrote until the stranger spoke, because the vote said the reading came first and the writing second, and the stranger was the reader now.

"Your reason for being here is in a book two hundred miles from here," said Tova Reed, who had been sitting at the end of the table throughout without writing. "The sheet that says the source is disputed. You read it and you did not buy. You came here instead, two hundred miles, to a fen with no station and no turnpike worth the name, because a printer printed a doubt. And you are going to write to a printer about what you heard here."

"Yes," said the stranger. She did not look away. "I am going to write to a printer about it. I have to. My house buys on print. If the print is doubtful I need a better print, and I cannot get a better print out of a firm that buys seed. And I will tell you what my letter is going to say, because you will find out in a month and I would rather you heard it from me. It is going to say that a body in this district keeps a book that any stranger may ask to have read, and that a woman in it read the whole of it aloud at a table to a buyer with a pencil, and that everything she read was worse for us than anything we had been told, and that we are eight days late with an answer we could have written in a morning. That is the whole of the letter. Nobody in this yard can stop me and nobody in this yard wants to, and I know that, and I am saying it out loud so that it is in your book too."

Dunmar Fitch wrote that down, with the hour, and read it back, and the stranger initialled it as a true account of what she had said.

Then the stranger asked about the eighty a day, which was the one figure in the whole morning she had gone back to twice.

"Whose is it."

"It is a figure a licensed man brought to this table on the twelfth," said Marek, "and he brought it with the district's return in his coat because his own charter does not let him give a figure to anybody without the return, and he gave it to us anyway, in front of six of us, with the return on the table. His name is in this holding's book. If you write his name down you are writing down a man who is under a monitored charter and who handed a figure to a body that could not use it against him. Your printer will not know that. Your printer will see a name in a book and a number under it and that will be the whole of what he has, and if he is cruel about it he will be cruel about it in print, which is the one place a man cannot answer."

The stranger wrote that down too, and read it back, and then she said something that Lissa Vale wrote on the back of her own page in her own hand and did not read out: *he told me the cost before I asked for it.* Lissa Vale told Marek about it afterward and not in front of the woman, and told him that the stranger had not been going to write it down anywhere, and that this was the second time in two days that a thing had been said in a yard that was worth more in a room than on a page, and that the stranger was not going to make a habit of it because a habit is a rule and she does not have the standing to make rules in a building that is not hers.

The stranger asked one more question that afternoon, and it was the question nobody in that yard had thought to be afraid of, which was what the third of the three conditions in clause three was.

"No one knows," said Marek. "It is a charter condition. The district has not printed what it is and is not required to describe a condition in advance of the examination at which it is tested. I have asked twice. There is a clause I cannot comply with and cannot read, and I have had a licensed contractor from Fennmere put it in his own words on the fifteenth, which is that every holder in this district has one and every one of us is frightened of the same blank."

The stranger wrote that down as well, and Marek watched her do it and understood, with a clarity that stayed with him for the rest of the day, that a thing in a book that anybody may read is not a thing held in trust by a body. It is a thing published by a body, and publication is not a promise, and the difference between the two is the whole of what this volume has learned and it is not a thing anybody in this yard can put back.

On the twenty-third, on her way out, with her notebook in her coat and her pencil behind her ear, she asked the question nobody in the yard had asked a stranger in six weeks, which was whether there was a use log for the fieldbook, a book that said who had held the instrument and when and why.

Lissa Vale looked at her for a moment, and then she answered in one word.

"No," she said.

The stranger wrote that down too.

She left on the morning cart going down, with her notebook full and her pencil worn short. Dunmar Fitch entered her going, with the hour: stranger from a town with no fen, read the use log and the standing log on the twenty-first and the twenty-second, asked about the oat and about the third condition and about the fieldbook's book, answered in four lines and in one word, going to write to a printer. He read the entry back and she initialled it as a true account, standing at the gate with her bag on her shoulder.

Marek watched the cart go and thought about what the yard had handed her: a season's water for a bean's question, the correct outcome, no thanks in it, because no one had asked the holding to be a good citizen. Tova Reed stood beside him with her case locked, saying nothing. Lissa Vale went back inside to the table where the fieldbook and the use log lay in the middle, and straightened both, though neither needed straightening.

Then she said, to nobody in particular, that there was still one rule in that building and it was hers and it was the oldest one, and that a person was anybody who came, and that a person who came and asked had to be answered on the page and not in the yard, and that there would be more of them, and that the next one would come asking for the thing that was in the use log and not the thing that was under the use log, and that this holding had never once written a line underneath anything and was not going to start because a woman from a town with no fen in it had got a pencil.
