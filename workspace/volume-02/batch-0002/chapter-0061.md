# Chapter 61

## A Trade Article

The journal came on the seventh in Renner's own hands.

Coll Renner walked up the fen road on a Thursday morning with a printed sheet folded into his coat and nine men behind him in his yard at Fennmere who did not come. He put the sheet on the middle of the seed-house table, beside the fieldbook and the use log, and he did not look at the bead tin in the corner and he did not look at the press with the March's tin under the ledger.

"I did not write it," he said. "I will say that first because it is the only thing in this room that is about me. I did not write it and I did not send it and I did not know it was coming."

Marek unfolded it. It was two pages, stitched at the top, with the mark of the professional body on the front and a date under it and a title that said nothing about a fen. Inside, under a plain heading, was a description of a result.

It said that a wetted lot of seed, laid against a living bead cut from grafted stock, would report the ground it was grown on, in four seconds, without a clerk. It gave figures for a lot of one variety — weight, water, time, number of trials — and it gave them straight, the way a man gives figures he has checked. There was no name on it. There was no license number on it. There was no yard and no district and no holder.

"It is accurate," said Tova Reed, from the other end of the table. She had read it twice while Marek read it once. "Every line of it. The time. The water. The number. That is the problem."

Renner nodded. He had taken his hat off and he held it in both hands, which was not like him. His man waited at the door. The yard outside went on with its morning: Ilsa Kern on the embankment with a bar, Jonas Byre at the channel, Gael Senn at the well house with her book, the pump running, the lamp fed, the tin unmoved under the ledger in the open press.

"Four things," he said. "I did not write it. I cannot stop it. It is technically correct. And a technically correct description of a device that makes a seed lie is the most dangerous thing anybody has ever printed about this holding. I have carried a bead for five years and I know what it does. Print the doing and every licensed man in the district can do it by winter."

Dunmar Fitch read it a third time, with a rule on the page, and then he set the rule down.

"It does not name us," he said.

"No," said Renner. "It does not have to. A reader who wants a bead does not need our name. He needs the figures. The figures are here. Any licensed man in this district can cut a bead out of grafted stock in an afternoon. Any seed house with a wet cloth can do the rest in four seconds. The article is not about us. It is about what can be done."

Lissa Vale had been standing at the press with her hands on the ledger. She did not touch the journal.

"Then what is wrong with it," she said. "If it is true."

"Nothing is wrong with it," said Renner. "That is what I came to say. Nobody in this fen can say what is wrong with it, because there is nothing wrong with it as a description. It is right. And a right description of a thing that overwrites is an attack, whether the writer meant it or not. A graft keeps the thing put in and loses the thing that was there. Print that in a journal that goes to four hundred doors and somebody will use it to make a seed report whatever history he needs."

Gael Senn looked up from the standing pressure log, where she had been entering the morning's line.

"Then the book is the defence," she said.

"Yes," said Renner. "It is the only one anybody has. And a book only works if somebody reads it. Your book is on this table and anybody may ask to have it read. Their bead is in a box and anybody may buy one. Which of those two is going to travel further by winter."

Nobody answered that. The answer was in the room and nobody wanted to say it.

Dunmar Fitch folded the journal shut and entered it as received, with the hour, a trade article describing a bead result with figures for a lot of one variety and no name and no license number, description accurate, read at this table. Anneke Roos initialled the entry as an opinion. Lissa Vale moved the journal to the end of the table, away from the fieldbook and the use log, so that nobody coming in afterward would think the three belonged together.

The afternoon of the seventh was spent the way afternoons in that yard were always spent, in work that did not stop because a paper had arrived. Marek went with Jonas Byre to the return main at the eleventh rod to check the cheap reed gauge clamped there on the twenty-first of a month, three-sixteenths above the mark at the last reading, pump on the idle. The reed stood steady. Jonas read it and Marek read it after him, and neither wrote a number that was not there, because a number with no baseline and no load was not evidence and not a claim, and both of them had learned that lesson in a flood.

"What does the article change," said Jonas, wiping his hands on his coat.

"Nothing in the ground," said Marek. "Everything on paper. A truth can be an attack and still be true. That is what we have to carry."

They walked back along the ditch where the thirty-one yards of new run lay under its fiber bed, with the four willow sets green at the head. The water moved steadily, eleven minutes from the main to the scour, the way it had since the twenty-second of a month. The graft held. The return line stood three hundred and ninety-nine short in a licensed hand. Both were true at once, and the yard had learned to hold both without dropping either.

At supper Tova Reed spread the journal on the table again and went through the figures line by line with Gael Senn, weight against water against time, checking each against the bead demonstrations of the thirtieth. The figures held. That was the worst of it. A false article could be answered. A true one could only be lived with. Dunmar Fitch sat with them and entered the checking itself, with the hour: figures checked against the yard's own trials of the thirtieth, no fault found, which is itself a finding. Anneke Roos initialled it as an opinion. Lissa Vale kept the press open and the tin where it was, fifty-two days at risk on the eighth, and said nothing about the journal at all, which was her way of saying what she thought of printers.

"What do we do," said Gael Senn, "when somebody builds one."

"We keep the book," said Marek. "A bead reports in four seconds what it was last made to hold. A book reports what was done, with the hour, whether or not it helps. They are not the same thing."

Renner stood with his hat in his hands, looking at the corner where his collar had sat on the thirtieth.

"I cut mine five years ago out of a grafted bean for a woman in a market," he said. "It has been right every time since, and every time it has been right it has made me less sure what right means. Articles do not carry what a man thinks at night. I will not build another this season, not for sale, not for hire. I have nine men and they will want me to, and I am telling this table first."

On the eighth Marek walked down to Fennmere with Renner to look at the yard where the nine men worked, because Renner had asked him to and because it was better than sitting in the seed house with the journal on the table. The yard was clean. The tools stood in order. The copper clips hung on a board in a row. A foreman who did not want to be told he was doing it wrong nodded to Marek and went back to his work.

"You see," said Renner. "I am inside that body. My license is the same license as the circular on four hundred doors. The Assembly is not a villain and it has not acted against you. A trade article in a professional journal is not an act by an office. It is a man writing down what works. Do not let a later room confuse the two."

"I will not," said Marek.

On the way back they passed the post cart going the other way, with the Thursday post in it. Tova Reed was at the seed-house table when Marek came in, with a letter open in front of her that had come on that cart. It was short. It was from a town with no fen in it, from a seed buyer Marek had never heard of, and it was addressed to a counting house in Cray.

She read it aloud, once, without softening it.

"He has read the article," she said. "He wants to know whether the Ashcombe black oat is the one in the index."

The room sat with that for a while. Marek thought about the tin with no label that had come on a cart eleven years ago through a man who wanted to be helpful, and the eleven years of arguing that had followed, and the four seconds on the thirtieth that had given a better answer and a lie in the same breath. He thought about the four hundred doors the circular had gone to, and the hundred and forty sheets the index went to, and the one professional journal that went wherever journals went, and how the three numbers together made a room eleven miles long that no body could hold.

Dunmar Fitch broke the silence the way he always broke it, with a question about procedure.

"Do we answer," he said. "The letter is to Cray, not to us. The trust will answer. Do we write anything on our own account."

"No," said Tova Reed. "The trust answers. We keep the book. The book says the oat came in a tin with no label eleven years ago and is not the oat and is not anything anybody can name, and the bead said Ashcombe and the season before last and the ground under a ley. Both stand. A reader in a town with no fen will have to decide which kind of answer he wants. We do not decide it for him."

Lissa Vale nodded once, slowly, and moved the journal further from the fieldbook, to the very end of the table, where the light fell off it.
