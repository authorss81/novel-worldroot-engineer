# Chapter 61

## A Trade Article

The journal came on the eighth in Coll Renner's own hands, and he came in daylight on a Thursday with one man behind him at the road and no box of tools between them.

He put the sheet on the middle of the seed-house table, beside the fieldbook and the use log and the two hundred pages of the Ashcombe index that had been standing open there since the morning before, and he did not look at the press and he did not look at the inside of Marek's forearm, where his own collar had been on the thirtieth.

"I did not write it," he said. "I will say that first because it is the only thing in this room that is about me. I did not write it and I did not send it and I did not know it was coming, and I have been up since the fourth and I have not been able to find out who did."

Marek unfolded it. It was two pages, stitched at the top, with the mark of the professional body on the front, and under the mark a line that named the district and the year, and under that a title that said nothing about a fen. The body was the same body Pike's circular had gone out in, the same body whose roll carried Renner's own license and nine other men's in this district, and its quarterly went to every address on that roll and to a list of others, and the list of others was four hundred doors.

Inside, under a plain heading, was a description of a result.

It said that a wetted lot of seed, laid against a living bead cut from grafted stock, would report the ground it was grown on, in four seconds, without a clerk. It gave figures for a lot of one variety, weight and water and time and number of trials, and it gave them straight, the way a man gives figures he has checked. There was no name on it. There was no license number on it. There was no yard and no district and no holder.

"It is accurate," said Tova Reed, from the other end of the table. She had read it twice while Marek read it once. "Every line of it. The time. The water. The number. That is the problem."

Renner nodded. He had taken his hat off and he held it in both hands, which was not like him. His man waited at the door with his hands behind his back. The yard outside went on with its morning: Ilsa Kern on the embankment with a bar, Jonas Byre at the channel, Gael Senn at the well house with her book, the pump running, the lamp fed, the tin unmoved under the ledger in the open press, forty-nine days at risk.

"Four things," Renner said. "I did not write it. I cannot stop it. It is technically correct. And a technically correct description of a device that makes a seed lie is the most dangerous thing anybody has ever printed about this holding. I have carried a bead for five years and I know what it does. Print the doing and every licensed man in the district can do it by winter."

"How many licensed men in this district," said Dunmar Fitch, from the end of the table with his rule beside his hand.

"Four in Tarrow, including me," said Renner. "And there is nobody in the next valley over who could not be one of the four inside a season, because the body examines on a working and not on a paper, and a man who has watched me do it four times in a yard has the whole of it."

"Nine men at your yard," said Marek.

"Nine men and four of them could do a holding for a season," said Renner, "and I have told my foreman about this article and he has read it over my shoulder, because I would have found out on Thursday at the latest and I would rather he heard it from me. That is what I have done about it. That is the whole of what I have done about it."

Dunmar Fitch read it a third time, with a rule on the page, and then he set the rule down.

"It does not name us," he said.

"No," said Renner. "It does not have to. A reader who wants a bead does not need our name. He needs the figures. The figures are here. Any licensed man in this district can cut a bead out of grafted stock in an afternoon. Any seed house with a wet cloth can do the rest in four seconds. The article is not about us. It is about what can be done."

Lissa Vale had been standing at the press with her hands on the ledger. She did not touch the journal.

"Then what is wrong with it," she said. "If it is true."

"Nothing is wrong with it," said Renner. "That is what I came to say. Nobody in this fen can say what is wrong with it, because there is nothing wrong with it as a description. It is right. And a right description of a thing that overwrites is an attack, whether the writer meant it or not. A graft keeps the thing put in and loses the thing that was there. Print that in a quarterly that goes to four hundred doors and somebody will use it to make a seed report whatever history he needs."

Gael Senn looked up from the standing pressure log, where she had been entering the morning's line.

"Then the book is the defense," she said.

"Yes," said Renner. "It is the only one anybody has. And a book only works if somebody reads it. Your book is on this table and anybody may ask to have it read. Their bead is in a tin and anybody may buy one. Which of those two is going to travel further by winter."

Nobody answered that. The answer was in the room and nobody wanted to say it.

"It is also the only defense anybody can sell," said Renner, after a moment, and nobody had asked him. "That is the part I would put in your book if you would let me. I could not stop a man in a seed house two hundred miles off from taking a shilling for a bead and doing it lawfully under his own license, and I would not be able to complain, and neither would you. What is printed in a quarterly of a professional body is not a raid. It is the trade. It goes where the trade goes, it costs what the trade charges, and every act of it is correct and timely and structural, and if it ruins a seed house in this fen that is a cost and not a crime."

Renner had said the last four words the way a man reads out a clause, and Marek noticed that it was the same voice the district used and that neither of them had any right to be amused by.

"It is the same sentence twice," said Dunmar Fitch. "Once for the district and once for you. I am entering that and I am entering it as a thing a body has been told by two different people on the same morning, and not as an accusation against either of them, because I have had three this season and I have been wrong about one."

Then he folded the journal shut and entered it as received, with the hour, a trade article describing a bead result, with figures for a lot of one variety, no name and no license number, description accurate, read at this table. Anneke Roos initialled the entry as an opinion. Lissa Vale moved the journal to the end of the table, away from the fieldbook and the use log and the index, so that nobody coming in afterward would think the four belonged together.

The afternoon of the eighth was spent the way afternoons in that yard were always spent, in work that did not stop because a paper had arrived. Marek went with Jonas Byre to the return main at the eleventh rod to check the cheap reed gauge clamped there on the twenty-first, two months back, three-sixteenths above the mark at the last reading, pump on the idle. The reed stood steady. Jonas read it and Marek read it after him, and neither wrote a number that was not there, because a number with no baseline and no load was not evidence and not a claim, and both of them had learned that lesson in a flood.

"What does the article change," said Jonas, wiping his hands on his coat.

"Nothing in the ground," said Marek. "Everything on paper. A truth can be an attack and still be true. That is what we have to carry."

They walked back along the ditch where the thirty-one yards of new run lay under its fiber bed, with the four willow sets green at the head. The water moved steadily, eleven minutes from the main to the scour, the way it had since the twenty-second, two months back. The graft held. The return line stood three hundred and ninety-nine short in a licensed hand. Both were true at once, and the yard had learned to hold both without dropping either.

At supper Tova Reed spread the journal on the table again and went through the figures line by line with Gael Senn, weight against water against time, checking each against the bead demonstrations of the thirtieth of last month. The figures held. That was the worst of it. A false article could be answered. A true one could only be lived with. Dunmar Fitch sat with them and entered the checking itself, with the hour, figures checked against the yard's own trials of the thirtieth of last month, no fault found, which is itself a finding. Anneke Roos initialled it as an opinion. Lissa Vale kept the press open and the tin where it was and said nothing whatever about the journal, which was her way of saying what she thought of printers.

"What do we do," said Gael Senn, "when somebody builds one."

"We keep the book," said Marek. "A bead reports in four seconds what it was last made to hold. A book reports what was done, with the hour, whether or not it helps. They are not the same thing, and the difference will not be printed in anybody's quarterly, because a quarterly sells the thing that works."

Renner stood with his hat in his hands, looking at the inside of Marek's forearm.

"I cut mine five years ago out of a grafted bean for a woman in a market," he said. "It has been right every time since, and every time it has been right it has made me less sure what right means. Articles do not carry what a man thinks at night. I will not build another this season, not for sale, not for hire. I have nine men and they will want me to, and I am telling this table first and I will be telling them on Friday, and if somebody offers me money for a collar by the middle of next month I will come up this road and say so in writing, because I would rather be the man who says it than the man who is found out."

He stayed the rest of the morning, on his own account, and did not ask to see the fieldbook and did not ask to see the use log, and Marek noticed both of the not-askings and understood them.

On the ninth Marek walked down to Fennmere with Renner to look at the yard where the nine men worked, because Renner had asked him to and because it was better than sitting in the seed house with a journal on the table. The yard was clean. The tools stood in order. The copper clips hung on a board in a row, and there were nine of them, and Renner counted them as he passed and said nothing. A foreman who did not want to be told he was doing it wrong nodded to Marek and went back to his work.

"You see," said Renner. "I am inside that body. My license is the same license as the circular on four hundred doors. The Assembly has not acted against you and is not going to. A trade article in a professional quarterly is not an act by an office. It is a man writing down what works. Do not let a later room confuse the two, and do not let me be the man who taught you to."

"I will not," said Marek.

"And one more thing, and then I will leave you to your morning." Renner stopped at the gate of the yard. "I have been trying since the fourth to work out how a man with a grievance got hold of what I did in your yard on the thirtieth. It was not you. It was not the fen's book, because your book does not describe the method. It was not the woman who came on the sixth with a list of eleven holdings, because she would not have known what to ask for. It was somebody who sat where I sat. That is the whole of my finding and I am telling you it and not keeping it, because if I keep it I am the only man in this district with a copy of it and a copy of a thing like that is how these things start."

They did not get to the bottom of it and they were not going to in that yard, and Renner said so and Marek wrote it down, with the hour, in the fen's own book, as a thing looked for and not found, and Dunmar Fitch read it back and insisted the word *leak* not go in and a different word go in instead, because a leak is a thing you put a rule to and this was not one.

On the way back they passed the post cart going the other way with the Thursday post in it, and the Thursday post was the day before, so what came past was a reminder and not a delivery. Tova Reed was at the seed-house table when Marek came in, with a letter open in front of her that had come on that cart that morning. It was short. It was from a seed house in a town two hundred miles from here, a town with no fen in it and no charter and no office of any kind in it that anybody had ever heard of, and it was addressed to a counting house in Cray.

She read it aloud, once, without softening it.

"He has read the article," she said. "He wants to know whether the Ashcombe black oat is the one in the index. He has asked the trust, because the trust is the only body he has heard of that stands behind a name, and he has asked in the ordinary way, and he has paid a shilling for the winter issue like everybody else."

The room sat with that for a while. Marek thought about the tin with no label that had come on a cart eleven years ago through a man who wanted to be helpful, and the eleven years of arguing that had followed, and the four seconds on the thirtieth that had given a better answer and a lie in the same breath. He thought about the four hundred doors the circular had gone to, and the hundred and forty sheets the index went to, and the one quarterly that went wherever quarterlies went, and how the three numbers together made a room eleven miles long that no body could hold.

Dunmar Fitch broke the silence the way he always broke it, with a question about procedure.

"Do we answer," he said. "The letter is to Cray, not to us. The trust answers. Do we write anything on our own account."

"No," said Tova Reed. "The trust answers. We keep the book. The book says the oat came in a tin with no label eleven years ago and is not the oat and is not anything anybody can name, and the bead said Ashcombe and the season before last and the ground under a ley. Both stand. A reader in a town with no fen will have to decide which kind of answer he wants. We do not decide it for him. And if he decides wrong, he will lose a shilling, which is what a buyer loses, and it is not our business and we may not make it ours."

Lissa Vale nodded once, slowly, and moved the journal further from the fieldbook, to the very end of the table, where the light fell off it.
