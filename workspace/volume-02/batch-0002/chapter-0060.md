# Chapter 60

## The Market Does Not Notice

The winter issue came to the seed house on the fifth in a parcel that was not addressed to them.

It was not Vesta Lund's parcel. It was the Fennmere post's own copy, left on the seed-house table by a carter who had no reason to leave it except that the rain had stopped and he had time. The wrapper said Ashcombe, in print, and under it a line that said winter issue, and under that a number that said how many copies had been printed. Marek did not read the number at first. Tova Reed did.

"One hundred and forty," she said. "To seed houses. For this winter."

She set it down beside the fieldbook and the use log, in the middle of the table, where things went now. The press stood open behind her with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved, forty-six days on the fifth. Nobody had lifted it. Nobody was going to.

Dunmar Fitch came in at about the ninth hour with his cuffs clean and his book under his arm, and he looked at the parcel for a long time before he touched it.

"Is that ours," he said.

"No," said Tova Reed. "It is theirs. It is what we refused."

He nodded and opened his own book to the page for the fifth and wrote without being asked. That was his habit. When a thing happened in the yard he put it where a hearing officer could find it, in forty words or fewer, with the hour on it.

"The refusal was entered on the twenty-eighth of last month," he read back. "The sum not received. A shilling a line for every line that names a variety. The number of houses the refusal reached is one."

Nobody said anything to that. It sat in the middle of the table with the parcel beside it. The lamp burned above them. Outside, the pump ran on with its steady tremor, and Jonas Byre could be heard at the culvert counting minutes out loud, the way he had since the crest night, writing down whatever the hook brought out and how big.

"It reached us," said Marek, after a while. "We heard it."

"Yes," said Dunmar Fitch. "One house. Ours. The index goes to a hundred and forty houses this winter and none of them will know a fen seed house said no to a shilling a line. A refusal nobody hears is indistinguishable from a holding that has nothing. I am putting that in because it is true and because I am the clerk, and not because I think it helps."

Tova Reed had her own book open. She had been doing arithmetic since the morning, in pencil, on a sheet of seed-house paper, and the arithmetic was worse than it had been on the twenty-eighth and she did not hide that either.

"On the twenty-eighth I refused a sum," she said. "A shilling a line. The ledger names forty-one lines. That is forty-one shillings. Two pounds and a shilling. The trust's copy is in a counting house nine hundred miles away. The trust's answer to the district's paper has to be written this week. The woman writing it does not have our arithmetic in front of her."

She turned the sheet round so Marek could see it.

"The forty-one lines are not forty-one sales," she said. "Nine hundred smallholders buy the index. If one in twenty buys a line because it is printed, that is forty-five sales a line. At our own price, kept by us, that is seed we do not have to buy in. Refused, it is nothing. Printed by somebody else, it is somebody else's."

Marek looked at the parcel and did not open it.

"Open it," said Dunmar Fitch. "We have paid for the knowledge. We may as well have the thing."

Tova Reed cut the string with the seed-house knife and laid the sheets out, one after another, on the table beside the open book. The print was clean. The names stood in two columns, with a district against each, and a price against some, and a mark against others. The Ashcombe black oat was there, in its column, with the printed name under it. Nobody at that table looked at it longer than they had to. Dunmar Fitch set his rule on the margin, checked the alignment of the columns the way he checked everything printed, and found it true.

"It does not know us," said Marek.

"No," said Tova Reed. "It was never going to. A market that has been refused does not stop. That was not the offer. The offer was that a landrace in one book in one fen is one flood from gone. The refusal does not answer that. It answers whether we will be paid for the copying. The copying goes on without us. One hundred and forty copies, two hundred pages apiece. Nine hundred smallholders. The arithmetic does not need our permission."

Then Dunmar Fitch asked the question that turned the afternoon into something the yard had not expected, and he asked it in the flat voice he used for procedure.

"What does the winter issue cost."

Tova Reed had the price in the wrapper and had not looked at it because she had been looking at the number of sheets.

"Two shillings," she said.

"Put it to the body," said Dunmar Fitch. "Not to buy the lines. To buy the sheet. If this holding is going to be refused in a market of one hundred and forty houses, then this holding ought to know what the hundred and forty houses know, and should not know it second-hand off a carter who had time because the rain stopped."

Nobody argued with that, and the vote, when it was taken standing in the seed house door with four households present, took under a minute and was not a vote so much as an acknowledgment that there was nothing to vote about. The two shillings went out the same afternoon in a hand to a carter going into Fennmere, because the post was two days off and this holding had spent a season waiting on posts, with a line in the seed house's own account against seed-house paper, and Dunmar Fitch entered the sum and the purpose in his own book with the hour, because a body that buys its enemy's printer and enters the cost is a body that has understood something, and a body that buys its enemy's printer and does not enter the cost is a body that will lie about it later.

The fen's own copy came down from Fennmere on the seventh, a day after the money went out, and by then the post's own copy had been lying open on this table for two days, and it went onto the middle of the table beside the fieldbook and the use log, in the place where things went, and stayed there for the rest of the batch.

Lissa Vale came out of the press with her hands wet and looked at the two hundred pages standing open on her table and did not touch them.

"Two shillings," she said.

"Two shillings a season would be nothing," said Tova Reed. "Two shillings is a subscription. That is the whole of the difference, and it took us a fortnight to see it."

"Nobody in this yard has read four hundred names in a book before," said Lissa Vale, "except the woman who read them twice in one afternoon because she is seventy-one and does not sleep."

On the morning of the sixth Marek walked the seed house with Lissa Vale and checked the ledger of varieties against the index, line by line, without writing anything. The three names the reader had lifted on the fourth stood on their sheet beside the ledger, entered against it and not in it: a bean, an oat, a rye that was not a rye. None of the three was in the printed issue. Lissa Vale touched the sheet once, lightly, the way she touched things that were true and not helpful.

"The reader said if anybody wanted to buy a line out of that book they could not tell," said Lissa Vale. "The index does not want those three. It wants the forty-one we refused. Both are the same lesson. A book anybody may copy is a book anybody may use, and a book nobody copies is a book nobody can check. We stand between the two with a shilling refused and nothing to show for it but the refusal."

"And two shillings spent," said Marek.

"And two shillings spent," said Lissa Vale. "Which is the first money this holding has put into a market in a month, and it went the other way, and I would like it in the book that I thought of it and Lissa Vale did not, because Lissa Vale is going to hear about it for a year."

"It is entered," said Marek. "With your name against the thought and not against the purchase."

On the sixth the yard tried to do the work as usual and found the refusal in everything.

Gael Senn came up from the well house at about the seventh hour with the morning's number on her page and read it back before she initialled it, which is the fifth sheet's rule for a return line and this yard's habit in everything else. The leak was five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, with two rules, in daylight, pump on. The man of about fifty who had come to hear the reading stood with his hands in his pockets and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that had not been said on the first of this month.

"The pit has four feet of water in it," he said at last. "And it is not up."

"No," said Gael Senn. "It is into the housing. That is the only job the housing has."

After she had gone Marek walked the outer field with Ilsa Kern and looked at the nineteen acres that were still standing and the drill where the Longneck stood in eleven trays' worth, leaning eleven degrees along its whole length. The starve was not visible from the top of the field. That was his own note, entered against the interest of the application, and it was still true.

"We refused three buyers," said Ilsa Kern. "The broker. The buyer. The contractor. All correct. All refused for reasons I would sign again."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Then why does the yard feel like a man who has lost something."

"Because we have," said Marek. "We have lost the money and kept the reason. The reason does not buy compost."

They walked the headland to the low corner where the toe ditch ran, fourteen feet wide and four foot six deep since the night's work in the flood, with the lip off the inside. The water stood steady. Jonas Byre was on the bank with two levy men, counting minutes out loud the way he had learned on the crest night, writing down whatever the hook brought out of the culvert and how big.

"The ditch holds," said Ilsa Kern. "It is ours. Nobody is going to buy it because nobody can carry it away. That is more than can be said for a name in a book."

On the evening of the sixth Marek sat with Gael Senn in the seed house and went through the monthly report, nine lines and short, a charter condition, due on the first of every month. The graft's four willow sets were alive and the anchor held. What the root had changed, beyond standing green where it had stood green, nobody could say, and Dunmar Fitch wrote that they could not say it, and Marek initialled it.

"We are filing a paper that says we do not know," said Gael Senn.

"We are filing a paper that says what we did," said Marek. "That is what the second wording is for. Where a thing required did not happen, the entry records that it did not happen. Where a thing changed and we cannot name it, the entry records that we looked."

She read the nine lines back, twice, and initialled each one. The lamp burned on its living bulb above them, fed and warm, and outside the pump ran on, steady, with the leak passing five-eighths into the housing and the pit holding four feet without rising.

At supper the yard talked about the index the way people talk about weather. The man of about seventy put his hand flat on the table and said his holding had bought off an index once, years back, and the line had failed in the second year, and nobody had written to anybody about it. The woman with the shilling said her sister's house had a printed name on the wall and the name was wrong and they had kept it because it was print. Tova Reed listened to all of it without writing any of it down, which was not her habit, and Marek understood that she was letting the room have one evening in which it was not evidence.

On the seventh Dunmar Fitch brought the parcel out to the middle of the table and put his entry beside it and read both aloud, in daylight, to five households who had come when a thing was read out. He read the refusal first and the index number second, and then he said the sentence the yard would keep.

"A refusal is a thing this body can only do in a room with people in it," he said. "The room we refused in had sixteen households in it, and four of them by paper. The room the index goes to is eleven miles long in every direction and there is no way to hold it. We have discovered that refusal is a place, and that the place is this yard, and that outside this yard it is weather."

Nobody argued with him. Anneke Roos wrote her initial against the entry, which is what she does when she is recording an opinion rather than doing the work.

The seventh hour of the seventh was when a carter came up the fen road with a message from Fennmere, spoken, not written: a licensed contractor had read something and had a thing to say and would be up the road before the week was out. He would not come past the gate to say it and Marek did not ask him to.

Afterward Tova Reed sat at the counting-house end of the table with the district's paper in front of her, the water-supply bylaw, second part, form C/4, served on the trust at Cray on the twenty-seventh, a copy of everything to go whether or not it helps, and she wrote two lines and stopped.

"The March's answer has to be written this week," she said. "I have written two paragraphs at that table before and put them on the Thursday post. This one is harder. The woman writing it is nine hundred miles away and she does not have our three acres in her."

Marek did not ask what she meant. He knew what she meant. The three acres at the top of the outer field were his own ground, worked on his own account for three seasons because the cooperative would not have them, gone sour in a week, and it is three weeks since, which is how a thing stops being news in a yard and starts being a fact about a man, and they were not in the cooperative's book as a loss to anybody but him. A woman nine hundred miles away could write about a charter condition. She could not write about a ditch that had taken a boot and let it go.

"The date," said Dunmar Fitch, from the other end of the table. "What is the date on the notice."

"The notice says fourteen days of the date of the notice," said Tova Reed. "And the notice came here on the twenty-seventh and it was addressed to Cray, and it was served on the trust when it got to Cray, and Cray is nine hundred miles by the March road and a hundred and eleven further by the ordinary cart. We are not the addressee. We have never seen a copy of that notice and we are not entitled to one. The district counts the day of service, and every clerk in that building counts it, and the only service date this yard can work at is the first of this month, which is four days on the road from the twenty-seventh, and on that date the last day of the fourteen is the fourteenth of this month. That is a date we have made up. It is the best one we have."

"Seven days from today," said Dunmar Fitch, and entered it, and read it back, and did not say the other thing, which was that nobody in this yard could check it, because the only person who could check it was nine hundred miles away in a counting house and had not written.

"Write what the book says," Marek said.

"I am," said Tova Reed. "That is the trouble. The book says we refused. It does not say what the refusal did."

Later that day Ilsa Kern brought the mill floor figures up from the seed house, labor twice a day with two names on it, and Dunmar Fitch entered them against the return. The compost stood at nothing put in, none available, specification four hundred hundredweight a month, last put in twenty-one hundredweight and a quarter on the fourteenth of last month by levy. The bed stood at three and a half degrees. The tin stood under the ledger, forty-eight days and unmoved.

"We are keeping a book nobody reads," said the man of about seventy, watching Dunmar write. "One hundred and forty houses get an index. One house gets our refusal. Two shillings buys us the other hundred and forty. What is the book for."

"It is for the one house," said Dunmar Fitch, without looking up. "And for the officer who comes. And for the stranger. It is not for the one hundred and forty. We knew that on the twenty-third and we know it now. A refusal nobody hears costs the refuser and nobody else. That is what refusing costs."
