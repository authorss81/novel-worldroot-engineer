# Chapter 60

## The Market Does Not Notice

The index came back on the fourth in a parcel that was not addressed to them.

It was not Vesta Lund's parcel. It was the Fennmere post's own copy, left on the seed-house table by a carter who had no reason to leave it except that the rain had stopped and he had time. The wrapper said Ashcombe, in print, and under it a line that said winter issue, and under that a number that said how many sheets had been printed. Marek did not read the number at first. Tova Reed did.

"One hundred and forty," she said. "To seed houses. For this winter."

She set it down beside the fieldbook and the use log, in the middle of the table, where things went now. The press stood open behind her with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved. The tin had been there fifty days on the fourth. Nobody had lifted it. Nobody was going to.

Dunmar Fitch came in at about the ninth hour with his cuffs clean and his book under his arm, and he looked at the parcel for a long time before he touched it.

"Is that ours," he said.

"No," said Tova Reed. "It is theirs. It is what we refused."

He nodded and opened his own book to the page for the fourth and wrote without being asked. That was his habit. When a thing happened in the yard he put it where a hearing officer could find it, in forty words or fewer, with the hour on it.

"The refusal was entered on the twenty-eighth," he read back, when he had finished. "The sum not received. A shilling a line for every line that names a variety. The number of houses the refusal reached is one."

Nobody said anything to that. It sat in the middle of the table with the parcel beside it. The lamp burned above them. Outside, the pump ran on with its steady tremor, and Jonas Byre could be heard at the culvert counting minutes out loud, the way he had since the crest night, writing down whatever the hook brought out and how big.

"It reached us," said Marek, after a while. "We heard it."

"Yes," said Dunmar Fitch. "One house. Ours. The index goes to one hundred and forty houses this winter and none of them will know a fen seed house said no to a shilling a line. A refusal nobody hears is indistinguishable from a holding that has nothing. I am putting that in because it is true and because I am the clerk, and not because I think it helps."

Tova Reed had her own book open. She had been doing arithmetic since the morning, in pencil, on a sheet of seed-house paper, and the arithmetic was worse than it had been on the twenty-eighth and she did not hide that either.

"On the twenty-eighth I refused a sum," she said. "A shilling a line. The ledger names forty-one lines. That is forty-one shillings. Two pounds and a shilling. The trust's copy is in a counting house nine hundred miles away. The trust's answer to the district's paper has to be written this week. The woman writing it does not have our arithmetic in front of her."

She turned the sheet round so Marek could see it.

"The forty-one lines are not forty-one sales," she said. "Nine hundred smallholders buy the index. If one in twenty buys a line because it is printed, that is forty-five sales a line. At our own price, kept by us, that is seed we do not have to buy in. Refused, it is nothing. Printed by somebody else, it is somebody else's. There is no third place for it to be."

Marek looked at the parcel and did not open it.

"Open it," said Dunmar Fitch. "We have paid for the knowledge. We may as well have the thing."

Tova Reed cut the string with the seed-house knife and laid the sheets out, one after another, on the table beside the open book. The print was clean. The names stood in two columns, with a district against each, and a price against some, and a mark against others. The Ashcombe black oat was there, in its column, with the printed name under it. Nobody at that table looked at it longer than they had to. Dunmar Fitch set his rule on the margin and checked the alignment of the columns, the way he checked everything printed, and found it true.

"It does not know us," said Marek.

"No," said Tova Reed. "It was never going to. A market that has been refused does not stop. That was not the offer. The offer was that a landrace in one book in one fen is one flood from gone. The refusal does not answer that. It answers whether we will be paid for the copying. The copying goes on without us. One hundred and forty sheets. Nine hundred smallholders. The arithmetic does not need our permission."

On the morning of the fifth Marek walked the seed house with Lissa Vale and checked the ledger of varieties against the index, line by line, without writing anything. The three names the reader had lifted on the fourth of this month stood on their sheet beside the ledger, entered against it and not in it: a bean, an oat, a rye that was not a rye. None of the three was in the printed issue. Lissa Vale touched the sheet once, lightly, the way she touched things that were true and not helpful.

"She said if anybody wanted to buy a line out of that book they could not tell," said Lissa Vale. "The index does not want those three. It wants the forty-one we refused. Both are the same lesson. A book anybody may copy is a book anybody may use, and a book nobody copies is a book nobody can check. We stand between the two with a shilling refused and nothing to show for it but the refusal."

On the fifth the yard tried to do the work as usual and found the refusal in everything.

Gael Senn came up from the well house at about the seventh hour with the morning's number on her page and read it back before she initialled it, the way the vote of the eighteenth of last month said she must. The leak was five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, with two rules, in daylight, pump on. She wrote it and read it and initialled it, and the man of about fifty who had come to hear the reading stood with his hands in his pockets and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that had not been said on the first.

"The pit has four feet of water in it," he said at last. "And it is not up."

"No," said Gael Senn. "It is into the housing. That is the only job the housing has."

After she had gone Marek walked the outer field with Ilsa Kern and looked at the nineteen acres that were still standing and the drill where the Longneck stood in eleven trays' worth, leaning a little along its whole length. The starve was not visible from the top of the field. That was his own note, entered against the interest of the application, and it was still true.

"We refused three buyers," said Ilsa Kern. "The broker. The buyer. The contractor. All correct. All refused for reasons I would sign again."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Then why does the yard feel like a man who has lost something."

"Because we have," said Marek. "We have lost the money and kept the reason. The reason does not buy compost."

They walked the headland to the low corner where the toe ditch ran, fourteen feet wide and four foot six deep since the night's work in the flood, with the lip off the inside. The water stood steady. Jonas Byre was on the bank with two levy men, counting minutes out loud the way he had learned on the crest night, writing down whatever the hook brought out of the culvert and how big. He nodded to Marek and went back to his count.

"The ditch holds," said Ilsa Kern. "It is ours. Nobody is going to buy it because nobody can carry it away. That is more than can be said for a name in a book."

On the evening of the fifth Marek sat with Gael Senn in the seed house and went through the monthly report that was due on the first of every month, nine lines and short, a charter condition now. The root had changed in ways nobody in the yard could name. The graft's four willow sets were alive. The anchor held. What the root had changed, beyond standing green where it had stood green, nobody could say, and Dunmar Fitch wrote that they could not say it, and Marek initialled it.

"We are filing a paper that says we do not know," said Gael Senn.

"We are filing a paper that says what we did," said Marek. "That is what the second wording is for. Where a thing required did not happen, the entry records that it did not happen. Where a thing changed and we cannot name it, the entry records that we looked."

She read the nine lines back, twice, and initialled each one. The lamp burned on its living bulb above them, fed and warm, and outside the pump ran on, steady, with the leak passing five-eighths into the housing and the pit holding four feet without rising.

At supper the yard talked about the index the way people talk about weather — as a thing that would come whether or not they watched it. The man of about seventy put his hand flat on the table and said his holding had bought off an index once, years back, and the line had failed in the second year, and nobody had written to anybody about it. The woman with the shilling said her sister's house had a printed name on the wall and the name was wrong and they had kept it because it was print. Tova Reed listened to all of it without writing any of it down, which was not her habit, and Marek understood that she was letting the room have one evening in which it was not evidence.

On the sixth Dunmar Fitch brought the parcel back to the table and put his entry beside it and read both aloud, in daylight, to five households who had come when a thing was read out. He read the refusal first and the index number second, and then he said the sentence the batch would keep.

"A refusal is a thing this body can only do in a room with people in it," he said. "The room we refused in had sixteen households in it, and four of them by paper. The room the index goes to is eleven miles long in every direction and there is no way to hold it. We have discovered that refusal is a place, and that the place is this yard, and that outside this yard it is weather."

Nobody argued with him. Anneke Roos wrote her initial against the entry, which is what she does when she is recording an opinion rather than doing the work.

Afterward Tova Reed sat at the counting-house end of the table with the district's paper in front of her — the water-supply bylaw, second part, form C/4, served on the trust at Cray on the twenty-seventh, fourteen days, a copy of everything to go whether or not it helps — and she wrote two lines and stopped.

"The trust's answer has to be written this week," she said. "I have written two paragraphs at that table before and put them on the Thursday post. This one is harder. The woman writing it is nine hundred miles away and she does not have our three acres in her."

Marek did not ask what she meant. He knew what she meant. The three acres at the top of the outer field were his own ground, worked on his own account for three seasons because the cooperative would not have them, gone sour in a week in the middle of last month, and they were not in the cooperative's book as a loss to anybody but him. A woman nine hundred miles away could write about a charter condition. She could not write about a ditch that had taken a boot and let it go.

"Write what the book says," he said.

"I am," said Tova Reed. "That is the trouble. The book says we refused. It does not say what the refusal did."

On the morning of the sixth Marek went down to the well house before first light with Gael Senn to take the week's check on the head, because the charter made the examination monthly and the habit made it daily. The lamp burned low. The pump ran on, steady, with the familiar tremor in the frame from about one thousand and forty upward. Gael read the dial from above the pit out of thirty years of habit learned from Jonas Byre, and Marek read it after her, and the two readings agreed.

"The housing is warm," said Gael Senn, with her hand near the six o'clock corner but not on it. "Five-eighths. Into the housing. The pit holds four feet and is not up a quarter of an inch."

"Write it," said Marek. "With the hour. Read it back."

She wrote it and read it back, in the cold, with her breath showing, and initialled it. The entry was short. The shortness was the point. A book that anybody might read had to be readable in the cold by a person with wet hands.

Later that day Ilsa Kern brought the mill floor figures up from the seed house, labor twice a day with two names on it, and Dunmar Fitch entered them against the return. The compost stood at nothing put in, none available, specification four hundred hundredweight a month, last put in twenty-one hundredweight and a quarter on the fourteenth by levy. The bed stood at three and a half degrees, measured twice with the soil iron. The tin stood under the ledger, fifty days and then fifty-one, unmoved.

"We are keeping a book nobody reads," said the man of about seventy, watching Dunmar write. "One hundred and forty houses get an index. One house gets our refusal. What is the book for."

"It is for the one house," said Dunmar Fitch, without looking up. "And for the officer who comes. And for the stranger. It is not for the one hundred and forty. We knew that on the twenty-third and we know it now. A refusal nobody hears costs the refuser and nobody else. That is what refusing costs. Write it down and it is still true in winter."
