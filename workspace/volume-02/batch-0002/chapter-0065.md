# Chapter 65

## The Seed Buyer Writes Back

The morning of the seventeenth had been spent in ordinary work, because the sheet went to press whether or not the fen watched it. Marek walked the nineteen acres with Ilsa Kern and found the Longneck standing, leaning eleven degrees along its whole length, the way Gael Senn had entered it. The graft's four willow sets held green. The pump ran. The leak passed into the housing at five-eighths. The pit held four feet without rising. None of it was news. All of it went in the book, with the hour, read back before it was initialled, because the habit was already there, and the body had voted it the day before, in daylight, for anything entered from that day forward.

The Ashcombe sheet had gone to press on the sixteenth, and nobody in the fen knew it, and it came down the road on the eighteenth in the Sunday post with the trust's letter behind it.

Tova Reed held it in both hands for a long time before she read it aloud. It was a single sheet, printed to go inside the winter issue, and it said, about one variety, that the source was disputed and that the printed name was not established. It did not name a holder. It did not name a district. It named the oat, and it named the doubt, and that was enough. The print was clean. The doubt stood in the same type as the names.

A seed house two hundred miles from here read it and did not buy that line. The letter saying so came on the same post, addressed to the counting house in Cray, with a second copy to this fen because the fen is the seed house that holds the ledger the buyer has asked about, and a buyer who has written to a trust about a name writes to the seed house as well and says in the second copy that she has done so.

The buyer wrote plainly, the way buyers write when they are doing arithmetic and not making a point: with the source doubtful, her house could not buy. She was sorry. She would look again when the name was established, and she would take a line of forty-five bags at a time when it was, and she would rather hear from the seed house than from the index.

The line was the March's. The March's holding was the trust's. The trust was a named party to a charter condition, with the name Salt March Dryfoot Trust, of Cray, on the fifth sheet. Tova Reed's decision on the twenty-eighth of last month to refuse a shilling a line had cost the March a sale. There was no other way to put it, and she did not try to find one.

She had the trust's own book open in front of her with a pencil in her hand and did not put the point on the page. Her hand did not shake. Dunmar Fitch held the book steady for her the way he held it for everybody, and nobody in that room hurried her, because the line she was about to write was the only thing the fen had that afternoon.

She opened the buyer's second copy first, at the same table, without getting up, the way she opened everything, with the knife and the ruler squared to the edge. Then she read her own entry back twice, because it was the first line she had ever entered that said she was wrong about a thing and not about a number.

"The seventh line against the trust's interest becomes the eighth," she read back, standing, while Dunmar Fitch held the pen ready. "And it is the first one that says I was wrong about something and not about a number. I refused the index to keep the lines unowned. The index has answered by marking one line doubtful. A buyer doing arithmetic will not buy a doubtful line. That is not an attack. It is a buyer doing arithmetic, and I was wrong about the cost. The March has lost a sale it would have had if I had taken the shilling, and I want the sentence in the book exactly as I have said it, and I do not want it softened by anybody including me."

The yard wanted to call it an attack. The man of about fifty said so first, loudly, in the yard afterward, with four households round him. A printer who marks a line doubtful after a refusal, he said, was punishing a refusal, and eleven years ago a Crown seed officer with a clerk and a list had copied the March's lines into a printed table and put the black oat in the wrong column, and everybody in this yard had spent a season being punished by a printer. Ilsa Kern said it second, more quietly, at the well house, with a bar in her hand and the water running brown past the toe. Marek listened to both of them and said nothing until the evening, and then he said it at the table, with the hour on it.

Dunmar Fitch would not let the word stand.

"It is not an attack," he said, and he made Marek put the proposal and the refusal on the page together, with Marek's name against the proposal. "A buyer who reads that a name is not established and does not buy is doing what our own reader did on the fourth of last month when she said she could not tell whether three names were wrong. We do not get to call it an attack because it hurts the trust. It hurts the trust. That is all it does. A district that prints a category cannot be caught at anything, because the category is true. A printer who prints a doubt cannot be caught at anything, because the doubt is true. The oat is doubtful. We have said so ourselves for eleven years, in two books, and one of those books is a book the reader may read. If a doubt is a wrong then eleven years of this holding is a wrong, and I would like somebody to say that in a yard before we decide the printer is the villain of it."

Dunmar Fitch read the sheet aloud afterward in the yard, to the five households who came, standing on the well house step the way Marek had read the fifth sheet on the twenty-third. He read the doubt first and the buyer's letter second, and then Tova Reed's eighth line third, and he did not hurry any of the three. The man of about seventy listened with his hand flat on the well house door, the way he had listened to the expiry sentence, and asked for his initial on nothing, because there was nothing to initial. A feeling about a doubt was worth nothing and it cost the holding nothing, and it was going to cost a great deal in a fortnight if nobody wrote down what was actually on the paper, and the paper was the only thing in that room that was a fact about the future.

In the evening Marek sat with Tova Reed at the counting-house end of the table and went through the trust's arithmetic for the lost sale, crate by crate, the way they had gone through the levy arithmetic on the twenty-fourth of last month. The sale would have been forty-five bags at the trust's price, kept by the March, carried north at the buyer's cost. Lost, it was nothing. The eighth line stood against it. Tova wrote the number of bags in pencil beside the entry and then struck it out, because a number of bags that were not sold was not a fact about a book, and Dunmar Fitch made her initial the striking, which she did.

The trust's letter was behind the buyer's and it was short, in a clerk's hand from Cray, nine hundred miles by the March road, and it asked the seed house to say, in writing, what it knew about the Ashcombe oat. Not what the trust knew. What the seed house knew, that held the ledger the index wants. The buyer's own question was enclosed with it, word for word, and the trust had written under it that the trust was not able to answer for a line it did not grow and had asked the seed house because the seed house is the body that holds the names.

And then Tova Reed did the thing that made this the worst day the fen had had since the eighth of last month, which was that she turned the two letters round so that Dunmar Fitch could see the second copy of the buyer's letter, which carried a line at the foot that a careful person writes when they are being careful. The line said that her house understood the seed house's position, and that she would be glad of an answer in writing, and that her house would wait until the fourteenth for one before it took the question to her own printer.

"The fourteenth," said Dunmar Fitch.

"The fourteenth was four days ago," said Tova Reed. "It was the last day of the fourteen days of the district's notice, and it is in this holding's own book in the clerk's hand, entered on the seventh and entered again on the fourteenth with the hour on both. We are four days past it on a notice we were never served with, and the trust is four days past the district's date on a notice we did serve them by forwarding, and I cannot write to Cray to ask either of those two things, because the moment this holding writes about a notice that was not served on it is the moment this holding is a party to a notice it was never given."

"Then we are four days late," said Marek.

"We are four days late," said Tova Reed, "and I am going to write the lateness down, in my own hand, under my own entry, and I am going to leave it there. A holding that is owed an answer and does not send it is a holding that will be sent for, and a holding that is sent for is a holding somebody else is holding. I would rather have four days of lateness in a book than a silence in one."

Dunmar Fitch then did the thing he had been avoiding for four days, which was to lay the two letters side by side and say the sentence that made this week into what it was.

"Two offices in four days have asked two different people the same question about a bean," he said, "and one of the offices is nine hundred miles away and has never seen this holding, and the other is a firm that buys seed. Neither of them got it from the district. One of them got it from a printed doubt and one of them got it from a seed steward who would not be named. The district did not tell either of them anything. The district only made a book, and the book was already open before the district knew there was a book."

"That is not an accusation against the district," said Anneke Roos.

"It is not," said Dunmar Fitch. "It is a record of a sequence, and the sequence is in my hand and in the hour, and in four years somebody is going to want the sequence and not the feeling."

Lissa Vale took the letter to the table and read it twice, slowly. The press stood open behind her with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved, fifty-nine days at risk on the eighteenth, and nobody had moved it and nobody was going to. The bed stood at three and a half degrees. The leak stood at five-eighths. The lamp burned above them, fed that morning, warm in the cold room.

She set the trust's letter beside the buyer's letter and the printed sheet, three papers in a row, and looked at all three for a long time before she picked up the pen. Dunmar Fitch waited with his book open. Tova Reed waited with her hands folded. Marek waited at the end of the table. Nobody hurried her, because the four lines she was about to write were the only answer the holding had to give, and a hurried true answer was still true but carried less weight in a cold room.

She wrote the answer in four lines, in her own hand, on seed-house paper, and she read it back twice before Dunmar Fitch initialled it as witnessed, with the hour. Her voice did not shake. It had not shaken on the fourth of last month when she refused the question before it could be asked, and it did not shake now.

"The seed house does not know," she read. "It has never known. It copied its own name off a Crown list in a year it does not name. That is the whole of what it knows."

Four lines. The truth. Nobody in that room asked her to lengthen it, and nobody asked her which year, because the year was not named and a book that names a year it does not know is a different book, and nobody asked her for the list, because the list was the Crown's and the Crown had printed four hundred copies with the oat in the wrong column once already.

Tova Reed folded the answer and put it with the trust's post for the morning, with her own eighth line beside it and the four days of lateness entered above it. The four lines stood on seed-house paper in Lissa Vale's hand, entered against the ledger and not in it, with the hour: the seed house did not know, had never known, had copied its name off a Crown list in a year it does not name. Dunmar Fitch squared the sheet to the edge of the table, the way a licensed engineer had squared nine pages once, and left it in the middle where anybody might look.

"The woman who asked the question is a buyer and she is owed an answer by the fourteenth, and the fourteenth was four days gone before we wrote one word of it. We are four days past on a notice we were never served with, and that is the second time in four days that this holding has found out what a date costs by not owning it. It goes on the Thursday post on the twenty-second, and it is four lines long, and it is late. I would rather send a late true answer than an early one I have not got, and I would rather have the lateness on the page than in a clerk's memory in Cray."
