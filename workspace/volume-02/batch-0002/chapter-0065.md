# Chapter 65

## The Seed Buyer Writes Back

The Ashcombe sheet went to press on the sixteenth without anybody in the fen knowing it, and it reached the fen on the seventeenth in the same post that brought the trust's letter.

It was a single sheet, printed to go inside the winter issue, and Tova Reed held it in both hands for a long time before she read it aloud. It said, about one variety, that the source was disputed and that the printed name was not established. It did not name a holder. It did not name a district. It named the oat, and it named the doubt, and that was enough. The print was clean. The doubt stood in the same type as the names.

A seed house two hundred miles from here read it and did not buy that line. The letter saying so came on the same post, addressed to the counting house in Cray, with a copy to the fen because the annex's fourth term required a copy of everything to go to Cray whether or not it helped. The buyer wrote plainly, the way buyers write when they are doing arithmetic and not making a point: with the source doubtful, his house could not buy. He was sorry. He would look again when the name was established.

The line was the March's. The March's holding was the trust's. The trust was a named party to a charter condition, with the name Salt March Dryfoot Trust, of Cray, on the fifth sheet. Tova Reed's decision on the twenty-eighth to refuse a shilling a line had cost the March a sale. There was no other way to put it, and she did not try to find one.

She entered that on the seventeenth, in her own hand, in the trust's own book and in the fen's, with the hour on it, in daylight, at the middle of the seed-house table, with the fieldbook and the use log beside her and the press open behind her. Her hand did not shake. Dunmar Fitch held the book steady for her while she wrote, the way he held it for everyone, and when she had finished he read the entry back twice, because it was the first line in the volume that said she was wrong about something and not about a number.

The morning of the sixteenth had been spent in ordinary work, because the sheet went to press whether or not the fen watched it. Marek walked the nineteen acres with Ilsa Kern and found the Longneck standing, leaning eleven degrees along its whole length, the way Gael Senn had entered it on the twenty-second of a month. The graft's four willow sets held green. The pump ran. The leak passed into the housing at five-eighths. The pit held four feet without rising. None of it was news. All of it went in the book, with the hour, read back before it was initialled, because the vote of the nineteenth had not yet been taken and the habit was already there.

At midday the post cart brought the sheet and the buyer's letter together, and Tova Reed opened both at the table without getting up, the way she opened everything, with the knife and the ruler squared to the edge.

"The seventh line against the trust's interest becomes the eighth," she read back, standing, while Dunmar Fitch held the pen ready. "And it is the first one that says I was wrong about something and not about a number. I refused the index to keep the lines unowned. The index has answered by marking one line doubtful. A buyer doing arithmetic will not buy a doubtful line. That is not an attack. It is a buyer doing arithmetic, and I was wrong about the cost. The March has lost a sale it would have had if I had taken the shilling."

The yard wanted to call it an attack. The man of about fifty said so first, loudly, in the yard afterward, with four households round him. A printer who marks a line doubtful after a refusal, he said, was punishing a refusal. Ilsa Kern said it second, more quietly, at the well house, with a bar in her hand. Marek listened to both of them and said nothing until the evening, and then he said it at the table, with the hour on it.

Dunmar Fitch would not let the word stand.

"It is not an attack," he said, and he made Marek put the proposal and the refusal on the page together, with Marek's name against the proposal. "A buyer who reads that a name is not established and does not buy is doing what our own reader did on the fourth of this month when she said she could not tell whether three names were wrong. We do not get to call it an attack because it hurts the trust. It hurts the trust. That is all it does. A district that prints a category cannot be caught at anything, because the category is true. A printer who prints a doubt cannot be caught at anything, because the doubt is true. The oat is doubtful. We have said so ourselves for eleven years."

He read the sheet aloud afterward in the yard, to the five households who came, standing on the well house step the way Marek had read the fifth sheet on the twenty-third. He read the doubt first and the buyer's letter second, and then Tova Reed's eighth line third, and he did not hurry any of the three. The man of about seventy listened with his hand flat on the well house door, the way he had listened to the expiry sentence, and asked for his initial on nothing, because there was nothing to initial. A feeling about a doubt was worth nothing and it cost the holding nothing, and it was going to cost a great deal in a fortnight if nobody wrote down what was actually on the paper, and the paper was the only thing in that room that was a fact about the future.

In the evening Marek sat with Tova Reed at the counting-house end of the table and went through the trust's arithmetic for the lost sale, crate by crate, the way they had gone through the levy arithmetic on the twenty-fourth. The sale would have been forty-five bags at the trust's price, kept by the March, carried north at the buyer's cost. Lost, it was nothing. The eighth line stood against it. Tova wrote the number of bags in pencil beside the entry and then struck it out, because a number of bags that were not sold was not a fact about a book, and Dunmar Fitch made her initial the striking, which she did.

On the same day the trust wrote to the fen. The letter came on the Sunday post, short, in a clerk's hand from Cray, nine hundred miles by the March road, and it asked the seed house to say, in writing, what it knew about the Ashcombe oat. Not what the trust knew. What the seed house knew, that held the ledger the index wanted.

Lissa Vale took the letter to the table and read it twice, slowly. The press stood open behind her with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved, fifty-eight days at risk on the seventeenth if the count ran from the nineteenth of a month, and nobody had moved it and nobody was going to. The bed stood at three and a half degrees. The leak stood at five-eighths. The lamp burned above them, fed that morning, warm in the cold room.

She set the trust's letter beside the buyer's letter and the printed sheet, three papers in a row, and looked at all three for a long time before she picked up the pen. Dunmar Fitch waited with his book open. Tova Reed waited with her hands folded. Marek waited at the end of the table. Nobody hurried her, because the four lines she was about to write were the only answer the holding had to give, and a hurried true answer was still true but carried less weight in a cold room.

She wrote the answer in four lines, in her own hand, on seed-house paper, and she read it back twice before Dunmar Fitch initialled it as witnessed, with the hour. Her voice did not shake. It had not shaken on the fourth of a month when she refused the question before it could be asked, and it did not shake now.

She wrote the answer in four lines, in her own hand, on seed-house paper, and she read it back twice before Dunmar Fitch initialled it as witnessed, with the hour.

"The seed house does not know," she read. "It has never known. It copied its own name off a Crown list in a year it does not name. That is the whole of what it knows."

Four lines. The truth. Nobody in that room asked her to lengthen it, and nobody asked her which year, because the year was not named and a book that names a year it does not know is a different book, and nobody asked her for the list, because the list was the Crown's and the Crown had printed four hundred copies with the oat in the wrong column once already.

Tova Reed folded the answer and put it with the trust's post for the morning, with her own eighth line beside it. The four lines stood on seed-house paper in Lissa Vale's hand, entered against the ledger and not in it, with the hour: the seed house did not know, had never known, had copied its name off a Crown list in a year it did not name. Dunmar Fitch squared the sheet to the edge of the table, the way a licensed engineer had squared nine pages once, and left it in the middle where anybody might look.

"The woman who asked the question is a buyer," she said, "and she is owed an answer by the fourteenth. Ten days from the fourth to the fourteenth, eight from the sixth, six from the eighth, four from the tenth. We will send it on the Thursday post and it will be there in time. A buyer doing arithmetic will read four lines and decide. That is what the book is for. Not to convince. To answer."
