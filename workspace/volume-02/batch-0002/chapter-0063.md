# Chapter 63

## Kellan Rusk

He came up the fen road on the eleventh with a book under his arm and a paper in his coat, and he came alone.

The morning was cold in the way the fen is cold before rain, with a low mist on the drains and the willow showing black against it. Marek saw him from the seed-house door at about the eighth hour, walking steady with his shoulders up, and he knew the walk before he knew the man. Kellan Rusk was forty-three, a well contractor under a monitored charter, employed and testifying, with a pump house at Fennmere and a crew that had dressed a wound once a year for four years for nothing. He had a right to the district's monthly return and a condition that said the record was open, and he had the return with him, folded, with the district's mark on it.

He set it on the middle of the seed-house table, beside the fieldbook and the use log, and he did not sit down until Lissa Vale told him to.

"Sit," she said. "You have walked eleven miles. You will drink something before you talk."

"I will talk first," said Kellan. "Then I will drink."

Dunmar Fitch opened the cooperative's book to a clean page and set his pen beside it. Gael Senn came in from the well house with her hands still damp from the morning's reading and stood at the end of the table. Tova Reed sat at the counting-house end with her own book shut in front of her. The press stood open behind them with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved, fifty-two days at risk on the eleventh.

"I have three figures," said Kellan. "Nobody in this yard has put them in one place. They are correct. I want them in your book before I say anything else about them, with the hour on them, because a figure that is not in a book is a rumor, and I have had enough of rumors."

Dunmar Fitch nodded and wrote the hour. Marek nodded. Kellan read the figures out, one after another, in a flat voice, like a man reading a gauge.

The first was his own side draw, from his own log, kept at his pump house and open to any member of the cooperative at any hour without notice: never above three hundred in a day, for six years. He gave the highest week and the lowest, and the page numbers where they stood, and Dunmar Fitch wrote the page numbers down.

The second was the head as the log claimed it: eleven hundred a day to Lantern Reach ground. He did not soften it. He read it the way the log had it, as a claim, and he said whose claim it was.

The third was the ground as the ground took it: seven hundred and twenty. That was the cooperative's own figure, measured on the twentieth of a month under a licensed eye, entered before the reading, and Kellan read it back from their own page to show he had read it.

"Put them together," said Kellan. "Eleven hundred claimed. Seven hundred and twenty taken. Three hundred drawn at my house. Eighty a day unaccounted for. Eighty a day for six years. That is the arithmetic. I have done it in my own book and I have done it in yours."

Nobody in that room liked how he said it. Nobody liked him less afterward for having said it, and everybody liked him less because of how he said it. He said it the way a man says a thing he has carried for a season and is tired of carrying alone, with a edge in it that made it sound like an accusation when it was meant as an account.

Anneke Roos looked at him steadily. "Say it again without the edge," she said, "and we will hear it better."

Kellan flushed. "I do not have another way of saying it," he said. "I have said it to my own men for six years and they hear the edge too. It is how I sound when I am right and nobody wants it."

"The three together," he went on, "with your standing pressure log and your seed annex and your use log, are enough to prove this holding's own draw against itself. That is the last thing any of you wants. It is also true. I am saying it to help, and I know what help costs in this yard."

Tova Reed looked at the three figures on the page and did not touch them. She had her pencil in her hand and she set it down carefully, the way she set things down when she did not trust herself with them.

"They are correct," she said at last. "I wish they were not. Eighty a day is one hundred and seventy-five thousand two hundred over six years. That is water this holding cannot account for and water the low quarter did not get. It sits in our book now beside our own shortfall of three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight a month. A later officer will put those two numbers on the same page."

"Yes," said Kellan. "He will. That is why I brought them here first. A figure travels and does not come back. I would rather it travel out of your book than out of mine behind your back."

"They are correct," said Kellan again, as though to make sure the room had heard it. "My monitored charter says I may not give a figure to a third party without the return, and I have the return, and I bring it. Here it is. Read it."

Dunmar Fitch read the return, front and back, with the district's mark on it, and entered that he had read it, with the hour, and read the entry back. The return was a monthly paper, plain, with the draw against each week and the clerk's initial at the foot. It matched the first figure. There was no fault in it.

"What do you want," said Marek.

Kellan put his hand flat on the table. His hand was scarred at the knuckles from years of iron work, and it shook a little, and he did not hide the shake.

"What I want is not money and it is not a reduction," he said. "I want to be asked at the next examination, in front of a district officer, to say the thing I said on a bench in the mud, with the figure in my hand. The name in the box. The office and not the man. I said it once in front of an inspector and a licensed woman heard it and said nothing. I want to say it again where it can be minuted. I was charter holder of record and I stood at the back for two hours and said two sentences nobody asked me for. I am asking to be asked this time."

Dunmar Fitch wrote that down as asked for, the way it had been minuted as asked for in the volume before, and he read it back, word for word.

"The yard does not agree to it in the room," said Anneke Roos, before anybody could agree to it. "It is asked for and it is minuted. That is all it is today. A man who is asked to speak in front of an officer has to be sent for properly, and this body does not send for anybody in an afternoon. We will take the figures. We will not take the man today."

Kellan nodded as though he had expected that, and some of the edge went out of him.

"Then the figures stand," he said. "They are in your book. You are now holding your own conviction, and nobody has yet decided what to do with it. That is the correct state of affairs. I would not have it otherwise. I have carried them long enough to know what they do to a room."

Lissa Vale poured him a cup of something hot and set it in front of him, and he drank it standing up, the way men drink in yards when they do not intend to stay. But he stayed. He stayed until the thirteenth, coming and going, bringing the return again when it was asked for, answering what was asked and nothing else. On the twelfth Gael Senn checked the figures against the standing pressure log, line by line, with two rules on the page, and found no fault in them, and entered that she had found no fault, with the hour, and read it back. On the morning of the twelfth Marek took him to the well house to show him the six o'clock corner, five-eighths, into the housing, pit holding four feet, and Kellan looked at it for a long time without touching anything, the way a contractor looks at another man's leak.

"You cannot mend that under load," he said at last. "The only way is to stop the pump. Stopping the pump stops the bed. I know it. You know it. It is in your seventh note and it will be in your ninth. Do not let anybody tell you later you did not know it in time."

On the thirteenth Lissa Vale read the use log lines that touched the head, out loud, at the table, all four of them with the lateness declared on the first, so that the three figures sat beside three lines of record and nobody could say afterward they had not been in the same room. The leak stood at five-eighths of an inch on the page, twice measured, at the seventh hour, pump on. The pulse stood where it had stood since the first, cause not established, nothing under it. The shortfall stood at three hundred and ninety-nine.

Marek walked Kellan to the gate on the evening of the thirteenth. The light was going and the drains were silver. Neither of them spoke for a while.

"You said it to help," said Marek at last.

"I said it because it is true," said Kellan. "Help is what you call it afterward if anybody calls it anything."

At the gate Kellan said the line that was the chapter, and Dunmar Fitch entered it verbatim that night, with the hour, because it was the whole of the movement's argument. Marek stood with him in the cold, with the mist coming up off the drains, and neither of them hurried the moment, because both of them knew what it cost to put everything true in a book anybody could read.

"You have built a book that anybody can read and you have put everything in it that is true about you, and a book like that is not evidence. It is an invitation."
