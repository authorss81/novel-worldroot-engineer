# Chapter 63

## Kellan Rusk

He came up the fen road on the twelfth with a book under his arm and a paper in his coat, and he came alone.

The morning was cold in the way the fen is cold before rain, with a low mist on the drains. Marek saw him from the seed-house door at about the eighth hour, walking steady with his shoulders up, and he knew the walk before he knew the man. Kellan Rusk was forty-three, a well contractor holding a monitored license on the Tarrow water record, which is a different roll from the rootwright roll and holds one name where that one holds three, employed and testifying, under a monitored charter with a gauge below the split that any party might read, an open log, a monthly return and a person who might be sent. He had a pump house at Fennmere and a crew that had dressed a wound once a year for four years for nothing. He had a right to the district's monthly return and a condition that said the record was open, and he had the return with him, folded, with the district's mark on it.

He set it on the middle of the seed-house table, beside the fieldbook and the use log and the Ashcombe index that had been standing open there for four days, and he did not sit down until Lissa Vale told him to.

"Sit," she said. "You have walked eleven miles. You will drink something before you talk."

"I will talk first," said Kellan. "Then I will drink."

Dunmar Fitch opened the cooperative's book to a clean page and set his pen beside it. Gael Senn came in from the well house with damp hands and stood at the end of the table. Tova Reed sat at the counting-house end with her own book shut in front of her. The press stood open behind them with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved, fifty-three days at risk on the twelfth.

"I have three figures," said Kellan. "Nobody in this yard has put them in one place. They are correct. I want them in your book before I say anything else about them, with the hour on them, because a figure that is not in a book is a rumor, and I have had enough of rumors."

Dunmar Fitch nodded and wrote the hour. Marek nodded. Kellan read the figures out, one after another, in a flat voice, like a man reading a gauge.

The first was his own side draw, from his own log, kept at his pump house and open to any member of the cooperative at any hour without notice: never above three hundred in a day, for six years. He gave the highest week and the lowest and the page numbers where they stood, and Dunmar Fitch wrote the page numbers down.

The second was the head as the log claimed it: eleven hundred a day to Lantern Reach ground. He did not soften it. He read it the way the log had it, as a claim, and he said whose claim it was.

The third was the ground as the ground took it: seven hundred and twenty. That was the cooperative's own figure, measured on the twentieth of last month, under a licensed eye, entered before the reading, and Kellan read it back from their own page to show he had read it.

"Put them together," said Kellan. "Eleven hundred claimed. Seven hundred and twenty taken. Three hundred drawn at my house. Eighty a day unaccounted for. Eighty a day for six years. That is the arithmetic. I have done it in my own book and I have done it in yours."

Nobody in that room liked how he said it. Nobody liked him less afterward for having said it, and everybody liked him less because of how he said it. He said it the way a man says a thing he has carried for a season and is tired of carrying alone, with an edge in it that made it sound like an accusation when it was meant as an account.

Anneke Roos looked at him steadily. "Say it again without the edge," she said, "and we will hear it better."

Kellan flushed. "I do not have another way of saying it," he said. "I have said it to my own men for six years and they hear the edge too. It is how I sound when I am right and nobody wants it."

"The three together," he went on, "with your standing pressure log and your seed annex and your use log, are enough to prove this holding's own draw against itself. That is the last thing any of you wants. It is also true. I am saying it to help, and I know what help costs in this yard."

Tova Reed looked at the three figures on the page and did not touch them. She had her pencil in her hand and she set it down carefully, the way she set things down when she did not trust herself with them.

"They are correct," she said at last. "I wish they were not. Eighty a day is one hundred and seventy-five thousand two hundred over six years. That is water this holding cannot account for and water the low quarter did not get. It sits in our book now beside our own shortfall of three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight a month. A later officer will put those two numbers on one page, be right to, and not know which of them he should be looking at."

"Yes," said Kellan. "He will. That is why I brought them here first. A figure travels and does not come back. I would rather it travel out of your book than out of mine behind your back."

"They are correct," Kellan said again, as though to make sure the room had heard it. "My monitored charter says I may not give a figure to a third party without the return. Here it is. Read it."

Dunmar Fitch read the return, front and back, with the district's mark on it, and entered that he had read it, with the hour, and read the entry back. The return was a monthly paper, plain, with the draw against each week and the clerk's initial at the foot. It matched the first figure. There was no fault in it, Dunmar said in those words, and added that a thing with no fault in it could be got at by anybody who asked to see it.

"What do you want," said Marek.

Kellan put both hands on the table and left them there. The knuckles were scarred from years of iron work, and the left one shook a little, and he did not hide the shake.

"What I want is not money and not a reduction," he said. "I want to be asked at the next examination, in front of a district officer, to say the thing I said on a bench in the mud, with the figure in my hand. The name in the box is an office and not a man. Schedule clerk, fen drains. The post has existed longer than I have. The man in it has changed four times that I know of. He does not come out. He signs the paper. The paper goes to a store. The store gives out the kit. I said two sentences nobody asked me for, in front of an inspector, on a bench, with a licensed woman on my left who heard them and said nothing, and I have not been able to put it anywhere since. I am asking to be asked this time."

Dunmar Fitch wrote that down as asked for, which is the form for a thing this body will not agree to in a room, and he read it back, word for word, and nobody was permitted to alter a word of it.

"The yard does not agree to it in the room," said Anneke Roos, before anybody could agree to it. "It is asked for and it is minuted. That is all it is today. A man who is asked to speak before an officer has to be sent for properly, and this body does not send for anybody in an afternoon. We will take the figures. Not the man today."

Kellan nodded as though he had expected that, and some of the edge went out of him.

"Then the figures stand," he said. "They are in your book. You are holding your own conviction, and nobody has yet decided what to do with it. That is the correct state of affairs. I would not have it otherwise. I have carried them long enough to know what they do to a room."

---

Marek did the thing he had been not doing for four days, because a licensed man was standing in the yard with a condition that said the record was open, and because a man who has just handed you a figure ought to be shown what it is made of.

They went down to the well house together at the eleventh hour with Gael Senn and Lissa Vale, and Lissa Vale wrote the use-log line first, on the page, and read it back: instrument laid on the head of this node at the eleventh hour of the twelfth, licensed man present, the man being Kellan Rusk of this district, a well contractor on the water record and not on the rootwright roll, the contact tested before the contact and the test written down, and Marek read it back after her and initialled it. The instrument came up out of the case Lissa Vale had carried down that morning, and it had been on Marek's mother's table since the twenty-first of last month, and it is a thing about four inches long, it is not a book, it has never read a page, and it was not going to start.

Kellan tested the contact with a plain wire gauge, because that is what the test is for, and wrote the reading of the test down before he let the instrument go near anything.

Then Marek laid it on the head.

It took eleven seconds. He could not account for the eleven seconds any better than he could account for the four on a cut face on the seventeenth of last month, and when it was over the four inches on the inside of his forearm were hot, and stayed hot, and his hand from the wrist down was no use to him for the rest of that day and most of the next, and he let Kellan hold the case down the well house steps because he could not get the latch with two fingers.

It said three things. It said them the way it had said three things on the thirtieth, off the head of the node in the time it takes to say them, and not in a sentence anybody would want.

> This is the mouth.
>
> There is more than one source and it will not say which.
>
> A node does not keep a list.

Kellan Rusk read them twice, and then sat down on the step of the well house, which was not a thing a man of forty-three does in front of witnesses, and said: "Then I have been measuring a mouth and calling it a well."

"You have been measuring a mouth and calling it a well," said Marek, "and you were right to, and so was the district, and so were we. A gauge below the split reads the mouth. Everything I have ever put in a log about water is a thing I measured about the mouth."

Lissa Vale wrote nothing, and Marek asked her why, and she gave him the reason in one sentence, and it was the same reason Dunmar Fitch had given on the third of last month about a different set of lines: *there is nothing in it that a man with a rule can check, and a line in a book that cannot be checked is a line somebody can use.* The use-log line said only that the instrument had been laid on the head at the eleventh hour with a licensed man present. It did not say what came out, and it was never going to, and both of them knew that in advance.

The reading went in the book as a contact and not as a finding, with the hour, and the cost of it went in as a private matter between a man and his own arm, and Marek went home with his sleeve down over four inches nobody in that yard was going to write about.

On the thirteenth Gael Senn checked the three figures against the standing pressure log, line by line, with two rules on the page, found no fault in them, entered that she had found no fault with the hour, and read it back. Marek took Kellan out to the outer field and showed him the nineteen acres, the drill with the Longneck leaning eleven degrees, the low corner, and the four willow sets at the head of the new run. Kellan looked at the six o'clock corner for a long time without touching anything, the way a contractor looks at another man's leak.

"You cannot mend that under load," he said at last. "The only way is to stop the pump. Stopping the pump stops the bed. I know it. You know it. It is in your seventh note, on the twenty-third, in your own hand, and the sentence is the reason that note is there. Do not let anybody tell you later that you did not know it in time."

Lissa Vale read the use log out loud, at the table, on the thirteenth, all six lines of it, with the lateness declared on the first, so that the three figures sat beside six lines of record and nobody could say afterward they had not been in the same room. The two last were short. The thirtieth of last month was a bead, a clip, a license and a witness; the twelfth was a contact, the hour, and the name of the man, and nothing else at all. The leak stood at five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, at the seventh hour, pump on. The pulse stood where it had stood since the first, cause not established, nothing under it. The shortfall stood at three hundred and ninety-nine.

On the fourteenth the fourteen days of the district's notice ran out.

Dunmar Fitch entered it in his own book in the morning with the hour, because a clock that nobody enters is a clock that has not run: the water-supply bylaw, second part, form C/4, served on the Salt March Dryfoot Trust at Cray on the twenty-seventh, fourteen days of the date of service, this holding's own computation of the last day of the fourteen being the fourteenth on an assumed date, entered as a computation and not as a fact, and no answer received by this holding and none receivable by it, because this holding is not the addressee, is not entitled to a copy of the notice, and cannot learn today whether the notice has been answered or not.

"Nothing in this yard knows," said Tova Reed, flatly, with the pencil down, which is how she said the things she had checked twice. "We know that on the twenty-seventh a form left here for Cray. We do not know when it arrived, or what day they count the fourteen from, and there is no copy of the notice in this building and we are not entitled to one, and I am not going to write to a counting house to ask, because the moment this holding writes about a notice that was not served on it is the moment this holding is a party to a notice it was never given."

"So it is the fourteenth," said Dunmar Fitch, "and we do not know whether that is bad news or a post going down a road."

"It is neither yet," said Tova Reed. "It is a date. Put the date down and let it be a date until somebody tells us it is something else."

Marek walked Kellan to the gate on the evening of the fourteenth. The light was going and the drains were silver and the pump had stopped for the night and started again at about the ninth hour the way it always did. Neither of them spoke for a while.

"You said it to help," said Marek at last.

"I said it because it is true," said Kellan. "Help is what you call it afterward if anybody calls it anything."

"You cannot get it back," said Marek. "You know that. It is in the book in a hand you cannot take out."

"I have known that since the twenty-first of last month," said Kellan, "and I have had three days to think about the rest of it, and here is the rest of it. I am not asking you to bury it. I am asking you to hold it, because a figure in one book is a figure in one household and a figure in two books is a fact about a district. And I am asking to be asked, so that when it is held it is held in a room with a district officer in it and not in a seed house in a fen. You are the only body in this district that keeps a book anybody may read. If I give the figure to anybody else, it goes into a drawer. If I give it to you, it goes into a table."

At the gate Kellan Rusk said the line that this holding will still be quoting in a year, and Dunmar Fitch entered it verbatim that night, with the hour, because a sentence that good does not get paraphrased by a clerk in a cold room. Marek stood with him in the cold with the mist coming up off the drains, and neither of them hurried the moment, because both of them knew what it cost to put everything true in a book anybody could read.

"You have built a book that anybody can read and you have put everything in it that is true about you, and a book like that is not evidence. It is an invitation."
