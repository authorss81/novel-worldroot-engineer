# Chapter 64

## The Order Of The Work

Coll Renner came back on the fourteenth with the form he had described in advance, and he came in daylight with one man and no box of tools.

The yard had been waiting for him without saying it was waiting. Marek had checked the north annex at first light, where the flowerpot of rye stood in the ground against the drill with the sack weighted by a stone, green and holding. The mark on his forearm was four inches, branching twice, permanent, the color of a healed burn. Lissa Vale had looked at it that morning without touching it and nodded once, which was all she ever did about it.

Renner laid the form on the middle of the seed-house table, beside the fieldbook and the use log, and he stepped back from it the way a man steps back from a tool he has made.

It was one page, printed, with a number in the corner that nobody in this district had seen before: form RW/3. At the top was a box for the licensed engineer, with his name and his district already filled in, in his own hand. Under it was a box for the four conditions — anchor, path, return, and witness — with the words printed and the lines empty. At the foot was a line for the order of the work, and the line was blank, and it was the only line on the sheet anybody wanted.

"It is not a contract," he said. "I said I would be back in a month with a form nobody here has seen, and this is it. A schedule of works. One page. The boxes are mine. The line at the foot is yours if you want it. I would like it in your hand. My foreman could carry it on a Tuesday to a holding he has never seen and do the work in the order it says."

Marek looked at the blank line for a long time. The room was full — Dunmar Fitch with his book, Anneke Roos with her coat still on from the low acre, Gael Senn with the morning's log page, Tova Reed at the counting-house end, Lissa Vale at the press with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved, fifty-five days at risk on the fourteenth. His hand went to the line and did not fill it in.

"No," he said.

"Give me the reason," said Renner. His voice was level. "Plainly. I have earned a plain reason. I have read your books nine times and I have put my own name on your mark."

"The reason is the one I gave you at the gate on the thirtieth," said Marek. "A list of things that have been done is a list of things anybody can do, and eleven fen holdings cannot afford the compost. Your form wants the order of the work so a crew that has never seen a holding can do it on a Tuesday. The work here is a graft fed about a twentieth of what it was designed to be fed, with a shortfall of three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight a month at four shillings, against a box that is empty. The bed stands at three and a half degrees. The leak stands at five-eighths. Write the order down and somebody will follow it and starve a field correctly. They will do it in the right order and it will still fail for want of a twentieth. I will not."

Renner nodded slowly. He did not argue. He took the form back and folded it once and put it in his coat. His man stood at the door with his hands behind his back and said nothing.

The morning had started early, with the pump running and the yard already at work. Ilsa Kern had been on the embankment since first light with a bar, checking the toe where the nine feet had been made fourteen, and Jonas Byre had counted minutes at the culvert while two levy men hauled weed. Gael Senn had taken the morning's line at the seventh hour — five-eighths, twice measured, pump on — and read it back before initialling, with the man of about fifty listening. The ordinary work did not stop because a form had arrived. That was the point of the ordinary work.

"Show me the boxes," said Dunmar Fitch, reaching for the form before Renner put it away. "I want to see what a schedule of works asks for, even if we do not fill it."

Renner unfolded it again and laid it flat. The box for the licensed engineer. The box for the four conditions, anchor and path and return and witness, printed plain. The blank line at the foot. Dunmar studied all three with a clerk's eye, the way he had studied the extract's caption on the twenty-sixth until the sixth word showed itself.

"It is a good form," he said at last. "One page. Four conditions. One line for the order. Whoever drew it knew what a foreman needs. That does not mean we fill it. A good form refused is still refused, and the refusal is still about the compost."

"Then what comes back instead," said Tova Reed from the other end of the table, "is what you brought unasked. Put it on the table. I saw the corner of it in your pocket."

Renner looked at her, and then he did. It was a price list and a route, written in his own hand on two sheets of yard paper, with figures against every line and a map of farms along the fen road where his men could work in a season. Day work at so much a day. A yard at so much a month. A copper collar at so much each. A crew of four for a season along the farms named, with the return to be found by the holder.

It was the first material anybody had put on that table in a season. Nobody said so, but everybody thought it. Dunmar Fitch set his pen down and looked at the figures for a long time.

"Read it aloud," said Tova Reed.

Renner read it. Every figure, every farm, every week. The figures were fair. They were lower than any Crown figure anybody had seen. They were worked out honestly, with the compost priced at four shillings and the shortfall shown, not hidden. They were still more than sixteen households could raise in a year. When he had finished his voice was hoarse.

When he had finished Tova Reed refused it.

"We refuse," she said. "And the refusal is about the compost and not about the man. A route that needs four hundred hundredweight a month at four shillings is not a route for this district, whatever the day rate says. Nine men cannot make compost out of a price list. Put that in the book, with the hour, and read it back."

Dunmar Fitch put it in, with the hour, and read it back, and Renner listened to the refusal the way he had listened to the method's refusal on the thirtieth — standing still, with his hands at his sides. His man shifted at the door and was still.

Afterward Lissa Vale brought out bread and cheese, because it was past midday and men who had read figures aloud were hungry whether or not they admitted it. They ate at the table with the price list between them and the form RW/3 folded in Renner's coat, and for a while nobody talked about work. Renner asked about the Longneck drill, eleven trays, leaning eleven degrees, and Marek told him it stood. He asked about the tin under the ledger, and Lissa Vale said it stood. He asked about the box, and Dunmar Fitch said it was empty, counted twice, the first levy to a fee and the second to compost on the fourteenth and the third not moved. Renner nodded at each answer as though checking items on a list only he could see.

"You keep a book I can read," he said at last, wiping his hands. "That is why I came back. Most holdings keep a book I cannot read and then wonder why no contractor will touch them. Yours I can read. It says you cannot pay. I believe it. That is worth more than a method."

"You are not beaten," said Marek quietly, after the entry was initialled. "A contractor who is refused a method and leaves a route on the table is not a man who was beaten. Refusing an offer is not the same as having none. We have none. That is the truth of it."

On the fifteenth Renner walked the yard with Marek and looked at the graft and at the pump and at the six o'clock corner where the leak passed into the housing, and he asked three professional questions about the head and Marek answered all three. At the gate, in the afternoon, with the light going flat over the drains, he said the last thing.

"The form will be back in a season with the line at the foot filled in by somebody else," he said. "That is what I am paid for. Somebody holds a license in this district and somebody wants a method, and if it is not yours it will be somebody's. I do not know whether that is a threat or a market, and I would like it entered that I said so."

Dunmar Fitch entered it that evening, with the hour, and read it back. The entry was short: form RW/3 received on the fourteenth, line at the foot blank, method refused for want of compost, price list and route received unasked and refused for the same reason, form to return in a season with the line filled by another hand. Anneke Roos initialled it as an opinion. Gael Senn read it back second, the way the vote said a person must.

After Renner had gone Marek stood at the gate for a long time, looking down the fen road toward Fennmere where nine men worked in a clean yard with clips on a board. He thought about the two hundred a year and the day work and the yard, and how the offer had removed every objection except the true one. A method under another man's license, liability in another man's name, no use of Marek's name at all. Eleven holdings that could not afford a cooperative. Compost at four shillings. A twentieth fed. The arithmetic did not care whose license stood on the page.

Tova Reed came out and stood beside him, with her case locked and the key on her belt.

"He will be back," she said. "With the line filled by somebody else. That is what he is paid for."

"Yes," said Marek. "And we will refuse it again, or we will not, depending on the compost. That is what we are paid for. Nothing."
