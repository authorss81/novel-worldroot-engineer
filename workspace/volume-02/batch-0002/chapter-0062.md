# Chapter 62

## Nobody Is Going To Walk It

Corvin Slee came to the gate on the tenth and would not come past the posts.

It was a Saturday and the yard was doing the Saturday work, and he had walked four miles to stand in the same place he had stood on the twenty-fifth, two months back. Marek went out to him because Slee would not come in, and Gael Senn came with him because she had timed his pail four times once and did not trust a number one man had timed.

"The district has written to us," said Slee. He had a folded paper in his hand and he did not open it. "For the survey. The line of the standing iron. Four men of ours chalked it on every gatepost and doorstep on the nineteenth and the twentieth, at our own hand and at our own cost. It is on paper in Fennmere now. In nobody's hands."

Marek nodded. The low quarter's own survey was the only survey of that ground anybody had ever had, made with a stick and a bucket of lime and a knotted cord, sent up the fen road on request and uncharged. It had been on the seed-house table on the twenty-ninth beside the Crown map. It was not there now. It was in Fennmere, on paper, and the district had written for it because the district writes for everything.

"They want it from you too," said Slee. "They write to everybody."

Behind Slee, on the fen road, stood a district man with a satchel. He was not an officer. He was a clerk sent out to look at a thing and write down what he saw, and he had the manners of a man who knew he was not welcome and had come anyway.

"Mr Vale," he said. "The district would be glad of a walk. The line as your holding finds it. To set beside theirs."

Marek looked at Slee. Slee looked at the road. The report of the collie, made on the nineteenth two months back, was in three places, the open book, the certified copy in a Fennmere file and the copy going to the March road, and Gael Senn had decided on the nineteenth to write nothing about the animal, with her name on the decision and not the animal's, and nothing had been written underneath the report since. Nobody in that yard was going to interpret it. That was settled and it was not going to be unsetted by a district clerk with a satchel on a Saturday.

"I am asked to walk it," said Marek. It was not a question.

"You are asked," said the clerk.

Marek stood at the gateposts for a long time. He thought about the four wells that had gone off between the twenty-first and the twenty-fifth, nine feet of gravel over clay, the pail coming up in a count of ninety on the sixteenth and taking five minutes forty on the twenty-sixth. He thought about forty-one houses still drinking off a bed his branch had killed from the top. He thought about the only leverage the fen had in the district on that ground, which was that the low quarter had a map of its own and the fen did not. He thought about his own hands, and the mark on the inside of his forearm, four inches, branching twice, and how little a mark weighed against forty-one houses.

He thought about what Ilsa Kern had said on the walk back from the low quarter years ago in another season, that a man who goes to look at a thing becomes the man who is answerable for it. He had not understood her then. He understood her now, standing at two posts with a clerk's pen waiting.

"No," he said.

The clerk blinked. "May I enter a reason."

"Yes," said Marek. "A holding that is owed a survey cannot also be the body that goes and collects it, because the collecting is the part that makes it theirs. The line was chalked by four men at their own cost for their own use, with the words *by four of us, at our own cost, for our own use* on it. If I walk it, it becomes my walk. If I write it, it becomes my book. The low quarter keeps its own map, on its own terms, and this holding is not the body that holds it. I will not make their cost into my leverage."

Slee lifted his head at that. For the first time that morning he looked directly at Marek, and what was in his face was not forgiveness and not anger but something older, worn smooth by use.

"You damaged us," he said quietly. "You know it. I do not forgive it and I do not have to. But you are not taking the map either. That is something."

"It is the only thing I can do for you," said Marek. "It is not much."

"It is what it is," said Slee, and turned up the road.

The clerk waited until Slee was out of earshot and then took out his own book, and Marek understood that the rest was going in the district's record and not in theirs.

"It is your right to refuse," said the clerk, writing.

"It is not a right," said Marek. "It is a standing. I have no standing on that ground. That is the whole of it."

Then the clerk asked, because clerks ask, whether the holding would put anything in writing, and Marek said that it would put a refusal in writing and nothing else, and the clerk said that a refusal in writing was still a refusal in writing and could be read against the holding later by a man in an office who had never stood at a gatepost, and Marek said that was correct and that he wanted it entered anyway.

The rest of the tenth was spent in work that refused to wait for a decision, and in an argument that had been waiting three days. Ilsa Kern took two levy men to the south embankment to cut willow for winter binding, a hundred and six feet the year before and less this year, entered in the working notes with the hour. Jonas Byre mended the wire cradle at the well house with old eye-hooks from the tool chest, though no mender had been in the fen since the sixteenth, two months back, and none was expected. Gael Senn took the evening line at the rail, the leak steady, the pulse quiet, and wrote cause not established where cause was not established, and nothing under it, with her name on the decision.

The argument was in the seed house at midday, and it was the woman with the shilling who started it, and she started it well.

"Mr Vale," she said. "I have signed every paper you have put in front of me since the fourteenth. I put a shilling in and I wrote three words under it and I would put it in again. And I am going to say the thing that is in this room and I would like it said to my face. Forty-one houses are drinking off a bed your branch killed, and the only paper in the world about that ground was made by four of them with a stick and a bucket of lime, and the district has written for it and it is in a drawer in Fennmere. If you stood up there and walked the line yourself, with your own book and your own hour on it, you would have a second copy of the only thing that anybody has ever had about that ground, and you would have had it for nothing, and eleven of us would drink one day longer while a man in an office read both. Walk it."

The room did not move.

"I know what I am asking," she said. "I sat at this table and I voted with the rest of you to open the book to any stranger who comes. I wrote my initial against half of what has been in it this month. And I am asking you to put one hour of a man's work into forty-one houses' water, and if you say no I will not say another word about it, and I want it entered that I asked and that I will keep my half of it either way."

Marek stood with his hand flat on the table the way the man of about seventy had put his hand flat on the well house door when he heard the date of the expiry.

"No," he said. "And the reason is the same one I gave at the gate an hour ago, and I am not going to give you a better one because you have earned it. If this holding walks that line, the line stops being theirs. Everything I have entered against my own interest for a season has been entered on the ground that I would rather be proved wrong by a book than be proved right by a man. If I take their chalk and put it in my book, then in four years a man in a room opens two books and one of them is mine, and he does not know that the other one was made by four men who paid for it themselves, and he weighs mine, because mine is the engineer of record."

"That is a lawyer's answer," said the woman with the shilling.

"It is a bookkeeper's answer," said Marek, "and I would rather have it in that coat."

Ilsa Kern said nothing at all, which in that yard was a position, and later at the embankment she told Jonas Byre that the engineer had been asked for the one thing he could give that cost him nothing and had refused it, and Jonas Byre said that was the third time this year, and Ilsa said it was, and neither of them said what the third time was, and they went back to the willow.

At supper Marek sat with the low quarter's paper in his head and could not get rid of it. Four men with a stick and lime and a cord, on the nineteenth and the twentieth, at their own cost. The only survey of that ground anybody had ever had. Sent up the road on request and uncharged. Now on paper in Fennmere, in nobody's hands, written for by a district that wrote for everything. He understood, the way he had understood the caption's sixth word on the twenty-sixth, that a paper in nobody's hands was still a paper, and that the hands it was not in mattered more than the hands it was in.

And then Slee came back down the road at the seventh hour, alone, and put the folded paper into Marek's hand, and Marek understood before he had it open that it was a copy of the low quarter's own survey, made with a stick and a bucket of lime on the nineteenth and the twentieth, given to him because he had been asked for nothing and had refused everything and a man who refuses everything is a man a thing gets given to.

"No," Marek said.

Slee did not seem surprised. He had a way of standing that took up very little road.

"Then what are you protecting," he said.

"Their right to be the only ones who have it," said Marek. "I know how that sounds. If that sheet is in this building then in a year one of the sixteen has to keep it, and one of the sixteen is a person and not a post, and then it is his to lose. It is in Fennmere in a drawer in an office and an office is a better keeper than a person and I have got no argument against an office at all this season."

Slee put his hands in his pockets.

"You are a strange man," he said, "and I have come four miles twice today, and I am not going to do it again."

He went up the road. Marek did not open the paper. He stood in the gate with it in his hand for about a minute and then he carried it in and put it on the middle of the table beside the fieldbook and the use log, which is where things went, and told Dunmar Fitch what it was, and Dunmar Fitch entered it as received and unopened and put a line under it in his own book that said the sheet is not of this holding and is not in this holding's book, and read that line back, and Anneke Roos initialled it as an opinion.

"It costs us," said Dunmar Fitch afterward, at the table. "Let us write that too. The low quarter keeps its map. We keep our hands off it. That costs this holding the only leverage it has in the district on the one ground where forty-one houses are still drinking off a bed it killed. A later room will want to know whether we knew that at the time. We did."

He wrote it, and Anneke Roos initialled it, and it stood.

Underneath it, in the same hand, Dunmar Fitch wrote the ninth note under section nine of the charter schedule, dated the tenth, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled: asked on the tenth in front of the district's own man to walk another body's survey and refused for standing and not for right, a copy of that survey offered and not taken, the low quarter keeping its own map at its own cost and this holding keeping its hands off it, the report of the animal of the nineteenth standing in three places with nothing under it, and the cost entered that the fen loses its only leverage where forty-one houses drink off a bed it killed.

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*

On the eleventh, a Sunday, the district form arrived, as Slee had said it would. It was a single sheet, printed, with a box for the holding and a box for the ground and a line that asked this holding to produce its own map of the low quarter.

Dunmar Fitch read it twice and set it down beside the fieldbook and the use log, in the middle of the table. The form was plain, printed, with the district's mark on it, asking in ordinary language for a thing that sounded ordinary: this holding to produce its own map of the low quarter, with the ground and the line and the date.

"The answer has to be that it has never been asked to," he said, "and that it would not be a map if it were. We have never surveyed that ground. We have no stick and no lime and no cord on it. A paper we made by walking another body's chalk would be our paper about their cost, and no officer in a cold room could tell the difference afterward. So we answer that we have none, and that we will not make one, and the reason is standing and not right."

Anneke Roos initialled that as an opinion. Gael Senn read it back. Marek signed it as engineer of record, against the interest of the application, because the only leverage the fen had was gone and the book had to say so.

That evening Corvin Slee's folded paper came back to Marek's mind, unopened, sitting on the middle of this holding's table in a building that had refused it twice in one day. The district had written to the low quarter for the survey. The district had written to the fen for a map. Two papers, two bodies, one line of standing iron chalked on gateposts at the height of the iron. He understood that the district was doing what districts do, writing to everybody, and that the writing itself was the pressure, not any decision in it. A body that answered both letters with the same map would have merged two costs into one book. The fen had refused to merge them. That was the refusal with teeth, and it left marks.
