# Chapter 73

## What It Is Worth

Coll Renner came up the fen road on the fifth of the month at about the eighth hour with no box of tools and no form and no collar in his coat, and he said at the gate what he had come for, because he had learned in this district that a man who comes to a gate with nothing is treated differently from a man who comes with something, and that the difference is not in his favor.

"I have been asked three times since the eighth of last month to cut a bead for money," he said, "by three different men, and I have said no three times, and none of the three has given me a reason and none of the three has taken offense, which is the part I do not like. I am telling you before the middle of the month, in writing, on the twenty-ninth, as I said I would in a yard on the eighth, and I am telling you now as well because I have read the fourth of your standing log and because somebody has been in my yard asking my foreman what a collar weighs."

"What does it weigh," said Marek.

"Two ounces of copper and a nut of wood," said Renner. "What it is worth is the whole of this chapter and I have come up this road to have it in a book while it is happening to me and not afterward, which is the only thing I have ever wanted out of a season and I have said that to you before."

He came in and the room was full, because the arithmetic had been going on for two days and it is easier to do arithmetic in a room.

The offer was on the middle of the table where it had been since the twenty-eighth, two pages of a Marrowfield letterhead, and beside it Tova Reed had put four sheets of her own, and on the four sheets was every number the fen had, and the four sheets had been read aloud to the five households who came and to the man of about fifty and to the woman with the shilling and to the man of about seventy, who had put his hand flat on the well house door at the word *sold* and asked for his initial on nothing, because a feeling about a sale is worth nothing and cost the holding nothing.

"Read it again," said Dunmar Fitch, "because a number that has been read once is a number somebody can be right about by accident."

Tova Reed read it again, and read it the way she reads everything, with the ruler squared to the edge.

"The offer is a shilling a line on ninety-one lines, which is two pounds a month, paid at the quarter, and the term of it is eleven years, which is the length of the book. Two pounds a month is eight shillings. Compost is four shillings a hundredweight. That is two hundredweight of the three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight we are short every month, and five hundredths of the gap, and the gap is seventy-nine pounds sixteen, and the return line has said *nothing put in, none available* for thirty days and has said it three months running now, and the bed stands at three and a half degrees and a soil iron cannot read the tenth of a degree, and the graft is being fed about a twentieth of what it was designed to be fed, and the nineteen acres are standing and the Longneck is leaning eleven degrees and the leak is at five-eighths and it did not move under a full draw on Saturday and it is not mended, and I have read all of that to this room and I am going to read it again now because the second half of the offer is not the money."

She put her hand on the second page.

"The second half is the term. For eleven years, this holding's name goes on every certificate issued off this book, as the keeper of record, and this holding undertakes that it will not afterwards say the seed was certified by anybody else. So in the fourth year of the term, on a morning like this one, a man in a shed will take a cutting out of a pot and a cutting out of a field bed and tie them with green withy, and he will be able to do it and sell it and certify it, because for eleven years this holding has been the only book in the trade anybody could check, and I have been the only person in it who could read a hand."

She sat down.

"It is not a price," she said. "It is a lease on the only thing in this fen that cannot be grown, and the rent is two hundredweight of compost a month, and I have never in my life been able to say a thing more clearly than I have just said it, and I would like somebody to tell me that there is a better reading of those two pages, and I have offered to take it from anybody in this room."

The man of about fifty took it up, because it was his to take up and because he had been the loudest in this yard all season and Dunmar Fitch gave him the floor for it, which was the rule.

"You have been talking about the book since the fourth of last month," he said. "I have read it and I have written my initial against half of it. And I will tell you what I think it is worth to this holding. It is worth a bed off three and a half degrees. It is worth a graft that is not being starved. It is worth putting four hundred hundredweight into a return main in a month and having the pump actually held at eleven hundred for a year instead of a day. It is worth a child in one of these houses having a proper winter in the fifth year of his life instead of the sixth, and it is worth it to me to put my hand flat on the table and say so, and I am saying so, and I would like it in the book with my name against it in the way you do."

"It goes in," said Dunmar Fitch, "with your name against it and the hour, and read back before it is initialled, and the reader is a person and not a room, and you are the person."

The woman with the shilling said that eight shillings a month was eight shillings a month and that she had said so on the tenth of last month about a survey and had been refused, and that this time she was not asking for anything and only wanted it in the book that she had said it.

Jonas Byre said that a crew of four at eighteen pounds was in Renner's price list and the whole of this holding's levy four times over, and that a man who cannot pay a levy four times over was not going to be paid by a firm at Marrowfield every quarter, and that the day the first certificate went out on a letterhead with this holding's name at the top of it, the levy would come round and would be four times what it was because the men would know there was money somewhere, and he said that out loud and then said he was sorry to say it and meant it.

Ilsa Kern said nothing, which in that yard was a position, and later at the embankment she told Jonas Byre that the woman from the counting house had offered to be told how to ask the question about cuttings and that was the most frightening thing anybody had said in that yard all season, and Jonas Byre said that, and neither of them said what it was, and they went back to the willow.

And then Marek said the thing he had come in wanting to say, and he said it badly, on purpose, because he wanted it minuted badly.

"Two hundredweight a month is two hundredweight a month," he said. "I do not care whose name is on the top of the sheet. There is a leak at five-eighths I cannot mend and seventy-nine pounds sixteen a month of compost I cannot buy and a third condition in clause three that nobody will describe to me and that will be tested on the first examination after this charter is dead, and the only two offers anybody has put on that table in a season are a form with a line at the foot of it and a page of paper with a name on it. Put the name on it. I will sign it. I would rather sell the record than lose the holding."

Marek looked at the floor.

"Write that down," he said. "Write it in those words and do not tidy it, and put my name against it, because in four years a man in an office is going to find the engineer of record's name at the top of the first certificate in this trade and I would like the page above it to say that he asked for it in a yard in daylight with the number two hundredweight written out."

Dunmar Fitch read that back twice and initialled it and did not soften one word, and Anneke Roos read it back as a person, and nobody argued with him, because he was not wrong about the compost.

And then Tova Reed stood up and put a second page on the table, and it was a printer's estimate, and it was the reason she had not slept much on the fourth.

"It is not the offer or nothing," she said. "This holding will set its eleven years in print. Ninety-one lines, every origin, the true name of every line and the wrong one beside it where the wrong one has been used, and the four lines I have never indexed, and my ninth line, and the statement on the front page that the seed house of Lantern Reach copied one of its own names off a Crown list in a year it does not name. Two hundred pages. A shilling a copy, to anybody, in the yard, on a Monday, for as long as there is anybody left to sell them to."

"And the cost," said Dunmar Fitch.

"Eleven shillings to a printer at Fennmere for the setting, which I am told is the same as the collar and I do not believe it, and which I am not asking this holding for." She put her own hand flat on the page. "The Dryfoot Trust is a named party to the seed arrangements of this node and this is the trust's own eleven years and I am the trust's steward and I have a printing account at Cray with a standing order on it, and eleven shillings out of that account is mine to spend and it is the only money in this room that is not short, and I am spending it, and it is being entered against the trust's interest for the ninth time and I would like a person to read it back."

Lissa Vale read it back, and Marek read it back after her, and the eleven shillings went in the book against the trust's interest, and Tova Reed struck out a figure she had penciled underneath it, which was the number of copies she thought might sell in a year, and made Dunmar Fitch initial the striking, the way she had made him initial the striking of the number of bags that were not sold.

"And what it buys," said Marek, "is about thirty copies and some of them in Marrowfield."

"It buys the record," said Tova Reed. "You will not sell a great many of them and I have struck the figure out myself. What you will do is that the first person who asks any of us anything about a line we keep will be able to read the whole of it without asking us, and a house that buys on print will read it, because it is in print, and it is true, and it is worse for us than anything anybody has ever told them. That is what a record is for. It is not for us. We have been arguing about that for a month and I have been the one arguing hardest that a thing only the six of us read is a diary, and I am not going to take it back now that it is inconvenient."

Renner asked the question in the middle of the afternoon and he asked it to Marek and not to the room, and it was the question of a man who had been in a shed all his life.

"You have both been talking about the record for a week," he said, "and neither of you has once asked me what the thing in the tin is worth. Let me put it on the table. It is four seconds and a wet cloth, and it reads the last hand, and every one of you in this room now knows that it reads a person, and a person is the only thing in this trade that cannot be grown and cannot be printed and cannot be held in a book anybody may read." He put his case on the table and did not open it. "I want to know what it says about itself, and I have asked for that once in a year and I was told no in a yard on the thirtieth of last month, and I am not asking again. I am asking whether the man who bought the collar off me is the one who lays it down."

Lissa Vale wrote the use-log line before anybody touched anything, and read it back, and Marek read it back after her, and it went in as the eleventh line and it recorded the date and the hour and the instrument and the purpose and the fact that a licensed man was present and named him and the roll he is on, and it recorded that the contact was tested before the contact and the reading of the test written down first in Renner's own hand, and it recorded nothing whatever about what came out, which is the practice of this book and which Lissa Vale has said out loud twice.

Renner fitted his own clip and took the collar out of its damp sand and laid the instrument against the wood of the bead, and the contact went, and it took eleven seconds, and Marek could not account for the eleven seconds any better than he had accounted for the four on a cut face on the seventeenth of last month.

The four inches along the inside of his forearm were hot before it was over, and stayed hot for the rest of the afternoon, and his hand from the wrist down was no use to him from the second hour, and Renner held the case for him down the seed-house steps because he could not get the latch with two fingers.

It said three things, the way it says three things, and not in a sentence anybody would want.

> The ring reads the last hand.
>
> It does not read the ground, and it never will.
>
> There is nothing here a buyer may not buy.

Renner read them twice. Then he sat down on the step of the well house, which is not a thing a licensed man of forty-one does in front of witnesses, and said: "Then I have been selling a window."

Nobody wrote it down. Marek asked Lissa Vale whether it could be, and she gave him the reason in one sentence and it was the same reason she had given on the twelfth of last month and the same reason Dunmar Fitch had given on the third of it about a different set of lines: *there is nothing in it that a man with a rule can check, and a line in a book that cannot be checked is a line somebody can use.*

Renner stood up and said the sentence that was his price, and he said it to the yard and not to the table.

"Do not enter it," he said. "I know you will not and I would have refused it anyway. Listen to what entering it would cost me and then decide for yourselves whether the fen is being charitable. I have four collars and nine men and a foreman who has never read a book. If I can put those three lines in a book that anybody may read, then any man in this district with a wet cloth can read them off a collar in a market and say that the ring reads the last hand, and he will be able to sell nine carriers for every line, because that is what the third one says, and I will not be able to say where he got it, because it will be in your book and your book is the only book anybody trusts." He picked his case up. "So the fen is going to decide this without it, and I am going to have to sell four collars in a season to men I cannot teach, and I am telling you that now so that when it happens nobody in this yard can say they did not see it coming."

Marek asked him at the gate, with his right hand in his pocket and his left arm held the way a man holds an arm that is no use to him, whether the three lines had settled anything, and Renner said no, and that they had cost him a day of work and four inches of somebody else's arm and had told him nothing he had not been told on the thirtieth of last month by a woman at the counting-house end, and that if those three lines had settled anything he would have to be a fool, and he was not a fool, he was forty-one and he had nine men and a yard.
