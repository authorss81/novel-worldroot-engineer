# Chapter 74

## In Front Of The Book

The body voted on the seventh of the month, in the morning, in daylight, in the yard, with the book shut under Dunmar Fitch's hands, and the woman from Marrowfield was at the gate for the whole of it because she was not of the body and because Dunmar Fitch said so out loud before anybody started.

"She is at the gate," he said, "because a body that takes a decision a buyer has paid for in a yard is a body that has already lost it, and I will not have it in a book anywhere. She will be told at the end. She is owed that and nothing else."

Nobody in the yard argued with him, because the rule that a person reads an entry before it is initialled had been in force for eighteen days and a half the room had understood it.

He read the two things out first, in full, both of them, and the room had the terms in front of it before anybody spoke. The Marrowfield offer, two pages, a shilling a line on ninety-one lines, two pounds a month at the quarter, eleven years, this holding named as the keeper of record on every certificate and forbidden afterward to say the seed was certified by anybody else. And the printing, two hundred pages, eleven shillings to a printer out of the trust's account, a shilling a copy to anybody, the true name of every line and the wrong one beside it, and four lines never indexed before now.

Then he said the count, because a vote taken without a declared count is a vote that gets read wrong later.

"The body is six of us and the households of the levy that put nothing in are not of this body and do not vote, and the man of about fifty and the man of about seventy are of the body and vote, and there is no third category and I have checked it twice."

Seven hands could be counted. Seven were in the yard.

And then the yard did the thing that had not happened in this holding before, which was that the thing that decided it was not an argument.

Tova Reed stood up at the counting-house end and asked for the fourth of the four to be read, and Lissa Vale read it, because Lissa Vale reads.

She read it in a slow careful voice and she did not look at Tova Reed while she read it and she did not stop and she did not soften one word of it, and it took about a minute and a half and it is in the book in the reader's hand and the reader is the fen's own keeper.

"The fourth of the four lines never indexed, entered in this fen's book on the fourth of this month and going to Cray on the Thursday post. The lot entered in this holding's ledger eleven years ago under the printed name of the Ashcombe black oat. It was entered by me, at the counting house at Cray, in the eleventh month, out of a tin that had come north in a cart with no label on it and had come through a man who wanted to be helpful, and I was not able to establish the line and I knew that I was not able to establish the line, and an unestablished name is not sown and this holding had nothing else that season and thirty-one households would have gone into the winter without a bean. And I wrote the name down."

She came to the end of it and the yard was the quietest it had been in a season, and the pump ran, and the lamp above the seed-house door was burning on its living bulb.

"I have carried that for eleven years," said Tova Reed, "and I want two things said about it and I want them said in this yard and not in a room. The first is that it was a decision about food and not about a name, and that I would take it again on the same ground and that a reader in a book four counties away is not going to know what the ground was. The second is that the printing says it on a page anybody may buy for a shilling, and that after today the name of the woman who did it is going to be on every copy of it in this district, and I did not stop that and I am not going to pretend to have tried."

Marek Vale stood on the well house step, because that is where he stands.

"I have been asked to say one thing in this yard," he said, "and I am going to say it badly and I am going to say it with the number in it, and I would like it read back.

"The largest wrong in the eleven years of this book was entered by the person standing next to me. Not by a printing office four counties away. Not by a man in a shed at Marrowfield who did what a man in a shed does. By her, at Cray, eleven years ago, out of a cart with no label on it, and I have read that sentence out to two households on this road this week and I would like it read to this yard before anybody puts a hand up, because a vote taken with that in the room is a different vote from one taken without it, and this room is the only place in this district where that sentence is going to be said out loud and I am not going to be the man who says it after the counting."

Nobody moved for a moment and then Dunmar Fitch read that back as well, in the flat voice he uses for procedure, and initialled it, and the reader was a person and the person was Jonas Byre, who read it twice.

Then the vote was taken standing, one at a time, with each hand named as it went up, and Dunmar Fitch entered the name and the hour against each one, because the vote of the twentieth of last month established that a book anybody may read has to show who decided and who only heard.

Seven hands. Five for the printing and against the certificate. Two against.

And one of the two was the engineer of record.

He put his own name in the book against his own vote and did not make a speech about it, and the reason is that he had already made the speech on the fifth and it was in the book in the words he had used and the room had read it back, and he said that a man who says a thing twice has stopped being a witness and become an advocate, and that the body had better not do that about anybody.

The woman with the shilling put her hand up for the printing and said why, out loud, in one sentence, which is her standing in this yard now and which nobody has ever tried to take from her: "Because it is true, and I have been drinking off a bed this holding killed for a year, and I would rather be on the right side of a book that says so than on the wrong side of one that says nothing."

Lissa Vale put her hand up first, before anybody, and would not say which way, and when Anneke Roos asked her to state it she refused and gave a reason and it is the reason she has given to everything since the fourth of last month.

"I have been the reader in this yard for a season," she said, "and the last thing a reader does is tell a room what a page means before the room has read it. I have my hand up and I will not say what it is for, and I would like the clerk to write down that I declined, and the reason, and that I did not decline to vote."

Dunmar Fitch entered that, and read it back, and she initialled it, and nobody thanked her and nobody said a word to her about the ninth tray or the forty-one trays or the black bend in the stem, which is what she had been owed since the twenty-fifth of last month and which is not owed by anybody, because there is no clause in a charter or a fifth sheet or a book about a woman being thanked.

Anneke Roos then did the thing she does, which is to wait until a decision has been taken and then to find out whether it was legal, and she found one thing and she was right about it.

"Eleven shillings out of the trust's printing account," she said, "on a standing order at Cray, in a month when the trust has not been asked, and the road is out at two places and a letter is three days there and three days back if it holds, and if it does not hold there is no answer at all before the type is set. You have spent another body's money on a thing that body has not agreed to, in front of a woman from a counting house, and I want it entered that I noticed it after the vote and not before it, and that I am not questioning the decision. I am questioning the paper."

Tova Reed had it written out already and gave it to her to read.

"The trust's steward may draw on the trust's printing account at Cray up to a shilling a page without reference," she said, "and two hundred pages at a shilling is the account and not my discretion, and there is a standing order on it that has been honored six years running. If the clerk in Cray disapproves of these two hundred pages the eleven shillings come out of my own hand and not out of this holding's box, and I have entered that, and the clerk is a man named in a letter and does not need to be named here."

"Read it back to her," said Dunmar Fitch, and the reader was the man of about fifty again, because he was still standing in the yard and it was his turn by the rule, and he read the six lines twice and said he did not understand the middle of the second one, and was told that that is the correct use of a reader and not a fault in it, and initialled the reading.

Afterward, in the yard, with the book still shut, Tova Reed put her hands in front of her and said one more thing.

"Two hundredweight a month," she said. "That is what the offer was worth and it is five hundredths of the gap and I want it entered that I said that in a yard and that I said it while I was losing and not before. The gap is three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight and it will be three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight on the first of next month and the first of the month after that, and the bed is at three and a half degrees, and the graft is being fed a twentieth, and the leak is at five-eighths and did not move under a full draw and is not mended, and nineteen acres are standing and the Longneck is leaning eleven degrees, and none of that is going to change because we chose the truth. It is a good trade. It is the only good trade anybody has put in front of this body in a season and it does not pay for the compost and I have never in my life been glad of a thing that cost me this much."

Then she went to the gate and told Ottoline Fray, in about ninety words, standing at the posts, and the buyer listened to all of it without once looking at the fen road behind her.

"Well," she said. "Then I will tell you what happens, and I am going to say it plainly and you will not like the second half of it. I will not take your book on a certificate at any price, and I will not take it at a shilling a line and I will not take it free. What I will do is put your eleven years in my winter list without your name on any certificate, at a shilling a copy, and I will sell them in four counties as a buyer's reference, because a house that keeps a book a stranger may read is worth money to a house that wants to say where its seed came from and has never had a way to show it."

She opened the case and took out four shillings and put them in Marek's hand.

"Four copies," she said. "And that is every shilling I am going to put into this fen this season and I have not given a discount in twenty-two years and I am not going to start with a body that has just told me the truth about itself in public at nine in the morning. And I want to say the second half, and you may write it down, and it is not a kindness.

"A line that nobody can prove is a line that nobody can sell. I have been in this trade twenty-two years and I have sold seed in a hundred and forty houses and I could not take you to the first one of them that could show you where its mother's seed came from, and I have never once been able to answer a question about a cutting because there is no column for a cutting, and I did not know until the twenty-eighth that that was anybody's fault but mine." She shut the case. "I do not want your method. I want a way of asking, and you have just given me eleven pages of my own ignorance in a form I can print, and I shall be poorer by four shillings and better at my business by the spring, and I shall send you a line of my own in a month, and I do not know what I shall do with it when it comes, and I have not the least idea how to ask that, and I would be obliged if somebody in this fen would tell me."

The four shillings went into the seed house tin that held nine, and Lissa Vale read the amount out loud before it was entered, because the vote of the twentieth says a person reads an entry before it is initialled, and the person was the man of about fifty, who had stood in the yard for an hour and a half and had counted seven hands and could not read a line, and who read the four shillings out slowly and correctly and put his own name against the reading of it, and said afterward that he had put a hand up for the printing and that he would rather not have been asked to read the money.

Underneath the vote, dated the seventh, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the twelfth note under section nine of the charter schedule: the offer from a house at Marrowfield for the use of this holding's eleven years at a shilling a line for eleven years, the arithmetic on the page, the printing entered in the same hour at eleven shillings out of the trust's account, the vote of seven in a yard in daylight with the names and the hours and one of the two against being the engineer of record, the fourth of the four lines never indexed entered in the fen's book and read aloud by the fen's own keeper and going to Cray, and the offer refused, and the four hundred hundredweight a month short, and the third condition not named, and the twenty-ninth of the month after next twenty-two days from this morning.

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*
