# Chapter 70

## A Certificate

The woman at the gate on the twenty-eighth said her name and her house before she was asked for either, which is the first thing anybody has done at that gate in a season.

"Ottoline Fray, of Fray and Daughters, at Marrowfield. Four generations and a counting house and forty-one smallholders, and I am the third one and I have been doing it twenty-two years, and I am not here about the oat."

Marek went out to her because she was at the posts, and Dunmar Fitch came to the gate behind him with the book open on the top step and wrote the name down as it was given, with the hour, and read it back to her, and she listened to it the way a person listens to a thing she intends to quote later.

She had a case in her own hand and a boy of about fifteen with a box, and she said at the gate that the case was hers and the box was her own and neither of them was a gift and neither of them was a sample, and that if the fen wanted to look at them they would look at them in the yard where anybody could watch, and Marek said that was the right way to do it and took her in.

She was about forty-one. She had a counting house behind her and she had spent a walk of two hours getting the dust out of a coat that had come nine miles on a cart, and she took the table in the seed house the way a woman takes a table she has paid for.

The case opened onto two things.

The first was a collar. The same article, and a better one than the fen's, with a bead of living rootwood in it that was the size of a small plum and not a hazel nut, and she said where she had got it and when and what she had paid and the name of the man, all four facts, without being asked, and Marek wrote the man's name down and did not enter the price.

The second was a sheet of printed paper, folded in three, and she unfolded it on the middle of the table beside the fieldbook and the use log and the Ashcombe index and put her finger on the top of it.

"That is a certificate of origin," she said. "Two hundred went out with my winter list in the spring and I have used thirty-one of them. It is on my letterhead and it carries a figure, and the figure is a bead reading, and the bead is in that case, and I do not check it against anything, because there is nothing to check it against. It takes four seconds and a wet cloth."

It was a good sheet the way a form is good, in that somebody had thought about where the lines went. Three ruled lines with printed heads: *ground* and *season* and *keeper of record.* Three lines, no more, and the room read it three times.

"Ground," said Tova Reed, from the counting-house end. "Season. Keeper of record."

"Ground, season, keeper of record," said Ottoline Fray, "and I will tell you the reason for those three and no more, because I have thought about it for a year and I have not told a soul, and I have got about four days before my winter list goes to press and I have come two hundred miles with a collar in a case to say it out loud in a room with the fen in it, and I would like the ground first."

She wet a twist of paper on the corner of the certificate, shook out about forty grains, and laid her collar on them.

It went green at the wood, and Marek felt four seconds of a shape through the copper that he had not felt before, and it was a place.

"Salt March," said Ottoline Fray. "The dry ground above the third sluice, that is Marrowfield's word for it and the march's word for it is the low dry. Last sowing. The season before that, a ley. That is a lot of the March's dry-land rye, and I have the certificate in front of you, and both of those are true."

Tova Reed did not look at the certificate. She looked at the boy, who was about fifteen, and then at the case, and then she put both hands flat on the table.

"Whose hand is in the keeper line," she said.

"It is in ink," said Ottoline Fray. "It is in my clerk's hand and it is the name of the man whose shed it was grown in, and I am going to tell you something about that line and then I am not going to talk about it again, because I would like to finish the four days in a state where I can still think."

She put her finger on it.

"That device cannot tell you a person's name," she said. "It has never told anybody a person's name and it is not going to start, and I have had mine since the spring and I have read about four hundred lots on it and I have never once had it hand me a name. So the keeper line is the one line on that sheet that no device in the world can fill in, and the person who fills it in is the person who sells the lot, and the person who sells the lot is me, and the name on that line is a man I have bought seed off for eleven years and have never once been in his shed."

Nobody said anything.

"So I have been selling a certificate," she said, "and on every one of the thirty-one I have put a keeper on it who did not put the seed in the ground, and the ground and the season on it are true, and the keeper is a man I chose, and the two facts sit on the same sheet in the same ink and a buyer sees one thing and not two."

Lissa Vale read the sheet out loud before it went anywhere near the book, and then she wrote the ninth use-log line and read it back, and it was: the twenty-eighth, the eleventh hour, the seed-house table, a collar in a copper ring laid against a wetted lot, a licensed man not required and not present, the lot the property of a house at Marrowfield, four seconds, and nothing at all about what the four seconds were. And Marek initialled it, and Jonas Byre read it back, and nobody remarked that the woman who had said on the twenty-fifth that a wet cloth was not a contact had written a line for a wet cloth anyway, and that a thing which cannot be checked is a thing that has to be counted, and a book with eleven lines of contacts in it and not one finding in it is a book that has learned something.

Then the woman at the counting-house end said the thing that made the twenty-eighth, in a voice so level that three people in that room afterwards said they had known she had had it ready.

"Show me the lot's own record," said Tova Reed. "Not the certificate. The house's record. What your firm wrote down when it bought the seed it grew this lot from, and the year before, and what the man in the shed said it was cutting out of."

Ottoline Fray did not pretend not to understand. She opened the box and took out a sheet of her own book, which is a very ordinary sheet with four columns and a clerk's hand, and set it on the table.

"Eleven parts carrier, one part line," she said. "That is what the lot is. It was grown out in the spring from a cutting and it is nine parts of a Marrowfield carrier and one part of the line, and the certificate does not say so, and it is not going to, and there is no column for it, and if there were a column for it the sheet would be a different shape and I would have had it set at the end of the year and not at the end of the spring."

"It says the line is the March's dry line," said Marek.

"It is the March's dry line," said Ottoline Fray. "All of it. Every grain in that bag is descended from a cutting of that line and the cutting came out of a shed and the shed is the only fact in the chain and the shed is a fact about a man and not about the line. Your reader is a device and it reports what is in the wood, and what is in the wood is the March's dry line, and it says the March's dry line, and it is right. I have not sold anybody a lie. I have sold them one part in eleven and told them what the one part is, and a man who sows it will get the line and a man who saves the seed off it and sows it again in a second year will get the carrier, and there is nothing on any form in this trade that will tell him which he has got, because I have had that question asked of me four times in twenty-two years and I have never had an answer worth giving anybody."

Tova Reed looked at the certificate for a long time and then she took her own pencil out and squared the sheet to the edge of the table with the ruler, the way she squares everything, and wrote on the back of it in pencil, four figures and a sum, and pushed it across to Dunmar Fitch to be read.

"He has got it right," she said. "Eleven parts carrier, one part line, and the device reports the line, and the certificate is true, and the harvest is the line for one season and the carrier for every season after it, and a seed house that certifies its own seed and then re-sows its own harvest has certified a thing that is not there." She sat down. "He has answered the question I was going to ask him. He has answered it before I asked it and he came two hundred miles to answer it, and I would like the room to notice that, because a man who answers a question before it is asked is doing me a kindness and I have not been in a room with one of those since the fourth of last month."

Then she said the other thing, and she said it to Ottoline Fray and not to Marek, and it took her some time to get to it.

"Now the shed," she said. "I am going to ask you one question and I would like the answer in a room and not in a letter, because a letter can be mislaid and a room cannot. Who gave your man the cutting."

"He bought it," said Ottoline Fray. "He buys cuttings. It is what the shed does. It is on his sheet and I have not looked at the sheet because I have never had a reason to look at the sheet, and I am not going to stand here and be blamed for a thing I did not do, Mr Vale, but I am going to say the true thing, which is that I have not looked at a cutting sheet in twenty-two years because nobody has ever asked me to and because there has never been a column for it. If you can tell me how to ask it I will put it in my own book this winter, and it will cost me about four lines a season and I will lose business in four counties that I cannot replace, and I would rather lose it than find out about it afterward, which is a sentence I have never said out loud in my life."

Tova Reed put both hands flat on the table and stayed like that for a while.

"Then that is a thing that can be taught," she said, "and it cannot be taught in four seconds, and there is a man in this district who has a form with a line at the foot of it that nobody will fill in, and I am not going to say his name at a table where a stranger is sitting."

She put her hands in her lap and looked at the certificate and then at the fen's own ledger standing open at the back of the shelf, ninety-one lines in eleven years, four of which had never been indexed by anybody including herself.

"Here is the whole of what I came for," said Ottoline Fray, and took out a fourth sheet, and it was two pages and it was her own letterhead, and it had been written before she left Marrowfield. "A shilling a line. Ninety-one lines, two pounds a month, paid at the quarter. Your eleven years, on my letterhead, as a source of certified origin, and your name on it as the keeper of record, and your name on every certificate I issue off it, and the undertaking that this holding will not afterwards say the seed was certified by anybody else."

Lissa Vale said, "Say the term."

"The term is eleven years," said Ottoline Fray, "and I will put the term on the sheet in a line of its own because I am not a fool, and eleven years is the length of your book, so at the end of it I hold your history and you hold a promise and the promise is the cheaper of the two things."

Tova Reed did the arithmetic on the back of a seed sack with a pencil and read it out, twice, and had Dunmar Fitch read it a third time with the hour, and it went in the book in her own hand, in daylight, at the middle of the table, and it is the ninth line against the trust's interest.

"Two pounds a month is eight shillings a month," she said. "Compost is four shillings a hundredweight. Eight shillings is two hundredweight. We are three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight short a month. So this offer buys us two hundredweight of the three hundred and ninety-nine, which is five hundredths of the gap, which is nothing, and it costs the fen the only thing in this building that cannot be grown, and the thing that cannot be grown is the record, and I would like the two numbers to be read together and not separately, because separately the first one is a fortune and the second one is a robbery and together they are what it is."

The yard wanted to call it a sale of the fen. The man of about fifty said so first, loudly, in the doorway. The woman with the shilling said it second and more quietly, and said that eight shillings a month in a season when the return line said nothing put in, none available, again, was eight shillings a month and that she had four children and one of them had not had a proper winter in four years.

Nobody rescued anybody and nobody was cruel. Dunmar Fitch read the offer out in the yard afterward to the five households who came, standing on the well house step, and read the arithmetic with it, and read Tova Reed's nine words under it, and nobody went up the road feeling better than they had come down.

Then the woman from Marrowfield asked the question that turned the afternoon into the next week, and she asked it in the voice of a woman doing arithmetic and not making a point.

"When is it wanted," she said.

"My winter list goes to press on the fifth of next month," she said. "I would want the book on the second. Four days from today."

"There is a cart to Cray in nine hundred miles and a hundred and eleven miles of ordinary road," said Tova Reed. "Three days there and three days back if the road holds, and the March road has been reported out at two places since the fourteenth, and the fourteenth was the day this holding worked out for itself that a fourteen-day notice had run out. I cannot ask the trust. I am a steward of that trust and I am nine hundred miles from its counting house and it is a named party to a charter condition and I am not entitled to an answer in four days and I have not had one in a month."

"You have not had one in a month," said Ottoline Fray, and did not soften it, and put her own sheet away in her case, and then said the sentence that the whole of the next two chapters turned on and that she said at a seed-house table in a fen, to six people, without any idea at all that she was doing anything except being honest.

"Then I will tell you what I will do, and I would like it read back to me afterward because I have not said anything like it in twenty-two years. If you cannot answer me in four days I will not take your book on the second. I will take it on the fourteenth, or the twenty-ninth, or not at all, and I will not go round a body that could not answer me in four days, because a body that could not answer me in four days has somebody nine hundred miles off holding its name, and the last time I took a line from a house I could not telephone, it went into nine hundred smallholders in a season and I did not find out for two years."

Nobody said anything for a moment, and then Lissa Vale read the whole of it back, as she read everything, in a slow careful voice, and Ottoline Fray listened to it with her hand on her case, and when it was finished she said, "That is right," and nothing else, and Lissa Vale wrote down in her own book that it was heard and that no answer was given and that the reader was a stranger who was owed nothing by this body and had offered it a date anyway.
