# Chapter 72

## The Third Time

Tova Reed chose the third of the month, which was a Monday, and she chose the hour, which was the ninth, and she asked for the floor.

"I have been in this room for a season at a table," she said, "and a table is where a body makes decisions, and what I am asking for tonight is not a decision, it is a count. I want every tray in this seed house on the floor and I want the tins open and I want the lamp on the floor where the lamp does not hurt anybody's eyes, and I want Lissa Vale to say what is in each thing before the collar goes near it, because she keeps the trays and I keep the lines and if either of us is wrong tonight the other one has to be able to say so from across the floor."

Lissa Vale said yes to that in one word, and then said four sentences of conditions, and one of them was that the man of about fifty was going to be allowed to sit down, and the man of about fifty said he had been going to ask, and Lissa Vale told him he was not to get used to it.

They put the lamp on the floor at about the ninth hour with a flat box under it and the fieldbook on a chair and the use log on a second chair, and the tables were pushed back against the wall, and the room looked like a room nobody lived in. Thirty-one trays came down off two shelves and nine tins came off a third, and the seed house floor was covered, and Dunmar Fitch sat down on the boards with his book on his knees because a man cannot read a book and stand up over thirty trays.

Lissa Vale read out what was in each thing as she set it down, in the order she set them down, in her own hand's order, which nobody else in that room could reproduce.

The first was out of a bulk sack, the fen's own bean, the lot the seed house buys by the bushel and does not choose.

The collar went on and it was four seconds and a place.

"This fen," said Marek. "The low strip above the toe ditch. The season before, after a ley."

"That is right," said Lissa Vale, "and it is right every time, and it tells us nothing we could not have told in eleven years by looking at the sack."

The second was the same variety of bean, out of the ninth tray.

"Kept off the second shelf, sown the same day, from the same sack," said Lissa Vale, "and it is the ninth tray of the sowing of the second year back, the one with the black bend in the stem, and I kept it against the drill because it was the worst of the forty-one and the only one with a clean root."

The collar went on and it was four seconds and it was not a place.

"The twenty-second," said Marek. "The second hour. A hand taking four things off a bench and putting one of them in a corner and leaving the other three where they were, and the third thing taken off the bench was thrown and where it went was in it as well."

Lissa Vale put her hand over her mouth for about a second and then took it away and did not explain it, and Dunmar Fitch wrote nothing, and the room did not ask her, and one of the nine lines in the standing log that morning had been a bed at three and a half degrees and none of it touched this.

They went through the floor.

Of thirty-one trays, six read as a place, and those six were the six that had come out of a bulk sack or a tin and had not been chosen by anybody since. The other twenty-five did not. They read as a hand, and a season, and a decision, and a set of decisions in the order they were made, and the newest of them was the only one the device would say, and every one of the twenty-five had been made by a person in this room or by a person this room has never met.

Dunmar Fitch entered the count and the hour and read it back twice, and Anneke Roos read it back a third time because she had been standing in the doorway refusing to sit on the floor for the first half hour and had then sat on the floor with her back against the press, and she said the thing that is her job.

"That is a finding about a class of thing," she said, "and it is in a book a stranger may read, and I would like somebody to say out loud what the class is before anybody writes the word seedkeeper over it, because that word is going to be in a printer's file inside a month and I would rather we had put it there ourselves with our own reason attached."

"You may write it," said Tova Reed. "Write it and read it back to me. The class is this: a lot that has never been chosen by anybody reports where it was grown, and a lot that has been chosen by anybody reports who chose it and what they chose, and the second one is a better answer and it is not a better fact."

And then Tova Reed unlocked the case.

She took out a small tin, and inside the tin there was a paper twist, and inside the twist there were about sixty gray grains, and she set it on the floor in the middle of the open space between the trays where everybody could see it, and she did not say what it was, because she had said what it was on the twenty-fifth of last month in front of five people.

Lissa Vale wet it and laid the collar on it.

It went green and Marek felt four seconds with a place in them, a long dry flat place with a hard pan under it, and a season, and a ley.

"Salt March," he said. "The low dry above the third sluice. The sowing before last, and a ley the season before that."

"Yes," said Tova Reed.

She did not say anything else for a while, and the lamp hissed on the floor, and five people in that room watched a woman look at a seed and not move.

Then she put her hand into the case again and took out a sheet of paper, folded twice, and unfolded it on the boards, and it was not a printed form and it was not a district form and it had no mark on it at all, and it was a list of eleven names in a clerk's hand with a heading in her own hand at the top.

She read it out herself, in her own voice, and she did not hand it to Dunmar Fitch, and she did not look at anybody.

"Eleven holdings. The eleventh of the month, two seasons back, the year the dry line failed. I cut cuttings of the March's dry-line rye and I sent them to eleven holdings, and I wrote this list at the counting-house table at Cray, and I have carried it in this case for two seasons." She put her finger on the bottom name. "The eleventh is this fen. There is no column for cuttings in the trust's book. Cuttings are not seed. So it is not in the trust's book and it is not in the fen's book and it is not in any book, and I have told nobody for two seasons, and the reason I did not tell you, Mr Vale, is not a good one, and I am going to give it to you now and you may put it where you like."

She looked up.

"The reason is that it was the only thing I have ever done that I could not put in a column, and I did it because the line was dying and a steward keeps a dying line by putting it in more ground than it can afford to lose, and I have spent eleven years telling seed houses that a line kept in one book is a line that can be got at, and I put this line in eleven grounds and no book at all, and I would have done it again, and I would like the room to write down that I would have done it again."

Nobody wrote it down. Dunmar Fitch held his pen and looked at her and did not put a word of it on the page, and Lissa Vale said, quietly, that a thing a person says in a room at night is a record when somebody reads it back and not before, and that she was not going to read it back tonight, and Tova Reed said, "That is fair and I would rather it were," and meant it, and Lissa Vale did not look at her.

Then Tova Reed said the arithmetic, and it is the sentence the volume turns on, and she said it to the floor.

"The line is not lost," she said. "It was never lost. It is in eleven places and it is in no book, and a man in a shed at Marrowfield has a cutting of it, and he has had it for two seasons, and he sold you a lot of it and he gave you a certificate and the certificate is true, and the certificate says Salt March dry and the seed says Salt March dry and I have sat in this room for two seasons writing *the dry-land line is still lost* in three books, including a copy that went to Cray, and it is a wrong, and it is mine, and it is the largest wrong I have entered in eleven years and I entered the eighth line on the eighteenth of last month and I told the room that was the first one that said I was wrong about a thing and not about a number, and I was wrong about that too, and there is nobody in this room who is going to soften it including me."

Gael Senn had her pen down and it was the middle of the night and there was no charge in the charter for a statement and no form for it.

"I am not taking that line tonight," said Gael Senn. "It is not an examination and there is no hour on the examination sheet for it and the term ends in twenty-five days and I have a form of my own to think about that has got nothing in it. Read it back to me at nine in the morning and I will take it then and it will go in the log in the ordinary way with the hour on it, and I would like the word *largest* taken out of it because a log is not a place for a word like that, and I would like *the line is not lost* put in plain because that is a finding about a seed and this is a seed book."

Anneke Roos said that the word largest was hers to keep and would keep it, and that two of them could say the same thing in two books.

The ninth line went in at the ninth hour of the fourth of the month, in daylight, at the middle of the table, in Tova Reed's own hand, with the hour on it, and it is the ninth line against the trust's interest, and it is in the trust's book as well, and it goes on the Thursday post to Cray, and it reads:

The dry-land line of this trust was not lost. It was cut and dispersed to eleven holdings in the eleventh month two seasons back and no record of it was made in any book, because the trust's book has no column for cuttings. Three books have carried the statement that the line is lost. That statement is mine and it is a wrong. The line is in eleven grounds. I cannot name a ground of the eleven that I have seen this season, and the proof that the line was in a shed at Marrowfield two seasons ago is a certificate of origin on the letterhead of a house at Marrowfield and a collar and a buyer, and the proof was correct when it was given and I will not have it softened by anybody including me.

And underneath it, in her own hand, and read back before it was initialled, she entered the four lines she has never indexed, which are the four the seed house has never copied into the ledger and has never put in front of anybody, and which are now in the book in her own hand and in the printing and on the road to Cray, and one of the four is the line they have called the Ashcombe black oat for eleven years.

The room did what the room does. Lissa Vale read all of it back, twice, and Marek initialled the ninth line, and Anneke Roos read it back a second time as a person and not a clerk, and the man of about fifty, who had been sitting on the floor against the press for two hours, asked whether he should put a hand up to anything.

"You are not of the body," said Dunmar Fitch. "You are of the yard. I entered that on the twentieth and I am entering it again tonight, and the count of hands in this building is not a vote and there is nothing to vote about tonight."

At the counting-house end, after the room had gone, Marek said the professional thing to her and not the other thing, because he had worked out by about the eighth hour of the evening that the other thing was not his to say.

"A certificate says a name," he said. "A ledger says what happened. Yours does both, and now anybody who reads it can see the hand and the decision in the same line, and that is worth more to a seed house than four seconds ever was, and it is worth more to a person, which is the part nobody prices."

"Say that in the yard," said Tova Reed, "and not at this table, and I will deny that I said it here, and I will say it in the yard, and it will be in the book in the morning, and I would like it entered now that I have said I would say it and that I have asked you to be the one who says it."

"Then I will say it in the yard," said Marek Vale, "and you will stand there while I do, and the first thing out of my mouth will be that the largest wrong in the eleven years of this book was entered by the person standing next to me and not by anybody in a printing office four counties away, and I want that in the book too, and I want it read back."
