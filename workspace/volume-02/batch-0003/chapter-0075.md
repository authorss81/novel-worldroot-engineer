# Chapter 75

## The Man Who Refused

Coll Renner came up the fen road on the tenth of the month at the fourth hour in the afternoon with a folded sheet in his inside pocket, and he took it out at the gate and gave it to Marek before he came in, which was not the practice in this yard and which he had thought about.

"Read it before we go in," he said. "It is somebody else's writing and I have not had it a day. It came to my yard on the eighth, in a hand that is not a clerk's, and I have read it eleven times since and I want you to have it before I start talking, because I am going to say things about it that I would rather you formed first."

Marek read it at the gate in the cold.

It was half a sheet. It offered a licensed man in the Tarrow district twelve pounds for a collar of a stated size and a stated bead, and it asked for a standing instruction that collars be cut on a Sunday, when no holding is holding an examination and no licensed man is on a road. It signed itself with a name and a town, and the name was not Coll Renner's own and the town was not Fennmere.

"I have refused it," said Renner. "In writing, on the ninth, and I have sent the letter back by the same hand that brought it, and I have kept a copy, and here it is, and the copy is dated and it is signed by me and it says no in one line."

"You came up here on the tenth," said Marek. "Your date was the fifteenth."

"I know what my date was," said Renner. "I said the fifteenth and I have come on the tenth, and I would like the clerk to enter that I came early and not that I forgot, because if a later room reads this it will want to know whether I was in time or whether I was not, and the answer is that I was in time and I could not sit in a yard for five more days with that in my pocket. You taught me that a clock nobody enters is a clock that has not run, and I have entered it, and I entered it before the date, and I would like that in your book with the hour on it and not after."

Dunmar Fitch entered that at the gate, with the hour, and read it back, and the reader was Ilsa Kern, who had been on the embankment and had come in because somebody was at the gate earlier than usual, and who read it and said that a man arriving early is a fact about a man and not about a promise, and that she had not understood the second half of it and would not pretend she had.

Inside, the offer went on the middle of the table beside the fieldbook and the use log and the Ashcombe index, and Coll Renner told it out once, in his own words, in the flat voice he used for a figure he had already checked twice.

"Twelve pounds is my quarter," he said. "That is not the point and everybody in this room will spend the next ten minutes on the twelve pounds and I would like it entered that I said they would, and that I said the point is the Sunday. I have nine men and a yard and a foreman and a client list of forty-one holdings I have worked for or quoted, and I could make forty collars before the sowing and I would be a man who made forty collars on Sundays in a season, and in four years there would be a bead in every shed in this district and not one line in any book about who made it or when or on what day of the week, and the trade would call that a mature market and it would be the death of the only thing anybody has that cannot be faked, which is that a thing was done at an hour in front of a person."

He put his hand flat on the table, and Marek noticed the gesture and did not think about it, because he was thinking about something else.

"You made a rule in this yard in the spring," Renner said, "that no work on this node is done between dusk and dawn without a licensed engineer standing in it, and the reason you gave was that a thing done at night is a thing nobody can check. I have read that rule in your book about nine times. I am not going to be the first man in this district to sell a night, and if I did there would not be a book in this fen afterwards that could do one thing about it, and I would have taken twelve pounds for it, and the book would have been right."

He sat down.

"So that is my answer and it is the whole of it and I have had five days to think of a better one and there is not one. Now I am going to say the other half, which is the part that costs me, and I would like the fen to hear it and not to do anything about it, because I know what this fen is and I have watched it refuse three men in six weeks."

He said it facing the table.

"There were two men, not one, and I have said two because there were two. One of them buys on the Ashcombe index and I know his name, and I am not going to say it in a book, and I want the reason entered, because the reason is the only part of this that belongs to you." He looked at Dunmar Fitch. "If I say his name in your book, then your book is a thing somebody can take off me. That is all. A name in a book anybody may read is a name anybody may use, and I have watched this fen spend a month learning that and I have watched it learn it twice, and I am not going to be a man who teaches it a third time by being the example. So I am not saying the name, and if a room wants to write that down, write down that a man declined to name a buyer and gave his reason and that the reason is in his own words and that this body accepts it."

Anneke Roos said, from the doorway, in the voice she used for procedure: "I would like that entered as a standing of this holding. A man may decline to name a buyer in a book this body keeps. The clerk enters that he declined and why, and the body does not ask again, and the body does not enter the name if it is told one. Put it under section nine and give it to me and I will initial it as an opinion, and I have never in this yard initialled an opinion faster."

"It goes in the book," said Dunmar Fitch, "and the name is not asked for, and I will not have anybody in this yard go up the road and find it out and come back with it, and if one of you does that I will enter that you did it."

Tova Reed asked the one question in that room that nobody else had thought of, which was how many collars already existed.

"Four," said Renner. "I have made four in eleven years and I have given away two and I have sold one and I have lost one, and the one I lost is the one in this yard, and there is a fifth man in this district who has one and I do not know where he got it, and I have not asked, and I have thought about asking all week and I am not going to, and I would like that entered too, because there is a licensed man in this district with a bead that nobody in this yard can account for and it is not my business and I am saying so on the page so that nobody can say afterward that it was."

The book took all of it. Dunmar Fitch read it back, and the readers were three people in turn because the vote of the twentieth says a person reads and a person is anybody who comes, and Marek asked at the end of it whether Renner wanted the refusal in his own words in the fen's book as well as in his own, and Renner said yes, and entered it as a copy, and initialled the copy, and said that if it was going to be in a book he was going to see it in a book, and that was the whole of his price.

Lissa Vale fed the lamp that night and Renner did not stay for it, and at the gate he said the thing that was his real errand and that he had put off all afternoon because he had wanted the rest of it said first.

"I want two hundred words on the last page of your printing," he said. "I am not asking for your method. I have asked twice and been refused twice and I am not going to ask a third time. I am asking for the last page of a two-hundred-page book that is going into four counties, in plain words, saying what a bead reads and what it does not read, and what eleven parts carrier and one part line means, and that a lot of one part in eleven is a legal sale and the trade has agreed to call it one. Two hundred words. Set it. I will pay the compositor and I will not pay for the paper and I will not put my name on the top of the page and I will put it on the bottom with my license number, because a licensed man who will not sign a true thing is not worth a copper collar."

Tova Reed considered it for about as long as it took her to square a sheet to the edge of a table.

"You may have the last page," she said, "and it will be the last page, and there will be two sentences on it under your two hundred words and they are mine. The first is that a lot of one part in eleven is a sale the law allows and the trade has agreed to and that no office in this district will call it a fraud, and that is not an opinion, it is a notice. The second is that this holding has printed its eleven years in the same book and has said in it that it does not know where four of its own lines came from, and that a person who can print that can print anything, and that a person who cannot print that cannot be trusted with the bottom line of a page."

Renner read the two sentences twice and then said, "That is fair, and it is the first time in eleven years anybody in this trade has offered to warn a customer about their own seed in the middle of a sale, and I am going to set the two hundred words in the same room as those two sentences and I would like it understood that I have had to be argued into it and that I argued back."

Then he said the other thing, at the gate, in the cold, and it was the last of it and it was unasked for and he entered it in his own book before he left, which he did every time.

"I have filled in my own copy of form RW/3," he said, "and sent it to a holding on the Marrowfield road that cannot afford a cooperative, with the line at the foot written out in my hand and my name on it as the licensed man, and I did it on the sixth, and there is no compost in that holding and I have told them so in writing, and I have done the thing you refused me for and I have told you first because you would have found it in a year and I would rather you found it in a day. Put it in. I would rather it was in your book than that you heard about it in a county."

Marek stood in the gate with the offer in his hand and the copy of a refusal in his coat and thought about a man who had come up a road eleven miles on a Monday to hand a body two pieces of paper and one of them cost him twelve pounds and the other one cost him the only method in the district that worked.

"Six more days to the fifteenth," he said. "You were in time."

"I was in time," said Renner, "and I have spent five days knowing that being in time is not the same as being right, and there is not one rule in this fen's book about that, and I would like somebody to write one, and I have thought about writing it myself twice and I am a licensed man in a trade and a rule from me would be a rule I could be fined for breaking, and a rule from this holding is a rule nobody can put me in a cell for, and that is the whole of my standing in this matter and I would like it entered that I know it."

---

The fen tried two things on the eleventh and both of them failed, and it entered both of them, and the two of them together are the reason this batch ends the way it does.

The first was a form. Tova Reed wrote to the district water office for the Tarrow district on the eleventh, because the Dryfoot Trust is a named party to the seed arrangements of this node and a market in certified origin touches the trust's seed, and because a body that has just refused a certificate on principle can at least ask whether there is anything to report. The answer came back on the Thursday cart in four lines and it was correct.

A licensed man's private arrangement with a purchaser is not a matter for the district. The district examines a man on a working and not on a paper. There is no form. If a person believes a licensed man has done a thing contrary to his license, the person may write to the Assembly and the Assembly will examine him, and the Assembly will not tell the person what it found. The letter was signed by a clerk and the clerk entered the hour and the sender was a woman nine hundred miles from the only office that could do anything, and there is no copy of it in Cray and cannot be.

"We have just proved the volume's own argument in a letter," said Tova Reed, and put the four lines on the middle of the table where anybody might look. "There is a form for the water. There is a form for the seed. There is a form for a form. There is not one form in this district for a man being offered twelve pounds to cut a collar on a Sunday, and that is not because anybody is hiding anything, and I want that written down, because I have spent this month being angry at offices and this is the first office in a season that has done everything correctly and left us with nothing at all."

The second was a walk. Jonas Byre went to two of the eleven holdings named on Coll Renner's price list, on the eleventh, in the afternoon, with the offer in his coat and the refusal in his hand, and he came back at the ninth hour with a longer face than he had gone out with.

He told them at the well house door, standing, and Dunmar Fitch came out and wrote it down with the hour, which is the standing practice and which had by then been in force for three weeks.

"I told the first one everything," Jonas Byre said. "All of it, the twelve pounds, the Sunday, the two men, the refusal, the date. And the man heard me out and thanked me and asked me one question, which was what my standing is in this matter, and I said I am a member of a cooperative eleven miles off this road and my license is not a license for anything. And he said, thank you, and shut the door, and I do not blame him, because I would have done the same and I have said so in the book."

"That is not nothing," said Ilsa Kern.

"It is not nothing and it is not a warning either," said Jonas Byre. "The second one did not open the door at all. He has heard about the collar. He wanted to know what it cost and I told him what it cost and he is thinking about it, and there is nothing in this fen's book or this fen's letter or this fen's walk that will change his mind, and I have walked nine miles in the mud to find that out and I would like it entered by the clerk so that nobody sends anybody else."

Dunmar Fitch entered it. Two walks, two doors, one shut and one opened and thanked, and no standing whatever, and the fen having tried the only two things a fen can try, and the answer being that a district has no form and a holding has no standing and a book is the only instrument left, which is the argument this yard has been making all season and which has never once in eleven weeks been as expensive as it was on that afternoon.
