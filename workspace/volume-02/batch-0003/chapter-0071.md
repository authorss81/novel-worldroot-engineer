# Chapter 71

## Eleven Hundred

The first of the month was a Saturday, and the district holds its sittings on Saturdays, and Gael Senn had been coming to this examination since the first of a month two months back and had never once had a district officer in the room for it.

On this one there was one, and there was a licensed engineer, and there was a clerk with a form case who had walked up the fen road behind them both.

They came at the eighth hour. Coll Renner was forty-one and had walked eleven miles and did not go near the well house until he had been shown the order, and the order was on the district's paper with a district mark and a date on the twenty-fourth of last month and Hilary Skeath's name at the foot of it in ink, and Marek read it out twice and then handed it to Renner, who read it a third time and gave it back.

"Nine hundred and forty," Renner said. "That is the test I was on about the year before last, at a holding up the Marrowfield road, and the pump there is older than mine. Bring the boards and I will say what I want moved and you will move it."

They moved what he said. The boards went under the housing and the sacking came off the pit and the pit stood four feet of iron water and did not go up a quarter of an inch, and the dial went on the discharge, and the eleven trays of the Longneck were moved off the bench at the well house door because a man cannot read a dial through a drill, and Ilsa Kern moved them herself and put them down the row in the order they had been in, because that is how she does everything.

Gael Senn took her morning line first, at the seventh hour, before any of it, because the examination happens at the seventh hour and the test happens at the ninth and the order of the two is not a thing anybody may choose.

The leak was five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, with two rules, and the second rule the one that reads smaller on that corner, and pump on, and it went into the housing and not into the pit, and the pit was not up, and she wrote all of that in the words she uses, and read it back, and a person read it after her.

Then the beat. The eleventh, and she took it at the rail, and it was nine in the quarter hour with one run of four and the rest single, and it was at the rail and not at the coping stone, and cause was not established, and licensed person present was **one**, which it had never been before, because a licensed man was standing eight feet away with a clipboard.

That is the whole of what she wrote about it, and she wrote underneath it that a licensed man was present and had not been asked and had said nothing, and her name on the decision and not the thing's, and nobody in that yard wrote one word under the beat about what a man being eight feet away means, and it was entered as a contact and not as a finding, and the panel from the twelfth of last month was in no book and would stay in no book.

The monthly report went up on the cart at the ninth hour with nine lines in it, the way it had gone every month since Nia Vale wrote one out in a press in the spring and put her name under the figures. Nine lines, and nobody in that yard could say what the root had changed, and the ninth line said that, and the cart took it, and the root did not change on the first of a month because anybody looked at it.

Then the test.

Marek put the pump to full draw at the ninth hour and held it for an hour and ten minutes, which was ten minutes longer than the district's order said, and he did that on purpose and entered the ten minutes in his own book before the test, because a test that is longer than the order is a different test and the difference has to be on the page before the figure is on it.

The head went up to eleven hundred and stayed there.

It was not a good feeling. It was the number the log had claimed for six weeks and it held on a cold morning with the wind off the drains, and the frame came up to a hum at about ten hundred and forty and the pump ran without a noise in it, and the Longneck came up eleven degrees and stayed there, and the pit did not go up a quarter of an inch, and the soil iron in the seed house was read before and after and read three and a half degrees both times, and the bed was not fed for an hour and ten minutes because stopping it stops the bed, and that went in the fen's own book as a fact about an hour and not as a cost.

At the ninth hour and forty minutes, with the pump at eleven hundred, Gael Senn went down into the pit with the rule.

"Five-eighths," she said. "Twice. The second rule reads smaller and it reads smaller."

"Write under that," said Marek. "Write under that it is the first time this has ever been measured under the load the district has just applied, and that it is the same figure it has been every day for thirteen days, and that the man who told me in the spring that it could not be mended under load was right and has not been asked and is not here."

Gael Senn wrote under that, in her own hand, and read it back, and read it back again to a man from the Fennmere yard who had come to the top of the pit steps to look at the pump and had not been asked to and stayed, and she said afterward that a thing read twice to a stranger is a thing the stranger can be relied on to have heard, and that was the reason and not modesty.

The test ended at ten minutes past ten in the morning and the head came down and the frame went quiet and the bed was fed and the pump went to the night setting the way it always does, and nobody in that yard felt the hour as anything at all except in their hands.

Dunmar Fitch wrote it all down. The hour it began and the hour it ended and the ten extra minutes entered before the figure, and the figure, and the two readings of the leak, and the bed, and the pit, and the Longneck, and the standing of the ninety-nine other things that were not being tested, which is what a record of an untested node is mostly made of.

And then he put the arithmetic on the page next to the figure, because the volume's whole argument is that figures belong together and a test that stands alone is a comfort.

"One thousand one hundred at the head," he said. "Seven hundred and twenty taken by the ground, which is this holding's own figure from the twentieth of last month and has not moved. Three hundred drawn at Kellan Rusk's pump house and never above three hundred for six years, on a return any body may ask for. Eighty a day unaccounted for, for six years, one hundred and seventy-five thousand two hundred, entered in a district file on the twenty-fourth of last month on a licensed man's own disclosure."

"And the test does not touch it," said Marek. "That is what I have got to say and I would like it said in my own hand. I performed a full-draw test on the district's order, at eleven hundred, for an hour and ten minutes, with a licensed engineer present and the paper the district's, and it is a true number, and it is the only true number anybody in this district has got, and it does not account for a gallon of the eighty a day. It never was going to. A test at the head measures the head. The panel on the twelfth said the mouth has more than one source in it and would not say which, and I entered that as a contact and not as a finding, and the panel is in no book, and this test is the same thing in a different instrument: a number about a mouth and not a number about a place."

Renner said the only thing a licensed man says at the end of a test, which is his figures, and they were the same as the ones he had put in his own book at a pump house in Fennmere six years ago, and he initialled the paper.

Then the officer's clerk put a hand on the case and took out a single printed sheet, and asked whether the holding would be informed of the third condition in clause three before the examination closed.

Nobody in that room had said the word condition out loud in five weeks.

"It is not to be described before the examination at which it is tested," the clerk said, reading. "I have it in a line of type and I am to read the line and not explain it."

"Then this is the examination at which it is tested," said Marek.

"No, sir," said the clerk. "The test of that condition falls at the first examination held after the twenty-ninth of the month after next."

The room was quiet, and the pump was on the night setting, and the lamp above the seed-house table was burning on its living bulb, which Lissa Vale had fed that morning and would feed that night, because the bulb has to be fed or it dies.

"Then name it now," said Marek, "because the district is in this room and this holding has asked three times and the second condition is a return main to specification and the first is four hundred hundredweight of compost a month and neither of them has ever been described either, and I have got a clause I cannot read and cannot comply with and I have had it in a stranger's notebook two hundred miles from here since the twenty-second of last month."

"Mr Vale, I am a clerk and I have a line of type and not a discretion," said the clerk, with the manner of a man who was not enjoying himself and was not pretending to. "The first two are not described because they are on the face of the clause. The third is not on the face of the clause."

"Can the clerk say whether it is a testable condition at all," said Anneke Roos, from the doorway, in the voice she used when she was not going to say it again.

The clerk looked at the officer, who was not in the room, because Hilary Skeath had not come up the road herself and had sent the paper and a man with a form case, and who had said on the twenty-fourth that a node with an untestable condition in it was a node she had to defend at every renewal, which had sounded like an act of kindness at the time and now did not.

"The clerk can say what the form says," said the clerk. "The form says: the third condition is tested at an examination, and no condition is tested twice in the same term, and the term ends with the charter."

Gael Senn had her pen down. "Write that down and read it back to me and then say the other half of it, because you have brought me a form and I am the examiner of this node and I am going to ask you the same question in my own words. If the condition is not tested before the term ends, is it tested at all?"

"Write what the form says, Miss Senn, and I will read it back."

Gael Senn wrote what the form said and read it back. Then she wrote a second line under it and read that back too, and it was this: *no condition is tested twice in the same term, and the term ends with the charter, and the examination at which this condition is tested is the first examination held after the term ends.*

The clerk did not stop her.

"And so nobody in this room will ever be told what it is," said Marek. "And the man who holds this node on that day will be told, and he will be told it in a room with a district officer in it, on a form, at an examination, and it will be a test he has had no notice of and no season to make ready, and every one of us in this yard will have handed it to him in writing without knowing what it was."

"That is the form," said the clerk. "I am not permitted to say anything else and I have been in the room for forty minutes and I would like to go home, sir, and I do not think there is anything in this room that makes that a hard request."

Nobody said the word. Dunmar Fitch wrote down the hour, and the clerk's name, and the form, and the two lines, and the fact that the clerk had asked to go home and had not been refused, and read it all back, and the reader was the clerk, who initialed what he had heard and nothing else, which is the practice now and which is the only reason the man from Marrowfield, standing at the top of the steps with his clipboard under his arm, was permitted to hear a form read in a fen.

Renner walked to the gate with Marek after and stood in the gateway in the cold, and neither of them spoke for a while.

"You have been up since the fourth of last month," Marek said at last. "I have been counting your days in my own book since the eighth, and you have been in this yard four times in six weeks and you have refused me a form twice and sold me a collar once, and I have never once asked you whether it was worth it to you."

"It is worth four hundred hundredweight a month to somebody," said Coll Renner. "It is not worth it to me. I have nine men and a yard and I do not take work I cannot pay for, and that is not a virtue, it is arithmetic." He shifted his case. "What is worth it to me is that in this district there is now one holding whose books a stranger can read, and there are four licensed men in it, and a thing that works has been in a trade article since the eighth of last month, and I would rather the two of those were in the same county than the same quarterly."

At the gate the pump came up for the afternoon and the drains went silver and the drains had been silver for a week and the fen was eight days past a date it had worked out for itself and had not been able to check, and the charter was twenty-nine days from ending, and nobody in that yard said either of those numbers out loud except the clerk, who had said one of them in a form.

The paper went down the fen road in a form case at midday, and it was the district's, and the fen was not to have a copy, and Marek had offered in front of the officer on the twenty-fourth of last month not to have the first copy and had meant it, and the district had not been interested in the offer, and there was one number in a file in Fennmere now that had been checked by somebody who was not in this fen and would be read at the next renewal, and the fen could not see it and could not take it back.
