# Chapter 69

## Four Seconds

The cart from Fennmere came up the fen road on the twenty-fifth, a Sunday, at about the eleventh hour, with a parcel the size of a fist and a note in a foreman's hand and a second note inside it in Coll Renner's own.

It was a copper collar. Turned, with a bead of living rootwood set in it, the wood about the size of a hazel nut, bedded in damp sand in a flat tin, and it was not scarroot and it was not an anchor and it was not from this holding.

Renner's first note said the parcel had been at the Fennmere gate since the previous Thursday. His second note was the one that mattered and it was four sentences long and not one of them was about the device.

"I did not make this. I am selling one I cut in the spring of this year and I have four left and I am not selling the four. If you want one to go up that road faster than a Tuesday cart, that is money, and I will say no, and I would rather say no in writing on a Sunday than in a yard on a Wednesday. You bought a collar from a man's yard. You did not buy a method and I have not given you one."

Lissa Vale read both notes out in the yard before she opened the tin, because the body had voted on the twentieth of last month that a person reads an entry before it is initialled, and she was the reader, and she read her own two shillings out as well.

The seed house tin held eleven shillings. The collar cost two. That left nine, and the nine were the seed house's float for a month in which a bag of lamp oil was not going to be bought, and Lissa Vale said so in one sentence and did not soften it and did not ask anybody. Dunmar Fitch entered it with the hour and read it back, with the second note copied into the book word for word, including *I will say no*.

"That is seven lines of use log on a Tuesday," Marek said. "Or six. Whatever it is, it is a line, and it is read back, and the licensed man is not here."

"He is not needed," said Lissa Vale. "The collar is not the instrument. The collar is a bead of living wood and a ring of copper, and a wet cloth is not a contact and a seed lot is not a node. You put the collar on a lot and the lot speaks. I have written six lines of contacts and a man of fifty wrote a line about a rootmark and I am not going to invent a seventh kind of line because somebody has sold us a thing in a fist-sized tin."

She said it to Marek in front of five people and then she read the parcel back into the book as received, and the collar went on the middle of the seed-house table beside the fieldbook and the use log and the Ashcombe index that had been lying there since the seventh of last month with its two hundred pages open at a column that did not name this fen.

The press stood open behind them with the March's tin under the ledger, unmoved, sixty-six days at risk on the twenty-fifth. The bed stood at three and a half degrees. The pump ran. The leak passed into the housing at five-eighths of an inch, twice measured at the seventh hour with two rules and the second rule the one that reads smaller, and the pit held four feet of iron water and was not up a quarter of an inch.

Tova Reed had been at the counting-house end of the table since the cart came, with her case locked and the key on her belt, and she did not say a word about the collar for forty minutes.

Then she put four lots on the table and she put them down in the order she wanted them read in, which was a thing she had never done before and which she explained while she did it.

"I have kept seed for eleven years," she said, "and I have not kept four lots. I have kept forty. I have brought four, and the four are here so that nobody can say afterward that I chose a lot that suited me. The first is ours. The second is the March's. The third is the same pot as the second and it is the graft. The fourth I am not reading today and I will say why when we get to it."

The first was a bean out of the fen's own saving, a small gray lot in a paper twist, out of the seed house's own tin, grown in the fen's own ground.

Lissa Vale wetted a corner of the twist and shook the grains onto the flat of a clean board and laid the collar on them.

It went faintly green at the wood. Marek felt it come up through the copper into his own fingers, where the metal was, and what he felt was not a field and not a direction and not a warmth with a length to it, the way it had been on the thirtieth of last month. It was four seconds long and it was dull and it was small, the way a hand is small.

"Here," said Tova Reed. "This season. After a ley. That is our bean and it is our ground and it is correct, and I want it entered that it is correct, because a book that only holds the surprises is not a book."

"It is entered," said Dunmar Fitch, who had the pen down already.

"And it is useless," said Marek.

"It is not useless," said Tova Reed. "It is exact. Say what you actually mean, Mr Vale, because you are about to say something you have not measured." She looked at the board for a moment. "It cannot tell this field from this fen. That is what you mean. A device that certifies provenance and cannot tell a field from a holding is a device that certifies a holding. That is a market for forty-one lines and not for four hundred acres, and the forty-one lines are the ones the index already wanted."

The second lot was the March's wet-line rye, taken from the flowerpot that had gone into the ground against the drill on the thirtieth of last month, from the ungrafted part of it, from the side of the pot where nothing had been joined to anything.

The collar went on and it went green and Marek felt four seconds of a different shape.

"Salt March," said Tova Reed. "The wet ground above the second sluice. Last season's sowing, and the ground was under a ley the season before, and the water was high in the spring. That is the March and that is our line and that is the lot I sent north in four crates in the spring, and it is the first time in eleven years that anything has told me the truth about a rye in four seconds."

"Enter it," said Dunmar Fitch, and entered it, and read it back, and the reader was Ilsa Kern, who had come in from the embankment with a bar in her hand and had stood at the end of the table for the whole of it and had not said anything.

The third lot was the grafted seedling. It was the same rye, out of the same pot, from the side of it where Marek had put a cut of a cutting from the field bed on the thirtieth of last month at about the fifth hour with a tie of green withy and Coll Renner's clip and a licensed man's name on the record.

Lissa Vale wet it and laid the collar on it.

The collar went green.

And nothing came through the copper that had a place in it.

Marek took his hand off the board and stood there. It was four seconds and it was the same four seconds, and what came up his fingers was not a field and not a season and not a ley. It was a length of time in which somebody had done five things in a fixed order, and each of the five things had been a choice, and the device gave him all five of them without giving him the ground any of them were standing on.

"The thirtieth of last month," he said. "The fifth hour. The north annex. A cut out of a pot and a cut out of the field bed. A tie of green withy, about nine inches. And a decision to keep one of two and throw the other away."

He said it slowly, the way a man reads a dial, and then he stopped and did not say the last thing, and the room waited for it.

"Whose," said Tova Reed.

"I do not know," said Marek. "It does not have a name in it. It has an order in it. It is not a name and it is not a place. It is a person doing arithmetic with a bad spring, and the arithmetic is still in there, and I felt it and I cannot tell you the sum."

Lissa Vale had not moved. She was looking at the board and not at the seedling.

"The ninth tray," she said. "In the sowing of the second year back. Forty-one trays and the ninth had a black bend in the stem about four inches up where it had gone over sideways in the wet, and it was the worst tray of the forty-one, and it was the only one of the forty-one that had a clean root on it, and I put it against the drill and I kept it because of the root and I said nothing about it to anybody because I did not want a conversation about a bad tray."

She looked up then.

"That is the worst tray I ever kept," she said, "and I kept it for one reason, and the reason is not a matter for a collar."

The pump ran. The yard was very quiet and the lamp above the seed-house table hummed because it burns on a living bulb and the bulb has to be fed or it dies, and it was fed that morning with water and a scoop of matter, and it was warm in a cold room.

Dunmar Fitch had his pen down and he had not written anything, and he asked the question that is the whole of his trade.

"May it be entered."

"It may not," said Lissa Vale.

"Lissa."

"It may not," she said, "and I would like it entered that I have said no, and that the no is mine, and that it is the first thing this holding has declined to enter since the book was opened, and I would like the clerk to write that down in my words and not in his."

"It is a bead of living wood in a ring of copper and it cannot be checked," she said. "There is nothing in it a man with a rule can look at twice and get the same answer, and a line in a book that cannot be checked is a line somebody can use, and I have watched a woman in a market be indexed twice and I am not going to be the reason there is a third time."

Dunmar Fitch read that back before he initialled it, and Gael Senn read it back after him because the vote of the twentieth says a person reads and a person is anybody who comes and a clerk is a person, and then it stood in the book with her name against it and not the thing's, which is the same shape the examiner had put on the ninth logging of the beat four days earlier and which nobody in that yard had ever thought to copy from anybody.

What went in the book was the fact of the test and the hour and the number of lots and the two results that were places, read back and initialled in the ordinary way, and the third result was not in it, and the reason was in it, and the reason was nine lines long and belonged to the woman who had said it.

Tova Reed had not written anything at all. She had her pencil down and both hands flat on the table in front of her, and when the room had finished she picked the pencil up and put it in her case and said the thing that was the whole of the twenty-fifth, in a level voice, looking at the board and not at anybody.

"It does not report a place," she said. "It reports the last hand. And a hand is a person, and a person is the only thing in this trade that has never been for sale, and that thing has just sold Lissa Vale's worst tray in four seconds, at this table, in front of six of us, and it did not ask her, and it cannot be asked, and the tin of the thing is standing on my table at two shillings and there are nine shillings left in the seed house."

At the counting-house end she took the parcel note and squared it to the edge of the table the way a licensed engineer had squared nine pages once.

"Then I am going to tell you about the fourth lot and I am going to tell you now, before anybody sends for it, because if I wait until we have read it in a room with the lamp on I will have arranged the room." She put her hand flat on the locked case. "The fourth lot is the last of the March's dry-land rye. Four winters of a line that has failed every year since the year I was loaned here. I have kept the seed of it in a case, nine hundred miles away, in a counting house, in a tin, because a steward keeps the seed of a line she cannot grow. It is in this room, in that case, and it is worth nothing whatever, and I have carried it nine hundred miles because I could not leave it in a drawer."

She put her hand off the case.

"I have never had it read," she said, "and I am not having it read in a yard in daylight, and I will tell you all of it and you may make of it what you can make of it. We will not read it this week. We will read it on the third of next month, in this room, at the ninth hour, and I have said the date, and I have said it because I have found out twice this month what a date costs."

Nobody asked her why. It was near three in the afternoon and the pump ran and the ordinary work went on round the table, because it always went on round the table, and at the fourth hour the collar was bedded in its damp sand and the tin was put at the back of the shelf behind the seed house ledger where Lissa Vale kept the things she would not leave on a table, and the note from Fennmere was copied into the book entire, and the collar was entered as received, and the entry was read aloud, and a person read it, and the person was Jonas Byre, who had come in off the culvert and had not been in the room for the last hour and who read it in a slow careful voice because that is how he reads anything.
