# Chapter 92

## The Refusal Clause

The fen worked it out in the yard on the sixteenth, standing, in about an hour and a half, and there were nine of them there and the argument went in three parts and each part was found by a different person, and the order they were found in is the reason the clause is the shape it is.

The first part was what the clause is for, and Marek Vale gave it in the morning, and it is nine words and it went in the book as his.

"A condition needs a no," he said, "and there is no no."

The second part was who may say the no, and that took until about the second hour, and it was Lissa Vale, and what she said was: "Not a person in the book. There is no person in the book. There are eight names in this holding and eleven households at the other end and a keeper who has never signed a paper in her life and has told us so to our faces, and if I write *the holder of the schedule at the second end* then I have invented a body that does not exist and the clause will be about a person who is not there, which is a clause about a ghost, and ghosts cannot say no, and that is the exact opposite of what we want."

And then Jonas Byre found the third part, and it is the shortest thing anybody said all week.

"Then write it about anybody," he said. "Anybody. A person standing at that fence with a hoe."

The clerk entered that, and entered who said it, and the reader for it was the man of about fifty, and he read the three of them back in order with the hour on each and Lissa Vale read the second one twice because it is the ninth line of something.

Marek Vale drafted it that evening at the north end of the seed house bench and it took him about an hour and it is good work and it is wrong, and he has entered that it is wrong in his own hand, and the wrongness is in this chapter in full.

> *A use of the seed-route schedule at Lantern Reach is a carriage of seed, in crates, over ground the district carries in schedule, at the rate set out in the terms of class 4. No person may make a use of this route except as provided in those terms. Any person in occupation of the second end may refuse a use of this route by writing in the book. A refusal requires no reason. The route is not used while a refusal stands.*

He brought it to the seed house door on the morning of the seventeenth and the composting bay and left it there over lunch, and Tova Reed read it standing up, and she found the fault in about a minute and a half, and she found it in the steward's way, which is that she asked one question about who loses.

"The first sentence," she said. "You have written down what a use is."

"It is a carriage of seed, in crates, which is what the route carries, which is on the second part of their own paper in their own words—"

"It is on their own paper in their own words and it is the wrong thing to have written in mine, and here is why, and it is the whole of it." She put her finger on the nail hole above it. "There are now two documents in this district that say what a use is. One of them has a number at the head of it and a number at the foot of it and a seal and a schedule of a class of use in nine miles, and one of them is a piece of yard paper with my name under it. Where those two disagree, the one with the number is the definition and the one without is an opinion. And we have just given them a door.

"Because here is what happens. Somebody puts a cart of stone on this road in the spring, which I am not saying they will and I am not saying they will not, and it is not a carriage of seed in crates, and so it is not a use within the meaning of our clause, because our clause has said what a use is and it is not that. And then a piece of paper we wrote ourselves comes back and tells us we had no power to say no, and it is our paper, and we wrote it, and we cannot complain about a word we put there in our own hand in a yard in daylight. You have not written a clause, Mr Vale. You have written a defence, and handed it to them with a door in it, and the door is the only part of this anybody will ever remember."

Nobody said anything for a moment, and then Marek Vale took the pencil and struck the first sentence out, and he did it from the first word to the last, and he did not scratch it, and Tova Reed looked at the strike and then at him and said nothing, because on the twenty-second of a month two months back she had struck a clause in one line in a yard and had said nothing either, and both of them knew the other had seen it and neither of them mentioned it, and the clerk entered that the first sentence was struck out and by whom and at what hour and did not enter why, because the why was in the steward's own words thirty seconds earlier and it was in the book.

And then Lissa Vale found the last thing, and she found it standing in the yard with the paper in her hand, and it is the only thing in this batch that nobody had thought of and everybody agreed with.

"You have written it to be read," she said. "Read. Every sentence of it is a thing on a page that a person has to hold and look at. And the only person this clause will ever be said to is standing nine miles off in a place where nobody has ever been handed a form, and she is not going to read this, and nobody in this yard is going to hand it to her, because she has refused two pieces of paper from this holding inside a month and was right twice, and the third thing that went out of here unasked came back in a coat pocket."

"So it has to be sayable," she said. "Short sentences. Nothing that has to be turned over. Nothing that sounds like a document when a person says it out loud, because the moment it sounds like a document she is going to hear an office in it and she is going to say no on principle, and she will be right to, and we will have wasted the only instrument we have got in this volume."

She read the struck draft out loud in the yard, and it took nineteen seconds, and then she said: "That is a document. Now say it to me as though I were standing at a fence and had asked you what it was for."

---

What went on the sheet on the seventeenth is this, and it is in the fen's own hand, and it is the whole of it.

> *A person in occupation at either end of this route may refuse a use of it in the ordinary way, by saying so to a person carrying it out. If it is asked for twice, the person refusing says so again. No reason is required. None is to be entered. A refusal holds from the hour it is said. There is no appeal from it and no office in this district can be asked to say otherwise. This sheet names nobody. Nobody is required to sign it. It is read aloud to anybody who asks to hear it, and a copy of it may be taken by anybody, including the blank.*

Ilsa Kern read it out from the north gate where the boards are, to make a person read it standing at a distance, and the man of about seventy repeated the whole of it back from where he was sitting against the well house wall, and he got one sentence wrong and put his hand up and said so before anybody asked him to, and the sentence he got wrong was the one about a copy, and what he said was: *and a copy of it may be taken by anybody, including a man who cannot write.*

Lissa Vale entered that as the correct reading and the clerk entered that a man of about seventy read the clause back from eleven feet and got a sentence wrong and corrected himself before anybody spoke, and the reader for that entry was the man of about fifty.

And the sheet went onto the same sheet as the schedule, under the seven columns, in the fen's own hand, and the schedule came off the well house door at about the second hour of the afternoon and went back on it at about the third, on the same four nails and in the same four holes, and the holes show, and the man of about seventy noticed the holes before anybody said anything about them and said: "It has been on that door once. That is worth telling in a book, because a thing that has been up twice looks like a thing that was put up wrong once and taken down."

And the two boards over the bay had seventy-one days cut into both of them, and Ilsa Kern said the number out loud on her way past and did not stop, and the number is still going up.

---

Then they priced it, in the yard, standing, on the seventeenth, and it took about ten minutes and it is the part of the batch that Marek Vale has entered in his own hand as a fault he found and did not fix.

"It cannot be tested," he said. "There is no object in it. Every clause I have ever written has an object and you can put the object in front of the clause and the clause either bites or it does not. This one has no object. Nobody can ever test whether it has been kept. It is the first thing I have written in eleven months that cannot be checked by a man with a rule, and I have spent this whole season putting things in books so that they can be checked, and I am going to write this on a door anyway.

"And here is why, and put it in the book because it is the reason and not the sentiment. A clause that can be tested is a clause somebody can be held to. The Crown can test the second part of that office's terms, and they can find out whether we kept thirty crates, and we kept six, and they can do something about it. A clause nobody can test binds the body that wrote it, and cannot be got at by anybody else, and cannot be waived, and cannot be taxed, and cannot be defeated by a clause in a schedule of a class of use with a number at the head of it, because the district is not a party to it and is not named in it and has no standing to be. So the whole of it is that I have written down a thing that cannot be enforced against anybody, and I have put it on a public door, and a promise a body makes to itself in public is the one kind of promise it can never get out of, because somebody has read it and the somebody is anybody."

"And here is what it costs," he said, "and I would like it written as a price and not as a speech. From the hour that sheet is on that door, any one of the eleven households, or any person standing in any of the eleven plots, or any child, can stop this route by saying so, at the fence, with a hoe in their hand. There is no notice. There is no reason. There is no appeal and no office that can be asked to say otherwise, and I have written that in myself on purpose, and I have written it in because if I leave that last part out then somebody in a bad month in three years will find a way round it and the clause will be a thing with a handle on it."

"So the road can be ended by a person nobody in this yard has met, at any hour, for no reason, forever, and there is not one thing this holding can do about it, and I have spent eleven months trying to get this holding to have a say in what happens on that road, and I have just given it away on purpose to a person I have met once, and she was right about both the things she refused and I have entered her reason without her terms and I am not going to be thanked for it because I have not done it for her."

And then somebody asked the obvious question in the wrong voice, which was the man of about fifty, who wanted to know who was going to take a copy of it to the second end, and the yard went quiet for the second time that afternoon.

"A copy of the sheet is the thing that goes on the post," said Lissa Vale, "and the sheet is the schedule, and there is already a copy of the schedule on that post, and the clause is on the sheet. So the copy goes on the post. And the answer to that is that nobody is going to take it, and the reason is not the rule about collecting, though the rule is right. The reason is that this holding promised a woman who cannot read a form that nobody would put her name at the top of a thing, and she has refused two pieces of paper inside a month and been right twice, and a third document going out of this yard to her would be a third time, and it would not be a kindness, it would be the fen tidying up after itself."

"So it goes on the post when a person goes," said Dunmar Fitch, "and not before, and nobody is appointed to take it and nobody has been told there is a copy, and I am entering all three of those and I am entering that I have entered them without being asked by anybody, which is the standing of this clerk and which Lissa Vale has told me is the only power I have."

Anneke Roos initialled it as an opinion in four words, and the vote was not taken, because there is no charter and a vote needs a motion, and the fen enters that the clause went on the door without a vote and that the standing of this holding is that anything this body does does not require a vote and that a vote is what you take when you are not sure, and Lissa Vale read all of it aloud in the yard at the fourth hour and a person read it back after her and the person was the man of about seventy.

Underneath the day's work, dated the seventeenth, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, is the nineteenth note under section nine of the charter schedule: a draft of a clause, struck out at its first sentence by the steward of the trust; a clause with no object in it, which cannot be tested, written for that reason; a person in occupation at either end, and anybody and nobody, and no name; no reason required and none entered; no appeal; a reading at a distance by a man of about seventy with a sentence wrong and a correction made before anybody spoke; a price in a body giving away a say; and a schedule taken off a door and put back on the same four nails in the same four holes, and the holes showing.

*An argument. This is worth nothing against a cut.*
