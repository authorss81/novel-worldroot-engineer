# Chapter 93

## The First Toll

Gael Senn measured the leak at the seventh hour on the eighteenth, twice, with two rules, the second the one that reads smaller on that corner, pump on, into the housing and not into the pit, and it was five-eighths of an inch, and the pit was not up a quarter of an inch, and she wrote it in the ordinary words and read it back, and the bed was at three and a half degrees, and she said the number twice.

And at about a quarter past eight, when the fen's cart went out of the gate with two men and a load of chaff and a sack of something bought with a shilling, a man came up behind it with a book under his arm and put a hand on the tailboard and said the word the fen had been reading on a door for nine days.

"Fourpence the crate," he said. "Second part. In the ordinary course and at the point of use, and the point of use is your gate because that is where the route begins, and I am appointed for the purpose and here is my name and here is the office, and I am not doing anything but the ordinary thing and I have done it four times this month in this district."

He said his name and it was Hedd Marlow, and Dunmar Fitch entered it and the hour and read it back to him, and Coll Renner was in the yard because he had come up the road at seven with a bar over his shoulder and had been watching the leak being measured with the patience of a man who does not say anything in front of a woman holding a rule.

The chaff went on to Fennmere. The cart came back at about the fifth hour and was stopped at the gate again, and Marlow wrote something in his book with a piece of chalk he had to hold in his teeth, and then he put the book flat on the tailboard and turned it round so that anybody standing at the gate could read it, which is not a thing an appointed man has to do and which is the reason the fen liked him by the end.

"It is the month," he said. "It is not the load and it is not the crossing and I have written two crossings in this column because two carts went out and two came in, and there is no column for a crossing because the rate is not on a crossing. The rate is on the capacity of the route as you declared it, and you declared thirty crates a month, and the rate is fourpence, and thirty times four is one shilling. So it is one shilling for the month and I am not going to ask you for a second one on the way back, and if anybody in this district tells you that a rate is charged twice on a road it went out on and came back on, they are quoting a document and not this one."

"One shilling," said Lissa Vale, from the step, and she had it in her hand before he finished the sentence, and she counted it out on the flat of the board step in front of two people, which were the man of about fifty and the woman with the shilling, because that is what the fen does with money and has done since a levy went to four times.

"One shilling, out of the tin, on the eighteenth of this month, at the eighth hour, given to Hedd Marlow of the district water office, appointed for the purpose, at the point of use," said Lissa Vale. "Read it back."

Marlow read it back, which nobody had asked him to do, and then he gave her a receipt.

It was a slip about the length of his hand, and it had a number of four digits at the head and a number of six at the foot and a printed box in the middle headed for the district holding number, and the district's name on it, and the date, and the words *rate paid, class 4*, and the amount, and a place for a signature.

"I am not permitted to sign on your behalf," he said, "and I am not going to leave a receipt unsigned, because a receipt nobody signed is a piece of paper, and a piece of paper a clerk can be asked for is worse than no receipt at all. There is a box. I have a use number for the node in my own book and it is not a holding number and I am going to write it in a box headed for a holding number, because that is what the office gave me, and I would like you to have the receipt with my number in it in my own hand rather than a hole."

So they put the use number in the box, and the second time a form in this district arrived with a hole in it and went back with something in it, and the difference was that this time a man had offered to make the hole himself.

And then the man of about fifty said the thing that everybody in that yard had been thinking since the ninth hour, and he said it to the gate and not to Marlow, and Marlow heard it and did not pretend not to.

"That slip goes on the door," he said. "Under the notice. Because in four years somebody is going to come to that door and read a class of use with a number on it and a schedule with a number under it, and there is going to be nothing on that door to say what this holding has actually paid, and the day that door has a receipt on it nobody has to write to anybody to find out."

"No," said Anneke Roos.

The yard looked at her.

"A number on the door makes the door a form," she said. "That is the whole of it and I have said it twice in eleven months in this yard and I am not going to soften it for a man who is right about the fact and wrong about the shelf. We put a schedule on that door in the middle of last month with no number on it, and we did it on purpose, and the reason we did it on purpose is that a form with a number on it is a form somebody can get a second copy of, and a second copy has to be copied honestly or it is a forgery and not a document. The door is the only thing in this district that belongs to the people who stop at it. Put a number on it and it stops being that and becomes a thing an office has an interest in, and the office will want it back, and then it comes off, and then a man is standing at a gate with nothing at all."

"Then the fact that we paid it goes where," said the man of about fifty.

"In the book, in the ledger, and on the step," said Anneke Roos. "Where everything else we have ever paid goes. And I will tell you what the difference is between a receipt on a door and a receipt in a book, and I have thought about it for about ten seconds. A receipt on a door is for anybody who comes. A receipt in a book is for anybody who asks. And we decided in a yard on the twentieth of a month two months back that the reader is a person and a person is anybody who comes, and that is a stronger promise than the one a piece of paper on a door makes, because on the door a man has to be able to read, and in this yard he does not."

Marlow said, "That is the first time anybody has given me a reason for anything in nine years and it is not a bad one," and the clerk entered that a person appointed to collect a rate in this district said a thing about this holding's book and that the clerk entered the words and not the opinion, which is the clerk's whole trade.

And then, because he was a man doing a job and not a man making a point, he said the other half of it before he went, and it is the reason this holding did not sleep much on the eighteenth.

"Can I say one thing that is in the fourth part and not in my own head," he said. "The rate is collected at the point of use. A point of use is wherever a cart goes out. Today there was one cart and there was one of me. If your route carries thirty crates a month there is one of me. If it carries five hundred there are five of me on this road, and not one of them is anybody, and I am appointed for the purpose and so is the next one, and neither of us knows the other and neither of us is paid by you. That is the ordinary way and I am not telling you it to frighten you. I am telling you because in nine years I have never once collected a rate off a body that had read the fourth part, and I would like somebody at this gate to have read it."

"Enter that," said Dunmar Fitch, "all of it, and the hour, and put in that he offered it and that nobody asked him for it and that this holding has entered the number of men on a road in an ordinary course as a fact and not as a threat, because it is not a threat and because a body that enters what it is afraid of has started keeping records for somebody else."

And the tin was eight shillings after it, and Lissa Vale said the eight out loud on the step, and then said the nine, because she had said the nine on the twelfth and the two figures had to be in the same breath in a book anybody may read, and the fen entered both with the hour and the reason, which is that a reader who is given one number and not the other cannot look at it twice and get the same answer.

And then the clerk entered the thing that the whole of the eighteenth was for, and entered it in the plainest words anybody has used in this book this season, and read it back twice.

*This is the first money this holding has ever handed to a district office for a thing it did not ask to be charged for. The levy was asked for and the shilling a copy was asked for and the two pounds a month was asked for and every one of those was somebody coming into this yard and being given a price in a room. Nobody came into this yard. A form came, on a cart, with a number at the head of it, and the price was on the form, and a man collected it at a gate and did his job and was polite and was right, and there is no person in that transaction to be angry at, and that is the whole of what is new about a toll and it is not the money.*

The fen entered the toll in two books. The ledger: the amount, the hour, the appointed man by name, two crossings and one month, one shilling, and the eightpence difference again, entered on its own line, not claimed. And the fen's own book: *a toll was collected on this holding at the point of use on the eighteenth of this month by a person appointed for the purpose, and it was paid out of the seed house tin, counted out on the step in front of two people and read aloud before it was entered, and it is entered as a toll and is not a bill and is not a debt, and the person appointed is not a creditor of this body and is not owed anything at the end of the month and is owed nothing at all.*

And Dunmar Fitch entered the last line of it, and entered why, and it is the whole of what happened this month in one sentence.

"Since the ninth of this month there is a rate, a class of use and a standing, and there is a person in a district's book whose job it is to ask for the standing in the ordinary course, and this holding has lost the last thing it could have kept by not having it, which is that nothing in this arrangement can be served on anybody."

Renner read that back without comment, and Marlow had gone by then, and the sun was off the door, and the man of about fifty was looking at the seven columns with the smaller figure under the larger one and the clause underneath them in the fen's own hand, and the count of the readings of the seventh column was nineteen under the form, and there is one line in that column in the life of the sheet.

"Mr Renner," said the man of about fifty. "You have been standing in that yard for two days with a bar over your shoulder and you have not said one word about the rate, and I have watched you not say it, and I would like to know what a bar over the shoulder is for, because it is not for digging, we have not got a ditch to dig, and it is not for carrying, because we are not carrying anything."

"It is for a cart," said Coll Renner.

He put the bar down against the wall of the well house and stood in front of them the way he stands in front of a thing he has thought about for a long time, and he gave his name and the hour, and it went in, and it is here entire.

"There is a use of that road I have wanted to put to you since I read the second part of that office's terms, and I have not put it, and I want it in the book that I have not put it for nine days and why. Not because I was waiting for permission. Because before Tuesday you were two ends of a road with no standing, and a man who asks a body with no standing for a favor is a man asking, and a body with no standing can only say yes or no and it cannot be held to either. You are an operator now. Operators have terms. And I have a thing I would like a term for, and I have been waiting nine days for a term, and there it is, and I will put it in on the twentieth, and not before, because tomorrow is the reading of the door and I do not put anything in front of a reading."

"And I will say the one half of it tonight so that nobody in this yard has to sleep on it," he said. "It is not seed. And I am not going to use your return column, because that column has one sowing in it and I would not put a cart in it, and there is a man in this yard who would not let me and he would be right. So I am going to ask you for the road and leave your column alone, and I would like somebody to write that down, because in about four days every one of you is going to think I said the other thing."
