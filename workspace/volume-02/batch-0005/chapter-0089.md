# Chapter 89

## A Class Of Use

The district's own cart came up the fen road on the tenth at about the ninth hour, and it was the same cart that brings the levy paper and has brought it four times this year, and the man on the tailboard was a clerk of the water office of about twenty-five with a case and a bundle of four sheets tied with a string, and he put his name on the delivery and did not put a house to it, and Dunmar Fitch entered the name and the hour and read them back to him at the gate.

Tova Reed took the four sheets off the tailboard and read the whole of them aloud in the yard before she did anything else with them, which is what this fen does with a document, and Lissa Vale read them back, and by the time the second reading was finished there were eleven people in that yard, which is more than there have been on a working morning since the fourth of a month four months back.

They were correct. That was the first thing, and it went in first, and Anneke Roos asked for it to go in first before anything else was said, and it went in first.

*Water-supply bylaw, second part. Schedule of terms. Class of use 4: the carriage of seed, in crates, over ground the district carries in schedule, between one keeper and another keeper, on a route whose first end is at a node entered in the district's book. Four parts.*

*One. This schedule is sent under the third point of the letter of the ninth of this month. It is not an approval of any route, is not a recognition of any holder, and does not make anybody a party to anything. It states the class to which a use of ground belongs and the rate at which that class is rated.*

*Two. Fourpence the crate. Reckoned monthly, on the capacity of the route as declared by the operator. This office has no power to measure the capacity of a route and has no officer appointed to measure it, and the declared figure is used because it is the only figure this office can act on.*

*Three. A use of ground in district schedule is conditional. In this class the condition is that the operator keeps the declared capacity in the ordinary course, and produces the record of the route to an officer of the district on request. The operator is the person in occupation of the node at the first end.*

*Four. The rate is collected by a person appointed for the purpose. It may be collected at the point of use or at the office, and the operator may pay at either.*

*Entered at the foot: second part, class 4, schedule of terms. A number at the head of the sheet of four digits, and a number at the foot of six, and a box headed for the district holding number.*

Nobody in that yard said anything for a while.

"Point two," said Anneke Roos, from the doorway, and she came further in than she usually does. "Fourpence the crate, on the figure on the door, and the office has no power to measure it, so it uses the figure the operator put on the door. And the figure on the door is thirty." She put her hand flat on the table, which she has done four times this season. "That is not a rate on what we carry. That is a rate on what we said we would carry, and we said it before we measured it, and nobody in this yard can now have the figure on the door changed without asking that office to change a class of use, and I would like somebody in this yard to sit with how that feels for about a minute before we start being clever about it."

They sat with it for about a minute, and the pump ran, and somebody's cart went past the gate and did not stop, which is ordinary and was not entered.

Then the man of about seventy, who had come and had stayed, said the thing that made the rest of the morning possible.

"The notice is on the left of that door," he said. "And the schedule is on the right of it. And if this one goes up, it goes on the left as well, under the notice, and it goes up whole, all four parts, and nobody reads part two on a door and thinks they have read it. A thing with a number on it that is worth fourpence a crate is exactly the sort of thing a man stops at and reads, and I have never yet known a man to read the bottom of a sheet."

So the four sheets were nailed up on the well house door on the left, under the notice of the second, on the tenth, at about the eleventh hour, and the door carries three things now and will carry four, and the fourth thing is not written yet, and Anneke Roos entered that the door was counted before and after so that a person in four years could see what had been added to a public door in a season and could not be told it had always been there.

And then Lissa Vale did the thing she does, and this time she did not say it.

She read the whole of the four parts back, and the reading was checked, and every figure in it was a figure a man with a rule could look at twice and get the same answer, and she initialled it, and she added nothing to it and she said nothing at all about having added nothing.

"You did not say it," said Tova Reed.

"I have said it three times this season," said Lissa Vale, "and you are all still here, and it has not stopped being true, and I have begun to think that a thing a person says three times in one season is a habit, and a habit is a thing an office can make a form of. So this time I am going to not say it and let the page stand and let anybody who wants to look at it look at it. If it is wrong somebody will be able to show me, and if it is right it will still be right, and a rule that has to be repeated to stay in force is a rule that is being kept alive by the woman saying it and not by the page." She squared the sheets against the edge of the door. "Enter that, and put the hour on it, and put my name on it, because I have just decided something in a yard and it ought to be findable."

It went in, and the reader was Jonas Byre, and he read it back including the reason.

---

The rest of the tenth was about the box.

The schedule of terms has a box headed for the district holding number, and it is six digits with a printed head, and this holding does not have one, and Anneke Roos said the sentence that a man at a counter nine miles off had said about a different form with the same box in it, and she said she was not going to pretend she had thought of it herself.

"A form with a box that cannot be filled in is a piece of paper with a hole in it," she said, "and Perrin Osse said that to me at a counter and I have carried it about ever since. There is a number in the district's own book for this node and the district put it on its own letter and we have it, and the number is a use number and not a holding number, and I do not know what a use number is and neither do you, Mr Vale, and I am not going to stand here and find out by guessing. So the box gets the use number, and the clerk enters that we put the use number in a box headed for a holding number, and the district may send us a form we cannot fill in and we will send it back with a note and we will do that as many times as it takes and we will not pay anything for the privilege."

Nobody argued with her about the box, and the fen has no vote on a box, and the fen entered that as well.

And then Marek Vale asked the only question in the four parts that had not been answered in the yard, and he asked it of the yard and not of the clerk, who was gone an hour before he thought of it, which the clerk entered as a fact about the order of a morning.

"Point two," he said. "It says the declared figure. The declared figure is on the door, thirty crates a month, and the measured figure is under it, six crates a month and none back, and both are in the same hand and the same ink and the smaller one is the true one. And this office is going to charge us fourpence a crate on thirty, because it has no power to measure us and we told it thirty. And if we go back to them and ask for it to be reckoned on six, they will do it, and it will take a morning, because it is a class of use and a class of use can be acted on, and we will save eightpence a month." He put his hand on the door, not on the sheet. "And I am not going to argue that we should do it. I want to say why in the open first, because I have been thinking about it since the ninth hour and I have got the wrong answer twice and this is the third one.

"It costs us eightpence a month. That is not the reason and anybody in this yard who thinks that is the reason is a fool. The reason is that the only thing this holding has done all season that anybody in the district cannot take away is a figure we published that is smaller than the figure we could have claimed. It is on a door. It is in a book anybody may read and it is going to Cray on Thursday. And the day we go to a district office and ask to be charged on a smaller number, we have told a district office that our numbers are ours to revise. And then the office has our number. And an office that has a number can act on it, and a number they can act on is a number they can change, and I would rather this fen had the one fact in this whole matter that an office cannot get at."

The yard was quiet.

"Pay the thirty," said Tova Reed. "Pay it and enter the eightpence and do not ask for it back, and enter why, and put the why in the same hand as the eightpence so that nobody can read the two apart."

---

On the eleventh, at the fourth hour of the afternoon, with the door in the sun and the four sheets warm to the touch, the arithmetic was done on four sheets of this holding's own paper, and it is the fourth time this volume has done arithmetic in a room, and it took about an hour and a half, and Ilsa Kern was not in it, and nobody in it was pleased.

Fourpence the crate. Thirty crates a month declared. A hundred and twenty pence. Ten shillings. One shilling a month.

And under that, in the same hand, on the same sheet, without a gap between them: six crates a month measured, none back. Twenty-four pence. Two shillings. The difference is eightpence a month, and it is a hundred and six hundredweight of nothing.

And under that: four shillings a hundredweight for compost, and three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight short, seventy-nine pounds sixteen. And a shilling a month buys a quarter of a hundredweight. And at a quarter of a hundredweight a month the rate on this bridge, if a body ever put it into compost instead of paying it to a district, would close the gap in a hundred and thirty-three years.

The man of about fifty read that last figure out loud twice, because he wanted it entered that he had read it twice, and then he said the sentence about it and the sentence is in the book.

"A hundred and thirty-three years," he said. "And the fen is eight people and I am one of the ones who will not be in the yard in a hundred and thirty-three years, and neither will any of you, and I would like it entered that I have thought about that arithmetic for about a minute and I have not been able to make it come out any smaller, and I am not going to say anything clever about it, because a clever thing about a hundred and thirty-three years is a way of not having read the sheet."

The fourth sheet was the one nobody wanted, and it is the one the day was for, and the last line of it is the sentence that turned the middle of this batch.

*Fourpence the crate, on thirty, is a shilling. Six crates a month and none back is two shillings. The route pays a shilling and carries two shillings' worth. The difference is entered and is not claimed and will not be asked for. This is a rate, and a rate is not a bill, and a bill is not a debt, and the difference between the three of them is who can ask the question.*

"Read that again," said Anneke Roos, "and read it slower, because I have been in this yard for a season and that is the first sentence anybody has said to me that I could not have written in a letter to a stranger, and I have written a great many of those."

Lissa Vale read it again, slower, and the clerk read it back after her, and the hour went on it, and the eighteenth note of the season was not written yet, because the man who wrote those was not in the room, and the yard knew it, and one of them said so, and the clerk entered that the note would come on the twelfth with the figures attached.

---

And then somebody said the thing that was underneath the whole afternoon, and it was said by accident, and it was said by the woman with the shilling, who had come to the gate with a basket and had stayed the whole time and had not spoken once.

"You have got the return column on that door," she said. "And you have got fourpence a crate on it now, and they are the same column. Because it is the only column on that sheet that was ever going to be worth money, and I have been looking at it for an hour, and the seed does not come back down it and the beans do not come back down it, and the thing that comes back down it is one sowing a year, and the district has just put a price on the column that has nothing in it."

Nobody had anything to say to that for a while, and then Tova Reed went and stood in front of the door, and read the seventh column out, with the number of the readings under it, which was eighteen, and she read the number too, and then she read the four parts again, in particular the second, and the yard understood what the man of about seventy had meant on Wednesday about the bottom of a sheet.

"It is not on the same column," she said. "Look at it. It is on the second column, the crate, and the crate is counted, and the return is the seventh. Fourpence is on the crate and the crate goes out and the return does not. So the district has put a price on a column that only ever goes one way, and the column that goes both ways is still empty of anything a district knows how to tax." She put her finger on the door under the printed head of the seventh. "And I am not going to say that out loud twice, because a man in a counting house nine hundred miles off could hear it, and I do not want it in a letter. But it is on the page now and it is in the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the fourth time this building has done a sum in a room and the sum got worse each time and every time somebody was honest about it."
