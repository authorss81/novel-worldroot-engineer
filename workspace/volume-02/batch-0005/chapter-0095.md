# Chapter 95

## The Dry End Refuses

The fen argued for about forty minutes on the twenty-second, at the seventh hour, whether to send anybody up the road, and it was the shortest argument of the month and it was taken in the yard standing, and it was against sending anybody, and everybody who spoke wanted to send somebody.

Tova Reed put it in a form that went in the ledger.

"A person standing at somebody else's fence while a thing is refused is a person who has made herself a party to a refusal," she said, "and we struck a clause out of a document in this yard a month ago on the exact ground that a person with no standing must not be made a party to a paper, and Mr Vale agreed with me in front of the whole of you, and I will not be the woman who undoes her own clause inside a month because the road is bad and we would like a result.

"Mr Vale is the engineer of record and he wrote the terms. I am a party to six clauses. Mr Byre carried the refused paper to her fence in the inside pocket of his coat and read out a document to her and came home with nothing in his hands, and if he goes up there tomorrow and a thing is refused, he will have done it twice."

"And I am not going," said Marek Vale, "and this is the fourth time in a season I have said a thing like that and I am not going to get better at it. Nobody from this holding goes and collects. A holding that is owed an answer cannot also be the body that goes and gets it, because getting it is the part that makes it ours. And if we go and get it, then in four years there is a line in this book that says somebody from this yard was told a thing at that place, and there is a woman at that place who has never been handed a form in her life, and she did not ask for either of us to come."

So Ilsa Kern went, and Jonas Byre went with her, and neither of them went to ask anybody anything.

They went out at first light on the twenty-second to put stone in the first break, which is fen work and had been wanted for a fortnight, because a loaded cart went over that place on the sixth of this month and a carters' man who has been over bad ground for thirty-one years said he would not put a second cart inside a week in the same place, because the place he has just used is a hole now, and it has been sixteen days since. The clerk entered the reason for their going and the rule it came under and the hour, and Lissa Vale read it back, and Dunmar Fitch entered that the going was work and not a collection, and that the standing of this holding is that anybody in it may be asked whether a journey is work or a collection and must answer, and neither of them was asked.

They were at the low dry from about the ninth hour. Ilsa Kern laid stone at the first break from about the eleventh hour, with a bar and a bucket, and Jonas Byre went on to the second break and walked it and came back, which he now does about once a month and has never once written down as anything but a walk.

And at about the second hour of the afternoon a cart came up the March road from the Fennmere side, and it was a low cart with a good team and it was not loaded, and Coll Renner got down off it at the low dry and walked to the fence of thorn and hazel, and there was no gate because there has never been anything at Wrenlow worth closing.

Ilsa Kern was about ninety feet away with a bar in her hand and she saw the whole of it and she did not move and she did not speak and nobody asked her to, and the fen entered all three of those things with the hour, and the reason, which is the rule, and the rule entered the reason so that the chain could not be seen to have broken anywhere.

Wenna Roke was in the second plot with a hoe when the cart came, and she came out to the fence, and Ilsa Kern said afterwards that she came out to the fence and not to the cart, and that she put the hoe down before she got there.

The refusal took about a minute and a half and there were two parts to it and neither of them was about Coll Renner.

"You have got a column for one thing a year," she said. "A sowing. That is all that column has ever had in it, in the whole time it has been on your door, and you have said so out loud at your own gate, and I have heard it from a man who came to my fence with a paper in his coat and stood where you are standing.

"And I told your man in the spring that a thing that goes back down your road goes to a counting house nine hundred miles off and does not stop at my fence on the way, and I did not say it to be difficult with him. I said it because it is the shape of the thing, and the shape of the thing is that everything about a road is written down except me.

"Your man wants eleven carts down that road and eleven carts back up it. Your column is one line deep. There is no column at my end at all, because I will not keep one, and you sent me four sheets in a coat pocket in the spring and I sent them back and I was right. So that road would have eleven things going down it and eleven things coming back up it, and the half that comes back up would go past my fence eleven times with nobody writing down what it was, and I would be the only person standing at the fence while it happened. And if a sack went past me in the spring and did not come back in the autumn there would be nothing in this district anybody could ever ask me about it, because there would be no column here and nobody in your yard would have been here. That is what I am refusing. I am not refusing you. I am refusing to be the only person on a road who is not on paper."

And then she said the second part, and Ilsa Kern entered that it was said without being asked for and that nobody at the fence asked for it.

"And I will tell you the rest of it, because you have not asked me and I would rather say it than have you come back and ask me. My answer is no, and my answer is no, and I am not going to give you a better one than that, and if you come back in a month I will say the same two words, and you can write down that I said them twice, and it will not help you."

Renner did not argue. He said one sentence, and Ilsa Kern wrote it down from ninety feet and did not move while she wrote it.

"I am not going to argue with a woman about a column," he said. "I have been on the wrong end of a column twice in nine years and both times it was me."

And he got back in his cart and went down the March road, and the eleven loads did not go, and the second break was not made good, and the low quarter has four wells off this morning and will have four wells off at the first hard frost.

---

He came up the fen road himself on the twenty-third, at about the tenth hour, with no cart, and he told them in the yard, standing, in about six sentences, and the clerk entered all six and the hour and read them back to him, and he asked for it to be read twice, which is a thing a man does when he does not want his own softening.

"Then she has refused it," Marek Vale said.

"She has."

"Do not tell me anything else she said."

"I am not going to." Renner said it flatly and without any performance about it. "I have not written it down. I did not write it down at the fence and I am not going to write it down here, and I want the clerk to enter that I declined to write it down and why, and the why is that I have got nine men in a yard and one of them cannot read, and I have been the man who said a thing out loud in a room with a man in it who cannot read, and I have thought about that for about a day, and I would rather be the second man in this district to keep his mouth shut about a person's words than the first to be a gentleman about it.

"And there is a second thing," he said, "and it is the reason I am here in person and not by a boy. A refusal said to a boy is a refusal that can be carried. I did not want this one carried to you by a man with a cart. If I am going to be turned down, I am going to be turned down by somebody who can remember my face."

Nobody in that yard asked him for the terms, and Dunmar Fitch entered that nobody had asked, and entered that the fen had wanted to and had been told no by the engineer of record, and Lissa Vale read that entry back and read the reason back a second time because it is the ninth line of a thing.

What went into the ledger, and into the fen's own book, in the engineer's hand and with the hour, is this, and it is the third time this holding has entered a refusal that way, and it is the first time one of them has been used.

*That a use of this route, put in writing on the twentieth of this month by a licensed contractor of this district, was refused at the second end on the twenty-second of this month by a person in occupation there. That this entry records the fact of a refusal and not its terms. The terms are not held by this body. They are not written down by the man they were said to, at his own request, in this yard, and the reason he gave is entered on the page above. They are not written down by the two persons of this holding who were ninety feet from the fence and did not speak, and the reason for that is the rule of the twentieth of a month two months back and the clerk's standing. They are not held by this body and this body will not ask.*

And the price, entered on its own line because the fen has a standing about lines that must not be missed: *that the route is smaller on the twenty-third of this month than it was on the twentieth, by one use of it, and that the use would have made the second break good, and that the second break is not made good, and that the offer of eleven pounds stands and is unanswered, and that this holding will not answer it in this season, and that the low quarter has four wells off and forty-one houses on ground this holding killed, and that no person in this yard has said that the refusal was wrong.*

The clerk read all of it, and the man of about seventy read the last line of it back, and asked for his initial to be put against nothing, and the yard stood about for a while, and the pump ran, and at the north end of the yard the two boards over the bay had seventy-seven days cut into them and the number was still going up and nobody went out to look at it.

And at the fourth hour Lissa Vale read the door out, because she reads it, and she read the schedule with its measured figure under its declared one, and the clause under the columns in the fen's own hand, and the notice on the left, and the four sheets of the class of use with all four parts, and then she read the entry of the refusal, because it is in the book and the book is a book anybody may read and there is a hole in a fence nine miles off with a woman standing behind it who does not know that it went in.

And Marek Vale said the thing that made the twenty-third a working day and not a bad one, and it is nine words, and it is in the ledger with the hour.

"Two documents, and nobody in this district can say which one governs."

"Four sheets with a number at the head of them," he said, "that say a use of ground in district schedule is a thing a district may put a rate on. And one paragraph in our own hand on a door, that says a use of this route is a thing a person with a hoe can stop. And the Crown's says what a use is and does not say who may refuse one, and ours says who may refuse one and does not say what a use is, because we were told not to say, and we were right, and I have not slept properly since Tuesday. And the ground under all of it is ground the district carries in schedule, and nobody has ever defined in this district what a use of it is when two instruments disagree, and there is no form, and there is no office, and there is not anybody I can write to about it. So it stands, and it stands on the second end of a road, and it is the only standing in this arrangement that is not on anybody's paper."

Lissa Vale read that back, and a person read it back after her, and the reader was the man of about fifty, who did not say anything else, and the count of the readings of the seventh column went from nineteen to twenty under the form in the clerk's hand, the middle of this month having been the fifteenth and the last of it being this day, and both of those were days this holding reads its door out on, and the days in between were not, and the number had gone up on the seventh of this month as well as on the first and the fifteenth, and that was a Sunday, and the reason it had gone up on the seventh was a man with a chalk cart who asked a question at the gate, and every one of those five readings is on the count in the clerk's hand and the count is the fen's own invention and no district office has ever asked for it.
