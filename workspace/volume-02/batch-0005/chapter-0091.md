# Chapter 91

## Conditional Use

Coll Renner came up the fen road on the fourteenth, which was a Sunday, with no case and no collar and no tools, and he said why at the gate before he came in, which is what he does, and what he does it for is so that nobody can say he formed an opinion first.

"I have had a clause of that office's in my head for nine days," he said, "and I would rather be in this yard on a Sunday when there is nobody here than on a working day when there is. And I would like it read before the body has an opinion about it, because a licensed man who reads a condition after forty people have agreed what it means is a man who has been brought in to agree with them, and I have been that man twice in my life and I did not know it either time."

The third part of a schedule of a class of use was read aloud in the yard on the Sunday at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, by Lissa Vale, and the man of about fifty read it back, and there were six people there and a dog.

A use of ground in district schedule is conditional. In this class the condition is that the operator keeps the declared capacity in the ordinary course, and produces the record of the route to an officer of the district on request. The operator is the person in occupation of the node at the first end.

Nobody spoke for about half a minute, and then Renner said the thing that made the whole of the rest of the week, and it was nine words.

"It is not a trick," he said. "It is a key."

And he gave the rest of it standing at the gate with the sun behind him, and the clerk entered it in his own words with the hour, and it is here entire because it is the reason this holding understood a Crown document correctly for the first time in a season.

"I have read four of these in nine years and every one of them was a key and not a trick, and I want the word trick taken out of this yard before the end of the week because it is the wrong word and it will make you feel clever. A trick is a thing that is aimed at you. A key is a thing that fits a lock somebody else built, and the lock was built before you arrived, and the lock is not aimed at anybody in particular because a lock is not a man. The thing that ought to frighten you is not the key. The thing that ought to frighten you is that you cannot tell who the lock was built for until somebody tries the key, and the only way to find out who it was built for is to have somebody try it, and nobody is going to try it for nothing."

"Then what is in the third part that we are not seeing," said Tova Reed.

"Two things, and one of them is a door," said Renner. "The first is the word *operator*. That office has never had a word for you before. It has had a word for a charterholder and a word for a person in occupation of ground and it has had your name in its own book since the ninth of this month as a local holder, and this is the first time it has put you in a class of use and a class of use has an operator in it, and a man who is an operator is a man who has duties and duties are the price of the word. That is not a trick. That is what happens to everybody. It happened to me when I took out a license and I have not been the same sort of man since and I have never once been able to say the day it happened.

"The second is the door. *Produces the record of the route to an officer of the district on request.* And that sentence is a door into a room, and the room has been built, and somebody in nine miles knows what is in the room, and this office has not told you and I do not think it is obliged to, because a schedule of terms tells you what you have to do and not what is behind the door you have to do it through. And I will tell you what I would do if I were you, and then I will go home, and you may do it or not do it and it will not be my name on it."

---

What Tova Reed did about the record of the route took the rest of the Sunday and most of Monday morning, and it is the first time in a season she has refused a document in front of another body, and the refusal was not of a demand.

"There are three books in this building and there is a fourth thing," she said. "There is the fen's own book. There is the fieldbook, which I have not opened and am not opening. There is the use log, which is twelve lines and is not a record of anything anybody may read without a licensed man in the room. And there is the schedule on the door, which is a form, and the ledger, which is a book and is two days old.

"And that office has used a phrase and not a number, and I want the phrase looked at before anybody in this yard decides what it means, because a phrase is where a door is. It says the record of the route. Not *the records of the holder*. Not *the book*. Not a form and a number. *The record of the route.*"

"That is the schedule," said Marek Vale.

"That is one reading of it, and it is the reading that costs us nothing, and I have been on the receiving end of that office for eleven years and I am telling you that a phrase is always read by the person who benefits. The record of the route can be the schedule and the ledger, which are two documents that describe a road and what goes on it. And it can be the fen's own book, which is eleven years and a half of everything this holding has done, including nine wrongs and a refusal and a woman's two sentences and a district's file of sixty pounds ten and a broker's offer, and if the reading is that, then the first officer who walks up that gate and asks for the record of the route gets the book, and every copy of it, and every copy that has gone to Cray for four hundred and eleven days, and he will get them in an afternoon from a counting house that has never had a district officer in it.

"So this is the answer and I have thought about it for a day and I would like it read before I say it, and I would like it read to a man who is going to be difficult about it, which is Mr Vale, and I would like the reader to be Mr Vale."

Marek Vale read the whole of it back including her reason for having it read, which is a thing he has done twice in a season and does not do as a courtesy.

"That is a refusal," he said, "and it is the right one, and I am going to argue it anyway because you asked me to and because if I do not argue it, nobody will."

"There is nothing in it to argue. Produce the schedule and the ledger to any person at any hour. Do not produce the book to anybody. And if it is ever asked for in writing, answer it in writing, in one line, with the reason on the page."

"There are two things in it," he said. "The first is that in four months somebody in nine miles will read *the record of the route* in a class of a schedule of a class of use and he will read it the way I read it, because it is the plain reading and because he has a file and a day. And when he has asked and been refused he will have a refusal, and a refusal is a thing that goes in a file with a date on it, and a file is a thing people make decisions out of. And I cannot make him read it the other way. I can only put a sentence in front of the word and hope.

"And the second is worse and it is our own doing and I would like it written down as ours. The ledger is two days old and it is a book anybody may read and everything this holding is proudest of is going in it, because that is what we did on the thirteenth, and we did it on purpose, and it was the right thing to do. But it means that the small true thing is in public and the large true thing is in a drawer, and in four years somebody in a room is going to notice that, and they are going to say that this holding publishes the parts that make it look careful and keeps the parts that make it look like what it is. And they will be half right. And we chose that. It is the same trade we have been making all season with a bigger print run."

"I know," said Tova Reed. "Put it in that way and put my name against it. I want it in the book that the steward of a trust standing nine hundred miles off knows exactly what handing over a rate does to a book anybody may read and did it anyway, on the thirteenth, and wrote the reason down at the time."

---

Gael Senn measured the leak at the seventh hour on the fifteenth, twice, with two rules, the second of them the one that reads smaller on that corner, pump on, into the housing and not into the pit, and it was five-eighths of an inch, and it had been five-eighths of an inch at the seventh hour of the first of this month, and she wrote it in the ordinary words and read it back, and the bed was at three and a half degrees and she said the number twice because a soil iron cannot read the tenth of a degree and she had said so before and would say so again.

Nobody connected any of it to the four sheets on the door, and the fen entered that nobody had connected any of it to the four sheets on the door, and entered it as a fact and not as a decision, because a decision would have meant somebody had thought about it and nobody had.

And at the tenth hour on the fifteenth, which is the middle of the month and is one of the three days this holding reads its door out on, Lissa Vale read the whole of it in the yard, and there were nine people there, and the count of the readings of the seventh column went to nineteen under the form in the clerk's hand.

She read the schedule first, all seven columns, and the declared figure and then the measured figure, and the route line, and the count of the readings. Then the notice of the second, all four lines, and the date on it. Then the four sheets, all four parts, and she read part two twice and part three once, slowly.

And when she had finished, nobody went, and the yard emptied the way a yard empties, and the sun came off the door and the four sheets went slack on the nail, and Marek Vale stood and looked at the door for a while, and then said the thing out loud for the first time in about four hundred words to a yard with four people in it.

"A condition is a word, and a word like that is only worth anything if there is a no on the other side of it," he said. "Every condition in every instrument ever written has got a no behind it. A charter has a no, because a charter can be broken and somebody will be fined. A lease has a no. There is no no behind this. That office has given us a rate and a class of use and a standing, and standing is the only thing this holding has wanted for eleven months, and it has come in a form, and the form has a condition on it, and the condition is on us.

"So I am going to say the whole of it plainly and I am going to say it twice, and the second time is at the gate, and the reason I am going to say it twice is that Lissa Vale will not enter a thing she has heard once and she is right and I am not going to waste a good rule."

Lissa Vale stopped him at the step.

"Out loud at the gate," she said, "and to the road, and if nobody is there then to the door, and I will put the hour on it, and the reader for the second will be the man of about fifty because he reads everything and I have stopped pretending I do not know that. And you will say it in the words you just used and not in better words, because the words you just used were correct and a man who improves his own sentence at the gate has decided he is writing it down."

So he said it at the gate, to a road and a hedge and one carter's cart that went by and did not stop, and the man of about fifty read it back at the well house door, and it went into the seed-route ledger with the hour on it, and the fen entered that it was said twice in a yard and at a gate on the fifteenth of this month by the engineer of record and that it is the first time this season that a thing has been entered as being said twice, and it went in with the reason, which is that Lissa Vale asked for it and Marek Vale agreed in about a second.

And out of that morning there came one more piece of paper, and it is the whole of what the fen decided about the record of the route, and it is one line, and Tova Reed wrote it and Lissa Vale read it and the clerk entered it and nobody added anything to it.

*The record of this route is the seed-route schedule on the well house door and the seed-route ledger on the seed-house table. Both are produced to any person at any hour. This holding's own book is the record of this body and is not produced to anybody, and the reason is on the page above it.*

It went up inside the seed house door on the left, on the sixteenth, at the ninth hour, and it is a piece of paper with no number on it and no printed head, and it is a refusal, and it is the first refusal this holding has written for itself rather than entered on behalf of somebody else, and it was written by a woman standing nine hundred miles from the ground it is about and she put her name under it and Anneke Roos initialled it as an opinion in four words.
