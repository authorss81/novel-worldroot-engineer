# Chapter 97

## A Useful Engineer

Both letters came on the same day, which was the twenty-sixth, and by different hands, and they were on the middle of the seed house table at about the fourth hour of the afternoon when the fen came in from the ditch, and nobody had asked for either of them and both of them had arrived because of a book anybody may read.

The first was from the district water office at Fennmere, and it was two sheets and it was a post.

*Engineer of record, seed routes, second part, bylaw district. Three pounds a month. A box at the office post and a seal and a counter-signature, and a man in the office who answers for the route and can be asked about it. The district has read the printed record of the seed house at Lantern Reach, which is in circulation in four counties, and the district does not send men to districts that have not got anything to maintain. On taking the post, the engineer of record delivers to the office the seed annex and the eleven years of books of the body he has been engineer of record for, for the purposes of the district's schedule check, and the office makes a copy and returns the original.*

Three pounds a month is more than the seed house tin has held in a month since the levy went to four times, and it is eleven times the rate on the road, and the fen read all three of those figures out of the two sheets inside a quarter of an hour and put them down in that order and then said the thing that made the rest of the day.

"That is the broker's speech with a different man on the letterhead," she said, "and I am not going to let anybody in this yard call it that either, because the man who wrote it has never lied to us and has four times been straight with us and one of those times he offered to fill in a box in a form for us himself. Ruben Ashe stood in this yard on the twenty-ninth of last month and told us what a district needs in order to send a form to a body. A box at a post, and a seal, and a man who answers. That is what that speech was for. I do not believe for one minute that this letter is a plot and I do believe it is the same shape, and a man who cannot tell the difference between those two things is a man who will be caught out inside a year."

The second letter was from the printer at Fennmere who set the two hundred pages and Coll Renner's two hundred words, and it was one sheet, and it asked three questions and gave one piece of news.

The questions: is there a second printing; how many; and would the seed house consider a separate pamphlet of the seed-route ledger, the one begun on the thirteenth of this month, for anybody who wants a copy of it and not of the rest.

The news: a man in a county the printer did not name had asked at his counter for a copy of the ledger and had offered money for it and had then asked whether it had a number on it, and the printer had said it had not, and the man had said that was a pity and had bought a copy of the printing instead.

"He is not a wrong man," the printer wrote. "He is a man who wants a number because a number is how he files things. I have not put his name down and I am not going to and I am telling you that I have had that conversation three times this month with three different men, and every one of them asked for a number, and not one of them asked whether the thing was true. That is not a complaint about them. It is a report on your decision, and I set the type and I have watched it go for four months and I would rather tell you than sell you a second one."

And then, at the bottom, in the printer's own hand and not in his office's: *There is a price for the next one and I am not going to write it on this sheet, because a price written on a sheet in a book that a body keeps becomes a figure anybody can check, and I do not want to be the reason a body in this district has a number. Come and see me and we will talk about money in a room with no book in it, the way it was done when your grandmother had her name printed.*

Marek Vale read that four times and then said one sentence about it, and it went in, and Dunmar Fitch entered it with the hour and the fact that it was said by the engineer of record and not by the clerk.

"Write the price on the sheet," he said. "Not this week and not to me. Write it on the next one, in the body of the thing, in a line with the rest of the business, so that anybody who reads the book can see what it cost and what it was worth, and take the money. Because a printing that was done for nothing is a favor, and a favor is a chain, and this holding has had four favors this season and two of them were against us and one of them was a letter that came back in a coat pocket, and I am not going to let a printer in Fennmere carry a favor for us out of goodwill and call it friendship in four years when he has a bill and we have not paid it."

The printer's other question is the one that got the standing written down, and it is not about a number.

Tova Reed said it at the north gate at the sixth hour with five people there, and she said it in about a minute, and it is in the book entire, and it is the sixth time her name has gone into a book in this district in a way she did not choose and has not complained about.

"Being readable is a position and it is not a virtue," she said. "Nobody has thanked this holding for being readable. Not the woman on the low dry, who walked eleven days for a book and then asked for her name to be kept off the road, and not the man with a chalk cart, who asked me for a number and did not ask what the column was for until a second man explained it, and not the four counties, and not the printer, who is not asking whether we are honest, he is asking how many. We are not thanked for it and we cannot be thanked for it, and I would like it entered in these words today, on the day a printer asks us for a second book, because in four years somebody is going to write that the honest fen was the one everybody knew, and the man who voted against this book said that in this yard on the twenty-ninth of last month and I wrote it down, and it will be in a book in four counties, and it will be the only true thing in it that is not about a column."

And she put her name to it, and that is the sixth time, and the clerk entered the count and the hour and the standing that she has stopped counting, which she said on the twenty-ninth of last month and repeated on the twenty-sixth of this month in one sentence and would not be thanked for either.

Then they argued the post for a day, and nobody could agree, and on the twenty-seventh at about the fourth hour they went down to the pit together with a book, because Marek Vale had said that nothing was to be said in that yard about a post that was not entered, and the fen's whole practice in eleven months had been that everything is entered and a thing that is not entered has not happened, and he was not going to have a letter in a drawer that decided the shape of the next three years.

They stood at the edge of it with the pump on and the frame humming from about ten hundred and forty and the dial where it had been since the first of last month, and Dunmar Fitch read out what the letter said and what it cost, and Marek Vale answered it in about a minute and did not soften it.

"I am not answering it because I have a better offer," he said. "I have not got a better offer and there is not one in this district. I am answering it because it costs one sentence and the sentence is that on the first of next month I take a post with a box at a post, and on the second of next month there is a schedule check with a box on it that somebody in nine miles can fill in about this holding, and the person they will ask to answer it is the man who answers for the district. And there is no corruption in that. Not in the letter, not in the office, not in the man who wrote it. That is exactly how it works everywhere and everybody who has ever done it knew it when they did it, and that is the point, and if I take that post I will be a man who knows it when he does it, and the only difference between me and a man in an office in a county I have never been to is that I have written down what a road with crates on it is."

"So the answer goes on Thursday, in writing, in one line," he said, "and it is the same one line that is on the inside of the seed house door about the record of the route, and it says the schedule and the ledger are the record of the route and this holding's own book is the record of this body and is not produced to anybody. And I am not going to insult a man who wrote me a polite letter by sending him a different sentence. I am going to send him the true one and he will understand it, because he wrote to a body that has a book in four counties and he has read it, and a man who has read a book like that knows what it is for."

---

And then, at the pit, with the pump running, because it was running and neither of them was going to stop it and talk over it, Marek Vale answered the question that is the question of this whole season, and he had known it was coming for nine days and had thought about it every day since the eighteenth, and he gave the engineer's answer and it is not the answer anybody wanted.

"You asked me at the gate on the twenty-ninth of last month who owns a grafted seed, and I have been let off answering it for a month on the grounds that I was the wrong man to ask, and I am going to be told what I am in a yard where it is entered or I am going to say it here and have it entered here, and I would rather it were here, and the reason is that I do not want to be asked twice in a yard and answered twice in a yard and have the two answers in a book.

"Ownership is a claim on a thing you made and cannot make again," he said. "That is the whole of what the word has ever meant and it is the only test in this world that has ever worked. If you can make it again it is a method, and a method is a thing anybody can take off you by watching you do it four times, and there is no law and no custom and no office in this district or any other that will stop them and there ought not to be.

"So nothing in this fen is owned by anybody. Not the bean. Not the March's line, and I want to say that in the hearing of the woman whose name is on the tin nine hundred miles off. Not the thing I cut in a flowerpot in the spring under a licensed man's clip with a record written by the licensed man and not by me. What I have is a line in a book. That is all. A receipt. And it does not survive my death in any way a person can enforce, and it does not even survive my death very well in the way that matters, because a person in four years can copy my line out of my book and have the same claim I have and neither of us can prove a thing and neither of us can stop the other.

"And that is the honest answer to the question as it was put, and it is a bad answer, and it is the answer an engineer gives, because an engineer is asked about a thing and not about a person."

He stopped, and then he said the other half, and he said it to her and not to the clerk.

"Your answer is better than mine and I am not going to pretend it is not and I am not going to give way on it, because you are not going to give way on yours, and we have not given way on anything in eleven months and I would not know what we were doing if we started now.

"You own the seed. I own the root. Those are two properties in two different places, one in a sack and one in the ground, and you cannot put them in the same hand without somebody losing something, and you have spent eleven years refusing to lose something and so have I, and that is why we cannot settle it between us, and it is not a disagreement about a fact. It is a disagreement about which of us is going to be the one that is told.

"And in four years you are going to be the one that is asked, and not me. You have your name on six things in a public ledger and on a printing in four counties and on a tin in a counting house, and I am the engineer of record of a body with no charter, and I am not on anything anybody can serve. So the answer to who owns a grafted seed in this district, today, is that it belongs to whoever can check the book, and today that is nobody, and in four years it will belong to whoever can check the book, and the man who prints the ledger in a county you do not know is going to be the person who decides that, and he is not a bad man and he does not care and that is the danger and it is not solved and this conversation has not solved it."

"It is not the answer either," said Tova Reed.

"It is the answer to the question I was asked," he said. "You asked who owns it and I have told you what ownership is. That you do not like it is a second question and I am not the office for it, and I have been the office for it for eleven months and it has cost me a bridge, a job and a woman's face at a fence, and I am going to hand it back."

She did not answer that, and it went in with the hour, and the clerk read it back, and Lissa Vale read the second half of it back a second time, and the fen entered that the engineer of record of this holding said on the twenty-seventh of this month at the fourth hour at the pit that the question was not answered, and that neither of the two people who said it was thanked, and that there is no clause in a charter or a fifth sheet or a book about a person being thanked, and that this is the third time that line has been entered in eleven months and the last time anybody should expect it to be the last.

---

A cart came up the fen road at about the sixth hour of the twenty-seventh and went on toward the low dry, and it was loaded, and it was lashed the way Nia Vale lashed crates on a hand barrow, and the man of about fifty watched it go past from the well house step and did not say anything, and nobody in that yard asked him anything about it, and Dunmar Fitch entered that a loaded cart had gone up the fen road on the twenty-seventh at about the sixth hour and that the cart has now been past that gate twice in a season and has not stopped either time, and that the count of the two is entered and the reason is not, and that the reason was not given and was not asked for, and the reader for that entry was Lissa Vale, and she read it back, and she did not look at the man of about fifty while she did it, and the fen entered that too, and it is the second time, and nobody has ever written down why either time and nobody is going to.
