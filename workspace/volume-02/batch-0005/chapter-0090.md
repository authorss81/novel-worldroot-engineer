# Chapter 90

## What Six Is Worth

The note came on the twelfth, under the closing figures of the eleventh, signed by nobody but Marek, and read back before it was initialled, and it is the eighteenth note under section nine of the charter schedule, and there is no charter any more, which the note says on its face.

*Four sheets of terms of a class of use, on the office's paper, of four parts, with a number at the head of four digits and a number at the foot of six, and a box headed for a district holding number that this holding does not have. Fourpence the crate, reckoned monthly on the capacity declared by the operator, this office having no power to measure it and no officer appointed to measure it. The declared figure is thirty. The measured figure is six. One shilling a month, against two shillings' worth of carriage, and the difference of eightpence entered and not claimed and not to be asked for. The compost line, nothing put in, none available, three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight short, seventy-nine pounds sixteen at four shillings, and a shilling a month being a quarter of a hundredweight, and the gap closed at that rate in a hundred and thirty-three years, read out twice by a member of this body. The second part of the terms states that this office has no power to approve a route and no duty to hear it read. A box on a form that cannot be filled in, and a use number put in it. A door that carried two things on the seventeenth of last month and carried three on the tenth of this month, counted before and after.*

*An argument. This is worth nothing against a cut.*

The count of the readings of the seventh column was eighteen under the form when the note was written, and the note says so, and the number did not change on the twelfth because the twelfth is not a day this holding reads it out on.

---

On the twelfth, in the second half of the morning, with the four sheets on the door in the sun and the sheet of arithmetic still on the table from the day before, Anneke Roos asked the question that had been in the room since the ninth hour and had got itself asked at last.

"What is a rate," she said.

"You have all got the answer," she said, "and I have been circling it since the cart came up the road and I am going to say it properly because it is going in the book and I want it in a mouth that is going to be wrong about it in a year. Somebody is going to come here in a year and tell us this shilling a month is a bill for something we took, and it is not. A bill is a thing a person hands you and says pay, and a bill has a person at the back of it, and you can go and ask that person what it is for and be told, and be angry, and be right, and be wrong, and get an answer. This is not that. There is nobody at the back of it. There is a schedule of a class of use and a rate on the schedule, and a class of use is not a person and cannot be asked and cannot be answered and cannot be wrong.

"And a debt is a different thing again and it is the one nobody in this yard has been in since the levy went to four times. A debt is a thing you owe and cannot get out of, and the reason we entered the four pounds sixteen as not received and not remitted and not carried as a debt against anybody is that a body cannot make a debt of a man who has chosen not to pay it and then write the word in its own book without becoming something else. So it is not a debt. Nobody owes anybody anything. This holding is paying fourpence a crate on a figure it published before it measured, on a road that carries a sixth of what it says, and the person it is paying is a form.

"So it is a rate. And the only thing a rate has ever done for anybody is put a price on a thing that already existed. Fourpence did not make this road. It was there. It was in the ground before the office had a category for it, and it will be in the ground after somebody finds a cheaper word. The rate is a price somebody else has put on our work, and the price is too high by two thirds, and we are going to pay the price, and here is the whole of what the price bought us."

She put the sheet down flat with her hand flat on it, which she has done four times this season.

"It bought us a class. Three days ago this road was a thing a man in a yard had said a sentence about and a form with seven columns and no number on it. Since the ninth it is a class of use in a district's book, with a number at the head of it and a number at the foot of it, and that means somebody in nine miles can put a rate on it and ask for it and collect it, and it also means that everything else a district can do to a class of use can be done to this one, in the ordinary course, by people with no opinion about us at all. We have been put in a book we did not write. It is the first time in this fen's life that anybody has done that, and I have had it eleven years and I have not said a word about it out loud, and I would like somebody to notice that I have not said it and to write down that I have not said it."

"I have noticed," said Dunmar Fitch, and entered it with the hour, and read it back.

And then the man of about fifty asked the question that nobody had asked and that everybody had been carrying, which was whether the rate counted toward the levy, and the room went quiet for a different reason.

"It is four shillings a hundredweight and a levy at eighteen pounds on this holding's sixteen houses," he said, "and now it is a shilling a month on a road, and I have heard a man in a yard two months back say that the day the first certificate goes out the levy comes round at four times what it is because the men will know there is money somewhere. So I want to know, in plain words, before somebody in nine miles adds it up, whether the fourpence a crate comes off the eighteen pounds or goes on top of it, because if it goes on top of it then this holding is putting a tax on itself and calling it a class of use, and I would rather be the man who said that out loud in a yard on the twelfth than find it out in a letter."

"It goes on top," said Anneke Roos. "It goes on top and there is nothing anywhere that says it may not. A rate on the district's ground and a levy on this holding's members are two different things with two different names and no form in this district that puts one against the other, and that is the whole answer and it is not a comforting one, because the next time anybody in this district comes after this holding for money they will have a precedent, and the precedent will be in nine miles and not in this yard. Enter that I have said it and enter that I am frightened of it, and enter the two in the same paragraph, and I would like the hour on it, and then somebody else had better say the thing that stops me being the last word on money in this yard for a fortnight."

Which is what Anneke Roos does, and everybody in that yard knows it, and Dunmar Fitch entered that the observation was hers and that it was in the book.

The woman with the shilling was in the yard for all of it and had not spoken, and she spoke now and it was the question the room had not thought to ask, which was who pays it.

"Now I want to know what it is off me," she said. "You are all talking about the holding and the tin and the rate, and the rate is fourpence a crate on thirty crates a month, and I have got four children and a levy on what my husband can lift, and if the fen pays a shilling a month out of that tin then the shilling comes off me one way or another, because the tin is not a magic box, it is three households and a bay. So tell me who pays it, in words, and do not tell me the fen, because the fen is me."

"It is me," said Lissa Vale. "It is this holding, and the levy is on sixteen houses and the rate is on the operator, and the operator is the occupation of the node, and the node is a shed and a pump and eight people. So no household pays it, and it comes out of the tin, and the tin had nine shillings in it as the float for a month in which no lamp oil was bought, and I have read that figure aloud in this yard before and I am not going to read it again."

"So a shilling a month," said the woman with the shilling, "out of nine."

"Out of nine," said Lissa Vale, "until somebody with a lever comes and takes the nine first, and then out of nothing, and I will put the amount on the step in front of two people and say it before it goes in the book, and that is the whole of the arrangement and it is not a good one."

The clerk entered both halves and the hour and read them back, and Anneke Roos entered an opinion on the margin, which is her standing and which nobody has ever yet asked her to explain, and which is four words and is not a sentence anybody in that yard is going to argue with.

And then the man of about fifty said the thing that made it a purchase instead of a bill, and he said it standing, and he said it carefully.

"We were offered two pounds a month for a collar in a yard ten days before the first of the month after next, and refused it in writing, and I argued for it and I voted against the book and I have not been thanked for any of it," he said. "And now this holding is going to pay a shilling a month, every month, to keep a road open that it built out of ditch work and willow and its own hands, to a form, forever. That is the same gesture as the two pounds and it is not the same thing, and I want the difference written down, and the difference is that the two pounds would have bought something and the shilling will not, and one of those is a purchase and the other one is a rate, and I know which one I would rather be doing and I know that wanting it is not a reason."

Nobody thanked him for that either, and he had not asked to be thanked, and the clerk entered that he had said the two offers beside each other of his own accord and that nobody in the yard had asked him to, and Dunmar Fitch entered the observation and Lissa Vale read it back.

---

The seed-route ledger was begun on the thirteenth, in the morning, at the north end of the seed house bench, and the reason it is a second book and not a column is the whole of what this batch turned on, and it took four minutes to say.

"A schedule with a rate on it is a bill," Marek Vale said, "and a bill wants a place to be posted, and the door is for what anybody may read. So the rate goes in a book, and the book is a book and not a form, and anybody may ask to have any entry in it read aloud, and the reader is a person, and a person is anybody who comes." He ruled a page and did not number it. "And I am going to write that rule at the top of it in the same words as the schedule, because the schedule is a form and this is a book, and a book anybody may read is the only kind of thing this holding has that an office cannot come and take, and I would like there to be two of them in this room by the end of the week and not one."

It is called the seed-route ledger, and it is a second book and not a copy of anything, and it does not contain a page of the fen's own book, and it does not contain a page of the fieldbook, and it does not contain the use log, and the clerk entered all three of those absences with the hour, because a reader in four years is going to want to know what is in it and is going to assume more than is.

The first entry in it is six lines and is the whole of the fen's position, and it is in Marek's own hand, and it is here entire.

*The route is a seed route. A rate has been put on it in a class of use. The rate is fourpence the crate on the declared capacity of thirty crates a month, being one shilling. The measured capacity is six crates a month and none back, being two shillings, and the difference is entered here and is not claimed. The rate is not a bill and the bill is not a debt, and the difference between the three of them is who can ask the question. This holding will pay the rate and will not ask for the difference back, and the reason is that the smaller figure on the door is the only fact this body has published that a district cannot get at, and this holding will not hand it over for eightpence a month.*

Anneke Roos read it aloud and initialled it as an opinion, which she does in four words, and the man of about seventy read it back because he was in the yard and had stayed, and he asked for his initial to be put against nothing, which is his standing, and which cost the holding nothing.

---

And then on the thirteenth, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, with the light off the door and the four sheets lying in a corner of it, Anneke Roos counted them.

"One," she said. "Two."

She stopped with her hand an inch off the third sheet.

"When are we reading the third one," she said.

Nobody in that yard had an answer, and the reason nobody had an answer was that the reason nobody had an answer was the thing Marek Vale had been turning over for two days, and he said it, and it is the last thing he says in this batch's second chapter and it is the reason the third part of a Crown instrument sat unread on a public door for two days and is not a failure of anybody.

"The second part is the part they meant us to argue about and we have had two days at it and we have got it right, and I can show anybody how, and the reason is that the second part is a number on a thing we had already measured, and everything in it is in the fen's own hand on the door." He put his hand flat on the schedule without touching the sheets above it. "The third part is a sentence, and a sentence can be read in a minute, and a sentence is exactly the sort of thing that gets read in a minute and then lives in somebody's head for four years doing work it was never examined to do. So we are not reading it this afternoon. We are reading it when we have written down the number and the ledger and what we are paying and why, and then somebody who is not going to be the man who drafted the six clauses is going to read it, and we are going to find out what we think about it before we find out what the office thinks about us."

Lissa Vale said that the reader on the middle of the month was on the fifteenth and would read whatever was on the door, and that she would read the third part then whether it had been argued over or not, and the clerk entered that the reader does not take advice from the body about what is on the door, and the man of about fifty said that was the first sentence anybody in this yard had said that was worth engraving, and the yard laughed, and the pump ran, and the third part of a schedule of a class of use sat on a door at Lantern Reach for two days with nobody in Tarrow Fen knowing what was in it.
