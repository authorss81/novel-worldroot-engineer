# Chapter 50

## The Seventh Note

The charter was two days old and its second clause had already started working, and it was working on them.

Marek found it at about the seventh hour on the twenty-third, standing at the seed-house table with the cooperative's own book open in front of him, and what he had in front of him was the standing pressure log — the book any member, any stranger and any clerk may read without notice — and the fact that it could not be added to since the seventh of the month because a district clerk had certified it and a certified book may only be added to by a district officer in the room.

That had been a lock. It had cost them the clean record on the eighth of a month and Lissa Vale had put the fieldbook and the use log on the middle of this table rather than back in the press and had not been sorry and was not going to be.

It was also, he understood about two hours later, the only thing standing between this yard and a district officer's handwriting in a book they can read.

"Because it is a condition now," said Dunmar Fitch. He had not moved from the end of the table. He had a clean cuff on and he had been listening for the better part of an hour. "Mr Bray is right and Mr Bray was right and I have been wrong about it for two days. On the twenty-first of a month this book was a promise we made to ourselves in case anybody ever asked. On the twenty-first of a month it is a thing a district officer may come and read, and any figure in it is a figure the district holds."

"It was always a figure the district held." Marek turned the page round. "That was the whole argument for it. A number a stranger can walk up to and check."

"A number a stranger can walk up to and check is worth nothing if the stranger does not walk up," said Dunmar Fitch. "That was never the argument. The argument was that a book we keep ourselves is a thing they have to come and argue with, and a book they have certified is a thing they have already read."

Anneke Roos had come in from the low acre with her coat still on and she had been at the table for six minutes and she said the thing that stayed in the room for the rest of the season.

"A book anybody may copy is a book anybody may use," she said. "I have been trying to say that since the fourteenth and I could not get a room to hear it because we were all so pleased that anybody at all could read us. There are two kinds of book. There is the book you keep, and there is the book you publish. We have published one. Nobody in this yard decided to publish it. The district did, two days ago, at our request, in a hearing we asked for."

Nobody argued with her, and the reason nobody argued with her was that Lissa Vale was at the other end of the table with her hands flat on the wood, and Lissa Vale had been in the room on the fourth of a month when a motion to enter the claim in the log was lost by nine to twenty-two, and she had not voted for it, and she had never said which way she had voted.

---

The thing she was talking about happened at about the eighth hour and it was Marek's fault and he brought it up himself, and it is the second sentence in the entry he made at the sixth hour on the twenty-second of a month, which is a section-nine note of which he is the only author, and it reads: *A charter condition is a thing somebody has to do and this is it.*

On the fourth of a month this body voted, thirty-five hands in a room of forty, to open its own book to any member, any stranger and any clerk without notice. The vote was carried and it was a private act of the meeting and it was deliberately not a filing, because the motion to enter the claim in the log had been lost nine to twenty-two with four abstentions the day before, and a room that had just refused to write a number down did not then hand the number to a district by another route.

Twenty-one days later a hearing officer adopted it as a condition of a charter, at the applicant body's own request, and put it on a fifth sheet of a district document.

"So it is a filing," said Ilsa Kern.

"It is a filing," said Marek. "It is a filing we made ourselves, out loud, in a room, with the hour on it, and the district wrote it down because we asked it to, and the district did not invent it, and I have heard six people in this yard take some comfort out of that sentence this month and I have taken most of it myself."

"Can we take it back."

"No," said Dunmar Fitch. "Not this season. It was adopted at the applicant's request and the district may not amend a condition this season without the consent of the body, and that is clause two on the fourth sheet, and I have read it four times, and it is the most useful sentence anybody has put in front of me in nineteen years of being a clerk and it is the sentence that stops me doing what I am about to suggest."

"Which is what."

"Which is that a body cannot consent to the removal of its own record, because the removal is the consent," said Dunmar Fitch. "I have been a charter clerk since I was thirty-nine and I have never once wanted to unwrite a page, and I am telling you that the mechanism which was put in that charter to protect this book's independence is now the mechanism that protects it against us. In six months one of the sixteen will want it out of the district's hands, and I will be the one asked to do it, and I will be able to say: this body cannot consent, and here is the clause, and here is the minute, and the minute is the district's."

Anneke Roos said nothing at all to that, and she wrote her initial against it, which is what she does when she is recording an opinion rather than doing the work.

---

They read the fifth sheet out in the yard afterward, because four of the five people who come when a thing is being read out had never seen it and because it is the sheet that governs everything else and it is one page and it takes four minutes.

Marek read it standing on the well house step, at about the ninth hour, in daylight, with the pump running, and he read all of it including the consequential sentence and including the second wording, and he read it in the order it is written and he did not stop to explain any of it, and the reason he did not stop is that he had spent the season explaining and had learned that explaining a document in a yard is how a document stops being a document.

It took four minutes and a man of about fifty asked one question at the end of it, which was what the word *expiry* did, and Dunmar Fitch answered it in one sentence, and the sentence was *the day the charter stops, and after that day it is a piece of paper on a door and the well does not know.*

Then Marek read it again, and the second reading was for the two people who had come late, and the second reading was slower and he stopped once, in the middle of the second wording, to say that the woman who wrote that sentence was in this yard on the first of the month and was twenty-three years old and had never held an office in her life, and that she had been asked whether she was sure, and that she had said yes, and that a hearing officer had put it in district law in nine minutes, and that he wanted both of those facts said out loud in a yard today while he was standing on a step.

The man of about fifty said: "Then it is the best thing in it."

"It is the best thing in it," said Marek, "and I argued against it for nine minutes and I was wrong, and I have written that down already, and I am not going to argue about it again in my life."

---

He went out onto the three acres at the ninth hour because he could not sit still, and they are at the top of the outer field, thirty inches of gravel over flints, four hundred and ten yards of ditch dug by hand across three autumns, and they went under for a week in the middle of last month and have come up sour.

The fen had not frozen and had not dried. It had gone from brown to a gray that had a green in it under the crust, and where the ditch was, the ditch was a crack with soft edges, and Marek put his boot in the crack and it took the boot and let it go and did not hold the weight the way it had held it for three seasons.

He stood in the middle of it and did the sum he had been not doing for nine days.

The return main is twenty-two rods of eight inch. The specification is forty-one of twelve. That is a deficit of a little under two thirds, and it is the reason the wheel at Fennmere went to a hundred and eighty-one on a morning when there was no water in the race, and the reason the cooperative could not get a figure for its own draw into a district hand.

To make it to specification is not a hole in a field. It is nineteen more rods of twelve inch dug in ground that has thirty inches of gravel over flints, with a new cutting, a new head wall, a new outfall, and a licensed man to sign it, and the district's own clause three leaves the date to somebody else and does not say which somebody.

And the leak at the six o'clock corner cannot be mended. That is the part he had been walking toward for nine days and had not said.

"You cannot take the sacking off a wound that is passing water at load," he said, to nobody, at the ninth hour, on his own three acres, to a woman of fifty-one who was not there. "You cannot get at it and you cannot pack it and you cannot dress it. The only way to take the load off a wound is to stop the pump."

"And if you stop the pump you stop the bed," said Ilsa Kern, who had been standing at the head of the ditch with a bar in her hand for some time and had not announced herself, because that is what she does. "And if you stop the bed the Longneck goes, and the Longneck is eleven trays out of the trust's own material and it is already leaning eleven degrees."

"Yes."

"And the leak is five-eighths and it is going into the housing and the housing is doing the only job it has got, and the pit is not rising."

"Yes."

"Then write that down," said Ilsa Kern. "Not the arithmetic. The other thing. I have read a book in this yard this season that said *the pit is not rising* and meant that it was the good news, and the man who wrote it had four feet of iron water in it."

Marek went back in and wrote it down, and it is the seventh of the six notes under section nine of the charter schedule, in the same book on the same pages, unnumbered, unfunded, dated, signed by nobody but Marek, and it closes with the same sentence that the other six close with.

*This is worth nothing against a cut.*

It is the second of the seven that is not about a cut, because the sixth was the compost. It is the first of the seven that is a measurement, and a reader in four years is going to want to know why the other six are about a cut, and I would like the answer to be on the page before somebody has to ask me for it in a room.

Underneath it, in the same hand, four lines:

*Twenty-second day, seventh hour, to the tenth hour. Return main as built, twenty-two rods of eight inch. Specification, forty-one rods of twelve inch. Making up the difference is nineteen rods of new cutting with a new head wall and a new outfall and a licensed signature, on ground of thirty inches of gravel over flints, and clause three of the charter leaves the date to somebody else and does not say which somebody.*

*Seepage at the six o'clock corner, five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, two rules, at the seventh hour. Pump on. It is a leak. It was a leak on the fourteenth of a month and it has not been mended and it cannot be mended under sacking at load, because the only way to take the load off is to stop the pump, and stopping the pump stops the bed at three and a half degrees with a row of eleven trays in it.*

*Entered by M. Vale, engineer of record, against the interest of the application. A chapter condition is a thing somebody has to do and this is it.*

---

The rider went to Fennmere on the twenty-third at about the fourth hour in the afternoon, carried by a boy off a load of slates that was going the other way, and it was two sheets and it asked for two things and the second thing was the smaller one.

The first was the district works schedule extract, all ninety rows of it, with the columns.

He had wanted that since the sixth. He had asked Perrin Osse for it twice and been told twice, in a clerk's square hand, that the district will not produce the table to anybody, because the table carries a column saying whether the work is Crown-held and producing it is an admission the district does not make to a body holding no charter. **Nobody hid anything. That was the point, and it was written in a register at a counter in a clerk's own hand, and it was true.**

There is a charter now. It was made at two o'clock on the twenty-first of a month by a hearing officer with four districts on her circuit, and it names this body, and it is nailed to the seed-house door under a copper tag that has been on that door since the eighteenth. The ground the refusal stood on went out from under the other answer two days ago, at about two in the afternoon and nobody had to decide anything for it to go.

The second thing was four lines long and it asked the district to fix the date that clause three leaves open, **and to say, in fixing it, what the specification is.**

That was not a request. That was Marek finding out, on the twenty-third of a month, that a clause which leaves a date to somebody else is a clause that makes the somebody else answerable to the district, and that he would rather be asked now than be given a date in the middle of a season with a mill board and a log and a hearing officer in the room.

They read it back before it went, at the table, in public, with the hour on it, and Anneke Roos initialled the first three lines and refused to initial the last two, for the reason she gave the last time, which is that initialling an opinion about what a man intends to do with a date would let a stranger in six weeks say the clerk and the witness stood behind it.

The answer came on the twenty-seventh, four days later, which is faster than four days is.
