# Chapter 51

## Nobody Moves

The return line falls due on the first of next month and the first of next month was seven days off, and the line is for something that has to be put in the return main, and there was nothing in this holding to put in it.

Four hundred hundredweight a month is the specification. Four shillings a hundredweight is what bought compost costs at the water-meadow farm, and four hundred hundredweight at four shillings is eighty pounds a month, and the standing specification against the twenty-two rods of eight inch that was actually built is three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight short, which is seventy-nine pounds sixteen. That is the number in Sera Quill's own hand on a license sheet that has never been filed anywhere, and it is carried forward on a charter clause and not discharged.

The March's tin has been in the press under the ledger since the nineteenth, on a bed at three and a half degrees, and on the twenty-fourth it is thirty-five days at risk, and nobody in the seed house has undertaken to move it and the trust has not asked.

Anneke Roos put the levy up on the twenty-fourth at about the fifth hour, at the seed-house table, on the same paper, in the same hand, with the same fourteen signatures, and it is the third time in a season.

Nobody moved.

"There is no motion," said Dunmar Fitch, at the fourth hour and a quarter, reading the paper the way he reads everything, which is twice. "I have been a clerk for nineteen years. I am going to say the thing that is true and then I am going to stop, and if I stop before anybody has spoken I am not doing it for effect. There is no motion."

"Somebody has to move it," said Marek.

"No. Somebody has to move it and nobody has, and the paper has come back with fourteen signatures on it the way it always does, and I want it noted that fourteen signed again and that the two households who did not sign last time have not signed this time either, and the two who put nothing in last time have put nothing in again, and the woman with the shilling has put the shilling in and written the same three words under it that she wrote last time."

The sum, when the counting was done, was four pounds five.

Which is the same sum the same sixteen houses raised ten days ago, on a paper with the same fourteen names on it, and it went into that return main on the fourteenth of a month, and it is now gone, and the same houses have just produced the same sum again on a worse morning, and nobody in the yard could stand in front of a table and explain the difference.

"It is not that they have less," said Tova Reed. She had been at the far end of the table with the seed annex open in front of her and she had not been listening to any of it. "I want to say that before somebody in this room does, because somebody in this room is about to. It is not that they have less. It is the same sixteen houses and the same money and the same fortnight, and there is a thing in this holding now that was not in it three weeks ago, and the thing is a certificate on a door."

Nobody said anything.

"It costs four pounds five to feed a wound," she said. "It costs nothing at all to be a charter holder, and the charter has made this holding legible, and legible holdings get charged for things. It is not a robbery. Nobody has written to anybody. It is a category."

Marek counted the money twice and entered the entry with the hour and the lateness, which was nil, and read it back.

*Twenty-fourth day, fifth hour, to the sixth. Return line due on the first of next month. Specification four hundred hundredweight a month. Nothing available to put in the return main. Sum raised on a levy, sixteen households, fourteen signatures, four pounds five. Short of the last levy by nothing, and short of the specification by three hundred and ninety-nine hundredweight, which is seventy-nine pounds sixteen. To the body: nothing. Licensed person present: none. No motion was moved. The paper was returned with fourteen signatures.*

The last sentence was Dunmar Fitch's and he made Marek read it back twice.

---

The sixteen households of Lantern Reach paid the levy. The levy does not reach Fennmere, and the levy was never supposed to, and this is the sentence that has been sitting in the middle of this yard since the twenty-first of a month without anybody standing up and saying it out loud.

There are forty-one houses at the bottom of the low quarter and four of their wells went off between the twenty-first and the twenty-fifth, and three of them are still off or worse than they were, and a schedule of damage for thirty-nine houses at one pound ten for fourteen days and two public pumps at a pound stands at sixty pounds ten shillings in a file in Fennmere, and the body it was served on is the body last responsible for the supply to that ground, which is this one, which is a body of sixteen households, none of which is in the low quarter and all of which is four feet of clay and nine miles from a standpipe.

"There are sixteen houses on this levy and forty-one houses on that bill," said Anneke Roos. "I have written both numbers in this book this season and I have never once written them in the same line, and I did not do that on purpose, and I noticed it about a month ago, and I have been not-noticing it since."

"And the fourth pound five is being put into a wound in a well nine miles from the people that bill is for," said Marek.

"Yes."

"So the people who lost the water are not being asked for the four pounds, and the people being asked for the four pounds did not lose the water, and there is no form on which either of those two facts can be written down together." He put his hand flat on the page, which is what he does. "Then the honest entry is that the levy is a levy, and the bill is a bill, and this body has no instrument that reaches either of them, and I would like it in the book in those words before anybody in this room works out a way of spending the four pounds five so that it looks like it does."

Dunmar Fitch wrote it in those words.

---

The paper itself was read out afterward, because in this building the paper is always read out and a sum nobody can check is a sum that is only ever a figure in a conversation.

Sixteen houses. Fourteen signatures. Nine of them against a round share, and five with nothing in the line, which is the same five as three weeks ago, and which is the whole of the news.

Two of the fourteen put nothing in and put nothing in this time either. They are the two that Marek taught to read a dial, in a year he does not put in the book, and neither of them has a share in this holding any more and neither of them has said so, and they sign because there is a paper going round and the paper comes to a door. One of them is a woman of about sixty and one of them is her brother and they did not speak on the road and they signed in the order they always sign in, which is hers first.

One put a shilling in and wrote three words under it. The three words are the same three words she wrote last time and Dunmar Fitch has them in his own book because he copies everything anybody writes on a piece of paper in this yard, and they are *and I am sorry*, and nobody has ever asked her what she is sorry for and Dunmar Fitch has never once printed the three words in a book that went out of this yard.

Two signed first, before anybody else, and one of those two is the woman with the shilling.

Two of the sixteen did not sign. Their names are not recorded, because the paper forbade it, and it forbade it in Lissa Vale's hand and Lissa Vale wrote the forbidding there herself and has never once said she would have written it differently.

"So the sum is the same and the roster is the same and the two who signed first signed first again," said Marek, "and I have written that down, and I want to say the other thing and I am saying it because nobody else in this room is going to."

He put the paper flat.

"There is no explanation for this in this building. It is the same sixteen houses, the same money, the same fortnight, and four pounds five is what four pounds five was three weeks ago, and three weeks ago we put it in a wound and it was enough to be worth writing down. The only thing that has happened in this holding since then is that a charter went on a door, and I do not know how to get from that sentence to this number, and I have looked, and I would like it on the page that I looked and did not find it, because in a year somebody is going to work it out and I would rather the book said I was looking early than late."

"It is not a reason," said Dunmar Fitch. "It is an observation, and observations are the only thing in a book that a district can take away and check and do nothing about, and that is why I enter them. Put it in as an observation. Do not put a cause on it. If you put a cause on this paper, some clerk in nine years will read the cause and not the four pounds five."

---

She refused the map on the second evening of the twenty-fourth and she refused it in a way that cost her something, and Marek did not know it was costing her something until the spring, when it was too late to do anything about it.

It came up the March road with a cart of nothing in particular, on a Thursday, in a fold of oiled paper tied with string, addressed to the Salt March Dryfoot Trust of Cray, for the attention of the steward, and marked *for check against the seed annex, two copies wanted, returned by the same hand.*

It was a Crown seed-route map of the Tarrow fen. Form S/4. A printed sheet, folded twice, about two feet by three when open, in five colors that had not been bright in a long time, with a scale in the corner and a legend in a box at the bottom with nine marks in it and a sentence under the box that said the map was compiled for the use of seed holders and traders and was not a survey and did not show every line.

Tova Reed read it at the seed-house table from about the sixth hour and by the eighth she had checked every variety on the annex against the four cultivation districts printed on it, and then she went to the door and called Marek by the name he does not like being called in front of a yard.

"No," she said.

"It is a check. It is for checking."

"No," she said. "Look at what it is for checking. It is compiled for the use of seed holders and traders. That is a market. It is a map made for people who buy seed, and it does not show where a line of wild root runs, because nobody trading in seed pays for that, and the nine marks in the legend are nine things a buyer needs to know and every one of them is about water."

"Then we will check the varieties on it and send it back."

"You cannot check varieties on it," said Tova Reed, "because it does not name any variety. It names four districts, and each district has a number against it, and the number is the district's estimate of what that ground will grow, and the estimate was made by a man in Auberron from returns, and a return is what somebody says they shipped."

She put her hand flat on it, which she does, and she was quiet for long enough that the lamp came on.

"I am going to say a thing and I want it written down in my own name and not in the trust's name, and I want it read back, and I do not want Mr Vale to say anything clever afterwards." She waited until Dunmar Fitch had the pen. "On the first of every month the trust is to be told what this bed is doing. That is the fourth term of the annex and it goes to Cray and it is in a public ledger and anybody may copy it. I put that term in myself, in my own hand, against the trust's interest, because I do not want to be asked again."

"And the map is the other half of that clause," Marek said.

"The map is the other half of that clause and it is nine years old and it is not mine," said Tova Reed, "and it is going to the March, because I have to write to the trust and tell them we hold it, and I am going to tell them what I am going to do with it first, and here is what I am going to do with it: I am going to keep it, and I am going to keep it in my case, and I am going to look at it every week of the season, and if the dry-land line ever comes back through a relay I am going to look at it and see whether the March is on it."

"That is not an answer to the map."

"It is the only answer to the map there is, and it takes eleven years," said Tova Reed, "and I have been doing it since I was twenty, and I want that entered and I want the year I started entered, and I want it entered as a refusal and not as a plan, because a plan is something this trust can be asked about later and a refusal is something it has already answered."

Dunmar Fitch read it back. Marek said nothing clever afterwards.

She took the map away at about the ninth hour and put it in her case with the annex and the March's own figures, and the case has a lock on it and the key is on her belt, and the key was on her belt in Volume 01 too, and nobody in this yard has ever asked her what else is in it.

---

Marek did not see the map again for two days.

He was out on the twenty-sixth at the seventh hour with a rule and a second rule because the number had moved since the fourteenth, and the number that had moved was the one thing in the yard that nobody was allowed to enjoy, and the man who had been the loudest about the pulse in the twelfth week of the season was the man who could not be shown a trend.

The corner had gone to five-eighths over a two rules and twelve days. The sacking under his hand was wet through and cold and there was a small run coming out of the corner into the housing at a rate you could see if you put a finger in it and did not need a rule for.

"Put your finger there again," said Jonas Byre.

"I have my finger there."

"You have had your finger there for forty seconds and you have not said a number," said Jonas Byre. "You are doing the thing you did on the twentieth, which is that you would rather hold a wound than write it down."

Marek took his finger out, and wrote the number down, and it was the same number.

"The pit is not rising," said Jonas Byre.

"The pit has four feet of iron water in it and it is not up a quarter of an inch, and I am going to write that it has four feet and is not up a quarter, because the other way round sounds like good news and it is not the good news. The good news is that the housing is doing the only job it has. The bad news is in the corner under my hand and I cannot get at it while the pump runs, and I cannot stop the pump until the eleventh trays are out, and the eleventh trays are out when the Longneck is out, and the Longneck is out in a week, and I have written all of that down, and now I am going to write the number down again because you asked me to and because you are right to ask."
