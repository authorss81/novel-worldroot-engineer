# Chapter 54

## Sunday

Nothing can be done on a Sunday in Tarrow Fen and there is no version of this building in which anybody pretends otherwise, so the twenty-seventh was a day of paper, and the paper was one page with a hand on it, and nine people looked at it in a seed house over nine hours and got nowhere, and the whole of what came of the day was the second thing they did instead.

Gael Senn was the one who found the thing, at about the second hour of the afternoon, and she found it by accident, and she was twenty-three, and she said afterwards that she did not want it and that she would have gone without it.

"It is the same hand as the other page," she said.

"Which other page."

"The one below it. Row six and row nine." She had the certified sheet flat on the table with a lamp on it and one finger, and her finger did not touch the paper. "The one above it is a different hand. It is slower. Row six is a quick even hand and row nine is a quick even hand and they are the same hand, and the hand in the seventh column of row six is the hand in the seventh column of row nine, and it is the hand a clerk uses."

Marek had been looking at the page for nine hours. He looked at it for another hour with a woman of twenty-three pointing at it and he could not see it and he said so, out loud, twice, and it is in the entry for the twenty-seventh because he asked for it to be.

"I have been reading one line for nine hours," he said. "That is what nine hours does. I would have got there on the second hour if I had not wanted to get there."

Gael Senn drew back. "It is not a discovery. It is what I do. I read twice and I do not trust a number one man has read once, and I have never trusted a hand either. When a person fills in a column every day for eleven years, the hand is not the man's. The hand is the job. You would know that if you had read seed orders for thirty years, and you have read seed orders for four."

Lissa Vale, from the end of the table, said: "She is right and it does not help us and both of those are true."

"Then what does help us," said Marek.

"Nothing today," said Lissa Vale. "The original is the thing that would help us and the original is not produced and we are not entitled to it, and being entitled to it is what you would be entitled to it for. Go and measure your corner."

Marek went and got his coat.

"You do not want to hear this," he said, "because you are right and I am going to be tiresome about it. I have thought about it for nine hours instead of reading the page. That is what I did with today. I want to write to Perrin Osse."

"Not today you do not," said Dunmar Fitch.

"She is a clerk at a counter and she has twice told us in writing that the register behind the page is not hers to produce and not ours to have. I am not asking her for it. I am asking her whether there is another form that would reach it. There might be. And if there is, we have a name for a thing inside a year instead of a sentence about a name."

"There is another form," said Dunmar Fitch. "There is always another form. That is not the difficulty and you know it is not the difficulty, because you have had it in the front of your mind since the third hour and you have been carrying it round this yard all day like a man carrying a bucket. The difficulty is that a district office counts the forms it has had from a body, and a body that has asked twice has told the office that asking works. You asked once. She answered inside four days and she told you the ground of the refusal had gone. If you go back with a second form on Monday, then in a year somebody in that building will read two forms from this body and remember that the first one produced a page. And the second one will produce nothing at all, on purpose."

---

Marek measured his corner at the seventh hour, because the sun is up until about half past three in the last week of that month and a rule does not read itself.

The seepage at the six o'clock corner was five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, two rules, pump on and continuous, and it was half an inch and a sixteenth on the fourteenth of a month, which is thirteen days back, and it had gone one sixteenth in thirteen days, and a hundredweight of compost is worth about half a degree on a bed and a degree is worth about a foot of head on this ground, and twenty-one hundredweight and a quarter was put into that return main on the fourteenth, so somebody in this yard could work out what a hundredweight of compost is worth at the six o'clock corner of a sunwell housing and come up with a number.

He did not work it out and he entered why he had not, in the book, at the hour, and the reason is the best sentence he has written in a season.

*Twenty-seventh day, seventh hour, in daylight. Seepage at the six o'clock corner, five-eighths of an inch, twice measured, two rules, pump on and continuous. Half an inch and a sixteenth on the fourteenth, at the seventh hour, in daylight, pump on and continuous. One sixteenth in thirteen days, and the day before the fourteenth there were nineteen hours of flood on the head of the same node and I have not made a figure of what that did, and there are two hundred and nineteen nine-yard lengths of ditch in this holding and I do not know how many of them are wet tonight.*

*I am not going to write a rate. A rate is a number somebody can use and I have two readings, and the readings are thirteen days apart, and the two ends of them are different kinds of day. If anybody wants a number here they may come and take the third reading at a stated hour on a stated load, and it will cost somebody a license, and I will help them ask for it. Cause not established. Not mended, because it cannot be mended under sacking at load, and the only way to take the load off is to stop the pump, and stopping the pump stops the bed, and the bed is at three and a half degrees with eleven trays in a drill that is still full of water. It is a leak. It was a leak on the fourteenth and it is a leak today.*

*Entered by M. Vale, engineer of record, against the interest of the application. A charter condition is a thing somebody has to do and this is it, and what somebody has to do about it is write it down twice.*

And under the six-o'clock corner measurement, in a different hand and a smaller one, Gael Senn wrote *I measured the second figure with the second rule and I asked for it to be written that I did, and Mr Vale read it back to me before he signed it.*

---

The Sunday post came at about the fourth hour and the post comes once a week on this road, and it came up the lane on a cart that was doing four other things.

Two of the packets were for the seed house and one of them was a bill for oil, and the fourth was addressed to a counting house nine hundred miles away and had come eight hundred.

**To the Salt March Dryfoot Trust, of Cray. For the attention of the steward. By the district office, Tarrow.**

It was one sheet, and it was a form, and the form had a number at the top, and Marek read the number before he read the form and did not know what it was, and read the form and then knew.

*Water-supply bylaw, second part, form C/4. Records held by a party. To the holder of a charter, or to a party to the seed arrangements of a chartered node, or to both.*

*Whereas the seed arrangements of the node at Lantern Reach are by the annex of the ninth of a month a condition of the charter of the twenty-first, and the Salt March Dryfoot Trust is a named party to that annex; and whereas it is proposed to renew or vary the said charter; now therefore the district requires of the above-named party, within fourteen days of the date of this notice, any record in its possession or under its control touching the node, its head, its branches, the water it has drawn, the seed held or grown upon it, or the ground supplied by it, and requires a copy of the same for the district file, and will supply a copy of anything produced to any other party to the annex.*

Marek read it twice and then said, to a table with five people at it, in the flattest voice he had:

"Condition four of a charter. The annex is a condition. The trust is a named party. And the annex's own fourth term is that a copy of everything goes to Cray whether or not it helps."

"That term is a condition of the charter," said Dunmar Fitch. "It is on the fifth sheet. It is in district charter law. I have read it four times this month trying to find a hole in it and there is not one and I have looked for one because I did not want it there."

Nobody in the yard had thought about the fifth sheet. It had been argued for nine minutes on the twenty-first of a month by a hearing officer with four districts on her circuit, on the grounds that a book which can record a thing that did not happen is worth more than a book which cannot, and Lissa Vale had won the argument in nine minutes without raising her voice, and then everybody had gone out into a yard and looked at nineteen acres of standing wheat.

"A copy of everything goes to Cray," said Tova Reed, "and the counting house at Cray receives it, and Cray is where the March buys, and where the March buys is where people who buy seed hear about a fen. And now the district has written to a counting house and asked it for everything it has about us, and offered to send that counting house everything we have about it, and offered, in the same sentence, to send that copy to any other party to the annex."

She set both hands flat on the form.

"There is a word for that in our trade," she said, "and the word is *route*, and the word has been in use for four hundred years, and nobody has ever in four hundred years seen a district use one."

"You are saying this is an attack," said Marek.

"I am saying it is a form," said Tova Reed, "and I have known a great many forms that were also attacks, and the ones that were also attacks were the ones where somebody got to choose the timing. This one arrived on a Sunday post, addressed to a party nine hundred miles away, and it came to this yard before it came to Cray, and I want that written down as a fact about the fen and not as a fact about the district, because I do not yet know which it is, and in fourteen days there will be a copy in the district file and I will not be able to say."

Anneke Roos had come in from the low acre in the wet and had been standing at the door with her coat running on the boards and nobody had made her take it off, and she said the sentence that decided the evening.

"The date is going to be the fourteenth of a month and the first examination under this charter is the first of next month," she said. "So we have got a form that leaves the district in the middle of next month and a form that ends a charter at the end of the season, and I would like somebody in this room to tell me which of those two is the one we should be frightened of, because in this yard we have spent a season being frightened of the wrong one on purpose, because the other one was a date we could not get at, and now we can get at both."

---

They did the arithmetic after she had gone, because it does not get done in front of a person who has just said something that made the room go quiet.

A form of that class goes to a named party. The named party is in Cray. Cray is nine hundred miles away by the March road and by the ordinary cart road it is a hundred and eleven miles further, because the March road is not a road, it is a line of about forty farms with a good surface on eleven miles of it.

Out: two days, because the Thursday post is not optional and has not been since before Nia Vale left on a hired cart. Back: two days. The district allows fourteen. **Ten days of slack on a form dated by the fourteenth of a month**, and a cart that had gone down on the Thursday would have been at the far end of the March road on the second day and could have turned round and been at the Fennmere counter on the fifth, and nine of the fourteen would be gone in three.

"That is not a coincidence and I do not think it is a threat," said Dunmar Fitch. "I think it is a district with four things on one form and a cart that goes when carts go. I have run this yard's paper for nineteen years and a form is a form, and a form with a date on it does not care what a cart wants to do. I am writing that down because I have spent this season being frightened of things and I would like the record to show at least one occasion on which I refused to be."

"You do not think it is a threat," said Anneke Roos.

"I think the district would be within its rights and would lose nothing by the date and has known the date for four days," said Dunmar Fitch, "and I think it wrote fourteen because fourteen is the ordinary period and I have never seen that office write a short one, and I think if I write *this was a threat* in that book and I am wrong, then in three years somebody will use my sentence about a threat to say that this holding is a frightened holding, and that is a thing a broker would say at a gate, and there is one of those coming back at the end of the season."

Nobody wrote *threat*. Anneke Roos initialled the line about not writing it, and then she asked for a line of her own.

"Entered by A. Roos," she said. "I asked that the word *threat* be written and not entered, and I am not withdrawing the asking. I have spent a season watching men in this yard be careful with words so that they could sleep at night, and I have never been sorry and I am not going to be, and if a district clerk in nine years wants to know what this holding was like in the winter, she can read a book that is frightened and not a book that is careful, and she can make her own mind up about which of the two it was."

Lissa Vale read that back and signed it, and then she squared the form to the edge of the table and did not say anything, and the lamp was on and the Sunday was going and the fieldbook was on the middle of the table with the use log beside it and the press standing open on the wall with the March's tin under the ledger at thirty-five days.
