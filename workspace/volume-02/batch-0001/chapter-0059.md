# Chapter 59

## The Column With A Hand In It

The reader came up the March road on the second of the month in the middle of the afternoon, and Marek had put out for her on the Thursday before, and the putting-out took eleven minutes, and the reason it took eleven minutes is that nobody in the yard could agree on what she was being paid.

The clause is a condition of a charter. Where a charter is conditional and provision is made for examination at the term, and provision is made for the holder to require the assistance of a person in the district water record, the cost of that assistance is the holder's, and the holder may not name the person.

So Tova Reed wrote the two paragraphs about the Dryfoot Trust at the counting-house table in Cray, and they went on the Thursday's post, and they said this: the district has asked the trust, as a named party to the seed arrangements of a chartered node, for any record in its possession touching the node; the annex's fourth term requires a copy of everything to be sent to Cray; and the district has offered to send the trust a copy of anything the trust produces. The trust therefore holds, or will hold, a copy of the seed annex, the standing pressure log, the monthly reports, the use log, and a certified page of a district register of authorisations, and the trust does not know which of those things the fen's own paper is about to enter in a ledger.

And the trust would be obliged to answer.

She said all of that to the seed-house table on the Friday, out loud, and read it back, and then she said: "I am asking for a reader. Not a rootwright. There is nothing in the node that needs a rootwright; the node has been read twice by two licensed people and they did not agree and the officer declined to decide between them. I am asking for somebody who can look at a hand and tell me whether it is the same hand, and I am asking for her out of the district water record, and there is one woman on that record within a day's cart of this road and she has been a seed steward for thirty years and she is seventy-one years old."

Lissa Vale made the tea, which she did not do for the broker, and did not do for the contractor, and did for the reader.

Her name was Osvalde Bream and she had kept the crop histories of eleven holdings in three districts for thirty-one years, out of a bookshop in a town with a market cross and a good bakehouse, and she had never been licensed for anything, and she said so in the first minute.

"Eleven holdings, thirty-one years, one book each, and the books are in a room upstairs and they are not in Cray and they are not in this district's water record and they are not going to be." She put her hand flat on the seed-house table. "I will come. I will not bring the books and I will not take a fee. I will look at a page and I will say what I see and it will be what I see and not what anybody wants, and if that is not worth anything to you then you have wasted a day's cart and I will go back on the Thursday post with somebody else's business."

"It is worth a day's cart," said Lissa Vale.

"Two things before I look at it," said Osvalde Bream. "One. I read hands, not names. A hand is not a man and a name is not a decision. A man can write a thing he has been told to write and it will look exactly like a man writing a thing he has decided to write, and thirty-one years of seed orders is the only qualification I have and I have never pretended it was more. Two. I will say who wrote it. I will not say why, I will not say what it was for, and I will not say whether anybody made him do it, and if a room asks me any of those three I will get up and go back down the road, and I would rather say that now than at the end of a day."

Nobody asked her any of the three.

---

She read the sixth column of the certified page for about eleven minutes and nobody in the room said a word.

"It is a clerk's hand," she said at last. "I said that on Thursday in a yard before I had looked at it, and I was wrong, and I am a slow woman with bad eyesight and I am not going to pretend to be anything else in a house I have been asked into."

"So Gael Senn was right," said Marek.

"The girl was right about the two rows and wrong about the column, and she was right about the column in this," said Osvalde Bream, and she turned the sheet a quarter turn and set her thumbnail on the top of the fifth entry in the sixth column. "A clerk's hand is even. It is even because a clerk's hand has to be even, because the next clerk has to read it. This is even in nine entries out of ten. Look at the fifth."

Marek looked at the fifth. The fifth was in a different slope and the second half of the word *schedule* was leaning about four degrees to the left of the first half, and the tail of the *c* was doubled back on itself as though the nib had been dragged.

"That is a man writing fast," said Osvalde Bream, "or a man writing in a hurry, or a man writing a thing he is putting into a book he does not want to have written. It is one of the three and I do not know which and it is not in the wood and it is not on this page and it is not going to be in anything you can get out of a district in a year."

She looked at it for a while longer.

"It is his hand," she said. "It is Aldren Vale's hand, and I will put my name to that, and I have not seen it for eight years and I have not seen it in a book for eight years and I have not seen it since the week before the shaft. It is his hand at the bottom of a page of seed orders, written at about the speed a man writes when the lamp is going and he is thinking about a wire. It is his hand on the third of November, the year before, on a sheet of order paper for a hundred and forty rods of twelve inch, and he made the W in *wire* the way he made the W in *weir*, and I have never in thirty-one years seen another hand make a W that way."

The room was very quiet.

"He was a root engineer," said Tova Reed. "He was the best in the district and you had seed orders from him for three seasons."

"He had seed orders from me for three seasons," said Osvalde Bream. "I did not have seed orders from a root engineer. I had them from the man who came to my back door and wrote down what I said he should grow, in his own hand, at about the speed of a man writing while he is thinking about a wire, and I have not had one since the week before the shaft, and I came here expecting a bad page of a district's handwriting, and you have had me a day's cart for a man I buried."

The date on the row was the first of September of the year he went.

"Order date," said Dunmar Fitch, reading it from the certified page with the pen in his hand and not writing. "The first of September. Seven months before the shaft."

"Two dates," said Marek. "One is the first of September, and that is seven months before he went in. The other one is on the last page of a fieldbook in a press on the inside wall of this building, and it is a year and eight months after he went in, and I wrote *probably a copying error, to be treated as one* into my own finding about it, and I asked for that wording myself, and nobody has tested it and it is still the only protection I have."

"And the two dates are twenty-seven months apart," said Tova Reed, who is the only person at that table whose work is a calendar. "And both of them are in his hand. And the one that is later is in wood, and wood does not have a hand, it has a *setting*, and the setting is a shape, and the shape is what licensed readers have been reading out of cut faces for a season and calling a target pattern and a work order, and a work order is a thing a person writes in advance."

"Which he did," said Marek. "Seven months before he went in. There is a column for his hand in a district register, in an item ordered on the first of September, and there is a *hold* in the same column and the same hand as a date, released on a Monday, and the Monday is nine days before he went into the shaft."

Nobody wrote that down for about a minute and then Dunmar Fitch wrote it down.

---

Lissa Vale asked the only question anybody in that yard wanted to ask, and she asked it before it could be asked, and she asked it in the wrong direction, and nobody in the room understood what she had done until she had finished.

"You will want to know," she said, "whether that is his hand or a copy of his hand. And I am not going to be asked, and I am not going to ask you, and I want it entered that I said that before anybody in this room had time to want it and after I had had about an hour to want it myself, and I am not going to be thanked for it either, because I have had the hour and I did not spend it on that."

"Mrs Vale—" said Marek.

"You are not going to be asked," said Lissa Vale, "by anybody in this building, and if a district officer asks you in a room in a year you will say the same thing and I will have said it first and in my own name and not in yours. A name in a book costs this building. It cost us the clean record on the second of a month, and it cost us three acres on the sixteenth, and it cost a woman her day rate in a market she is not in. It is going to cost us one more thing and I would like it entered that I do not know what that thing is yet and I have decided to be the one who says so before the yard works it out."

And then she sat down, and put her hands in her lap, and did not say anything else for the rest of the afternoon, and it was not the same silence as Sera Quill's silence on a bench in the mud, and it is not the fourth thing she has refused in six weeks, and it is not numbered, and nobody in that yard ever asked her about it.

---

What the reader did in the last hour of the day was not what anybody had brought her for.

"The first question is answered," she said, "and the answer is a name, and the name is worth nothing by itself, because the district certified the form of that page and not the column, and because it is a transcript, and because the original is the thing nobody produces. So you have a document that says a piece of ground was authorised, and a document that says the authorisation is a hand, and the two documents do not sit in the same book. You will not get the third document. I have been in eleven holdings and I have never seen the third document and I have stopped expecting it."

"Then what did you come for," said Marek.

"I came because the trust has your books," said Osvalde Bream, "and a counting house is a very different thing from a seed house, and I wanted to see the thing in front of the paper."

She asked for the variety ledger, and Lissa Vale gave it to her, and she read about eleven minutes, and then she stopped and read again for about four, and then she shut it and squared it to the edge of the table.

"There is a bean out of a place called the Ashcombe," she said, "and there is an oat, and there is a thing they call here a Reed's Acre rye, which is not a rye and is not anybody's. Three. I have never seen any of them in any book anywhere, and I have been in thirty-one years of other people's books, and they are not in the printed index either, and the printed index has four hundred names on it and I have read the whole of it twice this afternoon because it is in the room and I am seventy-one and I do not sleep."

"Where do you know them from," said Tova Reed.

"I do not," said Osvalde Bream. "I know what their letters look like. I have read seed orders out of eleven holdings for thirty-one years and a man who has got a variety he has not got another of will write a name he has made up or borrowed or misremembered, and a woman who has had a line for forty years writes it a particular way, and every one of those three on that page is written in the way of a person who is reporting something she has been given and does not know is wrong."

She put her hand on the ledger.

"I am not saying they are wrong. I have no way of saying they are wrong and neither has the trust and neither has the index and neither has this fen. I am saying that if you wanted to buy a line out of that book you could not tell, and that is not a matter of a shilling a line. It is a matter of a book."

Lissa Vale took a sheet of seed-house paper and wrote the three names on it in her own hand, and under them she wrote *read out by O. Bream, seed steward, of the Ashcombe, on the fourth of this month, from the seed house's own ledger, and entered here against the ledger and not in it*, and then she read it back twice, and then she put the sheet on the middle of the table beside the fieldbook and the use log where anybody may look at it, which is where things go in that building now, and neither of them asked the other for anything.

Tova Reed did not go to her case.

"Next week," she said. "And I will not be bringing the map, and I am not going to be able to tell you tonight whether I am keeping it or giving it away, and I would rather tell you that than decide in front of a stranger."
