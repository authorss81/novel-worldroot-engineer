# Chapter 55

## A Shilling A Line

She came up the March road on the twenty-eighth in a cart with a hood on it and one man on the box, and she was forty-four and she had come two hundred miles to be in a seed house for an afternoon, and she said so in the first minute without being asked.

"Vesta Lund. I run the Ashcombe index. It is a list of landraces and where they will grow and what a quarter of a bushel of them fetches, and it is printed every two years and it is bought by seed houses, seed traders, and nine hundred smallholders who cannot afford to find out anything else. I have been doing it twenty-two years. I am not a Crown officer and I am not a licensed rootwright and I do not read rootwood, and I want to say that out loud at the start because in this district everybody says what they are and nobody says what they are not."

She put a folder on the seed-house table, and it was thin, and it had been carried a long way.

"A shilling a line," she said. "Every line of your ledger that names a variety. I do not want your seed and I will not take your seed and there is a reason for that which I will give you for nothing because it costs me nothing. I want the names. The chart, the local name, where it was last grown, and the year somebody last grew it on purpose rather than by accident. That is a line. That is four things and I will pay a shilling for the four."

Tova Reed did not look at the folder.

"You are describing a ledger," she said.

"I am describing four columns of a ledger."

"You are describing a ledger with a price on it and a buyer and a printer in Cray, and the printer in Cray is two streets from my trust's counting house, and I have known that for eleven years and I have said nothing about it in a public room."

"No," said Vesta Lund. "I would not have said it either, and I have not come here to be clever about it. I have come because I have got nine hundred names on my list and four thousand lines in the notes and I cannot reconcile them without somebody who kept a book for thirty years, and there are not many of those left, and every one of them is a long way from anywhere."

"Give me the reason," said Tova Reed. "Not the polite one. The one you would put in a book."

Lund looked at her for a moment and then decided to give it.

"Eleven years ago I was in a village of about four hundred people on the west side and a man's grandfather had a bean in a tin that nobody else on that road had. I put the name in my index with the road and the parish and the year. Eighteen months later the seed trade had it, and there was a price on it, and it was a good price, and the old man sold his whole year's saving of it and did very well and died in a warm house. And in the third year the trade wanted a volume and there was no bean on that road any more and the price went, because a price is not for a bean, a price is for a bean you can get again." She shut the folder. "Your ledger is the only reason that bean is not currently the most expensive thing in four counties. And I know that because I have been in a village in the west where it was not true, and I did not cause it and I did not notice it for two years, and I have never told anybody that sentence until this afternoon."

Nobody at the table said anything for about half a minute.

"You are right," said Tova Reed. "And I am going to refuse you, and I am going to refuse you in my own name and not in the trust's name, and I am going to give the reason, and it is not a good reason, and I am not going to pretend it is."

She took the folder and did not open it, and she pushed it back across the table.

"The March has been indexed twice," she said. "Once eleven years ago, in my first year, and once six years ago. Both times it was done as a service and both times somebody in Auberron said the words *long-term preservation of regional varietal knowledge* in a room with a window. The first time we got a set of glass plates, a shelf in a dry room in Cray, and our own lines written on them in our own hand, and the shelf is still there. The second time we got a guarantee."

"Which was not a guarantee," said Vesta Lund.

"Which was a guarantee that no copy of the March's varieties would leave the trust without the trust's mark on it." Tova Reed's voice had not risen and had not changed register once. "What we got was a list of four named lines in an appendix to somebody else's book, and no mark on them, and the appendix says the source is the Dryfoot Trust, Cray, and the appendix is now in a printer's file in four counties, and the March's own book has those four names crossed out in a hand that is not any of ours, in a color that is not ours, and the crossing-out is dated, and the crossing-out is a *removal* and a removal is a fact about a book and a book is a thing anybody may copy."

She put her palm on the ledger on the table, which is the seed house's book and is Lissa Vale's to allow, and Lissa Vale allowed it.

"May I say one more thing," she said, "and then I will sit down, and it is about a book and not about you."

"Miss Reed."

"The March was indexed twice. I have told you the second one. The first one was in my first year and it was not an index at all, it was a gentleman from the Crown's own seed office in Auberron who came out on a circuit for three weeks with a clerk and a list, and he was very polite, and he took the trust's lines out of the trust's book one at a time and copied them into a printed table, and he did not put the trust's mark on any of them, and I was twenty years old and I had been a steward for four months, and I let it happen in a room with the windows open because he said the words *long-term preservation* and I believed him."

"Did you not get anything."

"I got a set of glass plates and a shelf in a dry room and it is still there," said Tova Reed, "and the shelf is the reason I know that what he took was not a copy. It is a copy. He copied them wrong. Two of them he copied into the wrong column and one of those is the Ashcombe black oat, and the trust's own book has it right and his book has it wrong, and he printed four hundred copies of his book, and **the Ashcombe black oat is the most widely believed name in this district and it is a man in Auberron's handwriting, not mine.**"

The seed house was quiet enough to hear the lamp.

"So when you come to me with a shilling a line," said Tova Reed, "I am not refusing you because you are buying the wrong thing. I am refusing you because I know the price of the right thing, because I have paid it twice, and both times I was told it was a service, and both times the trust's own book ended up with a hole in it and the hole is still in it, and I have not got over it in eleven years and I do not expect to."

"Was the four lines the same four that were struck," said Vesta Lund.

"I have never seen the appendix," said Tova Reed, "and I am not going to describe four lines to a woman who prints indexes, and if you write down what I have just said I will call it a conversation and I will deny the rest."

"I have not written anything," said Vesta Lund. "I have a bad memory for handwriting and a good one for faces, and yours is going in the second one, and I would like to say that is not a threat. It is the only compliment I have."

"And I am going to ask you one question and then I am going to sit down," said Tova Reed, "and it is not a fair question and I am asking it anyway. Is there a category in your index that you do not print."

Vesta Lund did not answer at once, and the answer she gave was the answer of a woman who has been asked a fair question unfairly and has decided to treat it fairly.

"There are two," she said. "One is a line that is held by one person in one garden and has no name anybody would print, and I do not print those, because a printed name is a claim and a claim is a thing you can be sued on and I have eleven hundred smallholders who have been told that the index is a list of things anybody may grow. It is a list of things you may buy. That is not the same list and I have never corrected it on the paper."

"And the other."

"The other is a line that is held by no one anybody knows of and is grown, and I do not print those either, for the same reason with the sign reversed," said Vesta Lund. "If a line is held by one person and you print it, you have told nine hundred people where it is. If a line is held by nobody and you print it, you have told nine hundred people it can be had. Both of those are on me and not on the holding and I have carried both of them for twenty-two years, and I would be obliged if the woman who has been indexed twice would not tell me today that she approves, because I have had that from a Crown seed office and it cost me nothing to receive and it was worth a great deal to them."

Lissa Vale said, from the end of the table, without moving: "It is entered."

Tova Reed looked at her.

"It is not your line to enter," she said.

"It is my table and my book and I keep the book," said Lissa Vale, "and I have entered it, and I have entered the words *refused to say whether she approves*, because you did not say, and I would rather have that in a book than a sentence in my head for six months."

---

Lund did not leave angry and she did not leave without leaving something, and what she left was a printed sheet folded twice, about two feet by three, in five colors that had not been bright in a long time.

"I was going to post it," she said, standing in the yard. "It is nine years old. It is a seed-route map of the Tarrow fen, form S/4, compiled for the use of seed holders and traders. It shows four cultivation districts, the district estimate against each of them, the holding boundaries the district recognizes, the nine-mile road, the mill, the sub-office, and every watercourse anybody has paid the district to know about. You are meant to check your varieties against it and send it back and I have a hundred and forty of them going out this winter."

"Then you have wasted a sheet," said Marek.

"Mr Vale, I have never in my life seen a district print a thing for the use of seed holders that shows a private boundary and not a public one." Lund put it on the table and squared it. "Look at it before you send it back. Not at the varieties. I do not care about your varieties. Look at whether everything on it is water."

He looked at it at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, with Tova Reed, and it took him ten minutes and the lamp was not on.

The Weir Row branch is on the district's own works schedule. It has three stamped executions against it. It is nine miles of open drain with a weir in it and a cut face at the bottom that a licensed reader read a work order out of in front of an inspector, in mud, in a flood, on the seventeenth of a month, and a whole bench of this yard's records is about it.

It is not on the map.

Not faint, not omitted by a printer's error, not under a smudge. There is a place on that sheet, between the embankment and the old common, where the district has drawn a line, and it is the same weight of line as the other lines, and there is no watercourse on it.

Tova Reed took a pencil — she asked for it, she did not take it — and put the point about two inches to the left of where the branch is, and wrote four words on the face of the map in her own hand, and no longer than that.

*no line here*

---

Lund came to the door for her hood at the fifth hour and looked at the map on the table and did not touch it and said one thing before she went, standing in the gateway with the wet coming across the yard behind her.

"I did not know that branch was there," she said. "I have been in this fen eleven times in twenty-two years and I have never known it was there. I have been reading maps like that one since I was twenty-one and I have never once looked at what is not on one."

She got up on the box and the cart went off up the March road and did not stop anywhere on the way down, and Lissa Vale came out of the seed house and looked at the four words in a strange hand on a Crown-printed sheet, and said, to nobody in particular, that a woman had come two hundred miles to buy a shilling's worth of paper and had left a thing on her table that was worth more than everything the broker had put on it, and then went back in and shut the door.
