# Chapter 57

## A Bead That Answers For The Seed

He came to the gate at about the eleventh hour on the thirtieth with two men and a box of tools and a license card, and he asked whether Coll Renner of Fennmere might look at a pump house, and Marek said the pump house is anybody's, and he came in and stayed until dark.

Forty-one. Licensed on the Rootwright Assembly's roll for the Tarrow district, which has three names on it, and he is the newest of the three, and one of the other two is sixty-one, lives eleven miles up a cart track, and has taken no paid work since his wife died, and is a better reader than anybody in this yard. Nine men in a yard at Fennmere and four of them would do a holding for a season. He had read everything the fen had published, which is the standing pressure log, the use log, the copy going to Cray, and the eleven hundred words of a statement that says its own account of a thing is a rumor with a witness in it, and he said the last one was the reason he was in the yard at all.

"A cooperative that has written down that it cannot prove what it knows," he said, "is the only holding in this district I have ever wanted to work for. I have read that page about nine times and I have read it to two of my own men and one of them has stopped me asking him to sign things without reading them, which I count as a return."

He wanted the method.

Not the secret. He said that first, before anybody had accused him of anything. "There is no secret. Anybody can read your books; that was the point of them. I want the *order of the work*. I want it written down in a form a man in my crew can carry out on a holding he has never seen, on a Tuesday, with a foreman who does not want to be told he is doing it wrong. That is all I want and that is the only thing in this district nobody has, and I have nine men and no way to teach them anything."

"And what do you offer," said Marek.

"Two hundred a year, and the day work, and a yard, and a copper clip that is not a piece of tin and a nail, and the method in writing under my name and my license, which means that if a man uses it and gets it wrong it is my license and not yours, which is the entire commercial value of it to me." Renner set the collar down beside his hand. "I do not want your name on it. If I wanted your name I would offer more money and you would know what I was buying."

Marek did not answer that, and what he thought about for the next half hour was that the man had removed the one objection Marek had ready, and that a man who removes your objection before you have said it is either very good at this or is doing it on purpose, and that Coll Renner of Fennmere has nine men and a yard and has been in this district for eleven years and put up a rain break over a pump house nobody was living in.

---

Then he took the box out and put a thing on the table that was about the length of a finger.

A copper collar, turned, with a bead of living rootwood in it, and the wood was about the size of a hazel nut, and it was in a tin bed of damp sand, and it was not scarroot and it was not an anchor and it was not from this holding.

"Provenance bead," said Renner. "You cut it out of a grafted seed. Graft anything into a seed — a line, a strain, a habit — and the seed keeps what you grafted, in the wood, and where the wood goes the memory goes with it. That is a fact and it is not a mystery and it has been a fact for about four hundred years. What is new is that we can put it in four seconds instead of forty years."

He wet a corner of a paper twist and shook a few grains of something out of it onto the table — an oat, a barley, a bean, small and gray and not worth anything.

"Lay the collar on the lot," he said. "Four seconds. And the lot tells you where it was grown."

He put the collar on the wet paper and the collar went faintly green at the wood, and Marek felt it, in the wood, through his own fingers, where the copper was — a pressure, a direction, a warmth, a shape of about a day's length with a quality he had no word for, four seconds, gone.

"That was a Marrowfield bean," said Renner. "Grown at Marrowfield, two seasons ago, on ground that had been under a different crop the season before that. Now. Another one."

He did it four times. It worked four times. It took four seconds each time and it did not vary and it was, as far as anyone at that table could see, exactly right.

"Well," said Tova Reed.

"Miss Reed has said the word the rest of us are thinking," said Renner. "Yes. And no. It is right, and it is a way of putting your own eyes out."

"Then say the rest of it," said Tova Reed, "because I have watched nine men in this yard look at a thing that works and stop asking questions, and I am not going to be the tenth."

Renner put his hand on the tin.

"A graft keeps the thing you put in and loses the thing that was there," he said. "That is not a fault. It is what the word means. If you graft a habit into a seed to move it a hundred miles, the seed gives up the habit it had where it was born, and what it gives up is not a copy of the old habit, it is the old habit. There is nothing in the seed afterwards that remembers Marrowfield except what I put there." He tapped the collar. "Put a different habit in and the Marrowfield goes. The lot will then tell you exactly and completely that it was grown somewhere else, and it will be as sure about it as a man, and it will be wrong in the way that only a very confident thing is wrong."

---

"I want one thing before I will look at it again," said Tova Reed.

"Ask."

"I have been a seed steward for eleven years and I have kept crop histories since I was nineteen, and there is a line in the March's book that is wrong. I know it is wrong the way you know a face. It is a black oat out of the Ashcombe, and it came in on a cart in a tin with no label on it, and it came in through a man who wanted to be helpful, and it has been in the trust's book for eleven years and it is not the Ashcombe oat and it is not anything anybody can name."

She put her hand out flat.

"That lot is in my case, in a paper twist, in the seed house, four feet from here. Fetch it. And put your collar on it. And do not tell me anything about it first, and do not tell me anything about it after, and I will not ask you a single question about it, and whatever it says, it says, and I will enter it in a book in my own hand with the hour on it."

One of Renner's two men went up the road and back with a paper twist and the collar went on and it went faintly green at the wood and the four seconds went through Marek's fingers in the copper.

And Tova Reed looked at it, and looked at the little gray grains, and did not say a word for about ten seconds.

"Ashcombe," she said. "Ashcombe, and the season before last, and the ground was under a ley."

And then she said, in a completely level voice, to a yard that had gone very quiet: "It is wrong. It has been in our book for eleven years and it is wrong, and you have just told me where it was grown and how, to the field and the season, and it is a better answer than eleven years of arguing, and it cost you four seconds, and it is a lie, and it is not a lie you told, and that is the part I want written down."

---

They read the instrument at about the third hour of the afternoon, on the trestle in the yard, because the seed house would not have it, and Lissa Vale wrote the use-log line before he touched it and read it back, and Renner fitted his own clip and tested the contact with a plain wire gauge and wrote the contact down before he went on, and the three lines came off the copper seam in the time it takes to say them.

> A bead will hold what is grafted into it.
>
> It will not hold what was there first.
>
> Put your name on which of the two you are keeping.

That is what it said, and it is the instrument's and not a person's, and there is no more of it, and it is not a reason and it is not an authority, and it has not gone into a book in this yard in anybody's hand and it is not going to.

Renner did not ask to copy it and Marek did not offer it and neither of them mentioned it again that week, and nobody in that yard put it into a document, because the rule about that had been made on the fourteenth in front of six people and it was the best rule anybody in Tarrow Fen had ever made.

---

The second demonstration was on the March's own rye, in a flowerpot, on a trestle, in the yard, at about the fifth hour, with the light going.

"Two things and then I am going," said Renner. "I am not being sentimental about this and I would like it entered that I said so. This pot is going in a field in the morning and I am going to do the graft, because I want to see it done once by a pair of hands that are not mine and I would like it recorded, and you are the only pair of hands in this district that has ever grafted anything."

"And the license," said Marek.

"Is mine, and the clip is mine, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that there is not a difference between a man who holds a license and a man who does not, and that the difference is the entire reason I am standing here and you are sitting down." Renner put the collar on the table. "There is no difference at all in what a graft is. That is the part I have never been able to get anybody in an office to understand, and I have wanted to say it out loud in a yard for eleven years."

Lissa Vale entered the line. Gael Senn witnessed it. Dunmar Fitch read it back. The rye went in at ten past five and the graft was made at twenty past and the collar was bedded in damp sand and the pot was carried out to the north annex and put in the ground against the drill, and Renner covered it with a sack and weighted the sack with a stone and said the stone was a stone and not a promise.

The mark came up on Marek's forearm between about the sixth hour and the seventh hour, along the inside of the wrist, where the vein runs, and it was the color of a healed burn and it was not a bruise.

It was a line about four inches long and it branched twice, and it was not straight, and it did not follow a vein.

Lissa Vale saw it first, at about the seventh hour, when she came out with the lamp for the row, and she did not touch it and she did not say anything for long enough that Marek took his sleeve down and put his arm away, and she said: "Leave it out."

"It is nothing," he said.

"It is not nothing and you know the difference between a thing that is nothing and a thing that is a record," said Lissa Vale. "Leave your arm where it is. Mr Renner, come and look at this before you go, and then write down what you see, because you are the licensed man here and I am not and he is not, and the three of us are not going to be able to agree about it later."

Renner looked at it for about two minutes and said it was a rootmark, and that it was a record of what he had connected and not of what it was worth, and that it was permanent, and that it would not wash off, and that he could not stop it and would not have stopped it and was glad of it, and then he took out the book he had been carrying since the gate and wrote four lines and the hour and handed the book to Dunmar Fitch, and Dunmar Fitch read it back before he initialled it, which he does.

And the four lines are in the cooperative's book in the clerk's hand with the licensed man's words in it, and the record is that it happened and the hour and who was standing there, and there is nothing in it about what a man has become, and that is not an oversight. It is the fourth rule this yard has made and it is the only one nobody has ever argued with.

"Do not tell anybody I am pleased," said Coll Renner, at the gate, in the dark. "I am not pleased. I have had one since I was thirty-one and I know what it is for. I have just made one of a man who has no license and no holding and three acres of sour ground, and I have written it down, and it is going to be in a book in a seed house on this road for longer than I am going to be in this district. That is not a favour. That is the bill."

---

The two of them stood in the gateway for about four minutes after that and neither of them said anything about the mark, and the reason they did not is that there was nothing to say about it that the man who had written it had not already said, and both of them knew it, and one of them had paid two hundred pounds' worth of bad nights for it once and the other had not.

Then Renner said: "The books."

"They are on the table."

"I know they are on the table. I read them on Friday." He shifted the box of tools under his arm. "I want the *order* of the work, and I have been trying to get it out of three books and I cannot, and I have known what I wanted since about the second hour this afternoon and I have been standing in your yard pretending I have not."

"Then ask for it plainly and stop getting at me sideways through a seed graft," said Marek.

Renner laughed, which he had not been expecting to do.

"Plainly, then. I want a book that says what a crew does, in what order, on a holding they have never seen, with a foreman who does not want to be told he is doing it wrong. Not a book of readings. Not a book of figures. A book of *the order of the work*. Every one of you has been keeping the second kind for six weeks and the first kind is the only thing that is worth anything to me and it is worth nothing at all to you, and I have spent a day trying to assemble it out of the order your own pages are in."

"You could have asked at the gate."

"I could have asked at the gate, and if I had, you would have said no, and you would have been right, and I would have gone home with nothing and a bad opinion of you, and I have nine men and I cannot afford either." He put the box down. "So I asked for the reading instead, and I got the reading, and the reading cost you a man an hour he did not have and it cost me a bead I cut five years ago out of a Marrowfield bean for a woman in a market who wanted to know where her seed had been, and neither of us got what we came for, and we are both going to pretend that was an accident."

"What do you want the order of the work for."

"Because in three years I am going to be standing in a yard somewhere in this district with a holding that has a wound in it and a foreman who is frightened, and I am going to have to say *first this, then this, and if it does not hold at that point, stop* — and the whole of that sentence is a thing that has to be true in somebody's book before it is true in anybody's hands. That is all. There is no money in it for you and there is no money in it for me and it is the only thing I have ever wanted out of a season."

Marek looked at him for a moment.

"No," he said. "And I am going to tell you why in a sentence and then I am going to bed, and you are not going to like it, and it is not going to be about you."

"Go on."

"A book of the order of the work is a list of things that have been done," said Marek, "and this holding is a book of things that have been done by sixteen households who can raise four pounds five on a levy, three times, on ground that goes sour, on a node with a leak in it that I cannot mend and a return that is a quarter of what the work needs, in a season in which the charter expires on the twenty-ninth of the month after next. If I write down the order of the work, then the order of the work is a thing you can do. And there are eleven fen holdings in this district that cannot afford a cooperative, and I have been in two of them in the last year, and in both of them the reason nothing was done is not that nobody knew the order of the work. It is that nobody could pay for the compost."

Renner stood in the gateway in the dark and did not say anything at all for long enough that a dog two fields off stopped.

"You have had that ready since about the fourth hour," he said.

"Since the first," said Marek. "I wrote it down on Friday night and I have been waiting all day for you to come and ask for it, and I have got it in a book here, and I have written it against you and I would like you to read it before you go, because you are the only man in this district who will know whether it is right."

He got the book down off the middle of the table where it had been since the twenty-second of a month, in front of anybody who wanted to read it, and put it in Renner's hands, and Renner read the whole of it standing up in the dark, about a hundred and sixty words, and it took him four minutes.

"That is a bad book," said Coll Renner. "That is a bad book and it is the first honest one anybody in this district has ever put in front of me, and I have got a yard full of men who are going to want me to be angry about it and I am not going to be, and I am going to take the book and I am going to take a copy of the copy that goes to Cray, and I am going to be back on this road in a month with a form that nobody in this district has ever seen, and I am not going to tell you what is on it, and I would like it written down that I said that in advance."
