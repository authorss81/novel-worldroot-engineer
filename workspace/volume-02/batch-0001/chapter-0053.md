# Chapter 53

## What The District Owes

It came on the twenty-sixth at about the third hour of the afternoon, by hand, and the hand was a girl of about sixteen in a district coat who would not come past the gate and said four words to Lissa Vale and gave her two packets and a folded sheet and went back down the lane.

The folded sheet was Perrin Osse's. It was one page and it was in her square hand and it said:

*The extract is produced. The extract was not refused on its merits at any time. The ground of the refusal of the seventh day of a month was that the table carries a column stating whether the work is Crown-held, and that producing it is an admission this office does not make to a body holding no charter. On the twenty-first of a month a body in this district held a charter. The ground of the refusal is gone. Nothing has been decided.*

*P. Osse, at the counter, the twenty-sixth, ninth hour.*

*One further sheet is enclosed and is not part of the extract. It is a certified copy of one page of a district register of authorisations, for the item named on it. The register itself is not produced and I am not able to produce it and you are not entitled to it, and the sheet is certified as a true copy of the page and not of the column. The form number is on the sheet. I have put it there because a copy without a form number is a copy of something.*

Marek read it three times and then read it aloud to the room, because that is what the room does with a page.

"Nobody decided anything," he said.

"Nobody decided anything," said Anneke Roos. "That is the sentence. Write it in the book in those words, because in six months there will be a man in a room telling you we were lucky, and lucky is a thing that happens to people. It was not luck. It was a form going out on the twenty-third of a month and being answered by a clerk at a counter because a form is a form, and the ground went out from under the other answer on the twenty-first, and the other answer was the answer to a question about a body that did not exist. I have waited nineteen years for this office to be given a form it had to fill in. I did not expect to be thanked for it. I am not going to be."

The extract was what it had always been. Six columns, ninety rows, and not one head printed over any of them, on thin paper that has been folded and unfolded enough times to have gone soft at the creases. Row forty-one was where it had always been, with four dates and a marked hold printed in the same hand and the same column as a date, and the hold released on a Monday, and the two stamped cut faces under it.

"Put it on the table and nobody touches it," said Dunmar Fitch. "If we put a pencil on it we are describing a document and the description is ours. Read it out and put the reading in our book with the hour, and then it is a thing we did at an hour, and not a thing we think about a document."

He read all ninety rows out loud. It took a quarter of an hour, about a third of them were items in districts the yard had never heard of, and one of them was a school.

"School," said Lissa Vale. "Read that one again."

Row sixty-eight. Item, a school. Order date, the ninth of a month. Execution columns: a mark, a mark and a dash. A hold in the fifth, printed in the same hand and on the same line as the date that would have gone in it, and two hands under the hold.

"That is a school," said Lissa Vale, "and somebody has put a hold on it, and a hold is a thing you put on a job to stop it being done until somebody releases it, and this table is a table of jobs, and there is no column for why."

"There is no column for why anything is in it," said Dunmar Fitch. "Read on."

"Then here is what I want somebody to care about with me," said Marek, and he had the low quarter's entry in the certified copy flat on the table with his finger on it — the nineteenth and the twentieth of a month, four men, a stick and a bucket of lime, at their own hand and at their own cost — and the sheet itself was still nine miles off in a house in the low quarter. "The reason the district would not give us this table in the first place was written in a register at a counter by a clerk who does not have to tell us anything. She said the table carries a column saying whether the work is Crown-held, and producing it is an admission the office does not make to a body holding no charter."

"Yes," said Anneke Roos.

"And there is not one word on this sheet that says *Crown-held*," said Marek. "Six columns and I have been reading them since the third hour. The first is the item, the second is when the work was ordered, two of them are executions, one of them is a hold, and the sixth is the one we have all been calling a third execution since the spring, and I have read the top of that sheet as a form number and a place for a date every time the district has put one in front of us, and if there is anything else on the top of it I have not seen it."

He turned the sheet round on the table so it was the right way up for Dunmar Fitch, and the clerk put his thumb and forefinger on the top right corner and held it at arm's length the way a man does when his eyes are going, and then he brought it in to about six inches and read the line of type across the top of it twice, and put the sheet down.

"The line of type at the top," said Dunmar Fitch. "It is the caption, and a caption on a form this office sends out ninety times a year is a list of what the columns are, in their order, and it is set in the same type as the entries under it, in the same line as the form number and the place for a date, and it has been in front of both of us every time this district has handed this sheet to anybody in this district. A body that has been reading a table for six weeks reads the items and not the top of a sheet, and the top of a sheet is the part that is not a number, and a man with a rule looks at the numbers. The sixth word on that line is *Crown-held.*"

"Then the sixth column is not an execution," said Marek.

"It is not," said Dunmar Fitch, and read the figures off it. "Two letters. Y in forty-one rows, N in thirty-one, and a dash in the other eighteen. And our item is Y, and it has been Y since the first of a month, in the year before he went, and there is no column anywhere on this sheet that says what Y does."

"It was not hidden," said Marek. "It was a letter, in a column of figures, on a table a hundred people have read, and a dash in a column of dates looks exactly like a date that has not happened yet."

---

And the second sheet was different, and it was why she had sent it.

A single page. A printed head across the top with a form number in the corner, and a rule under it, and eleven rows, and eight columns, and a head printed over the first six of them and nothing whatever over the last two — no rule, no word, not a hairline.

This is how the page reads, and the first four of those names are the sheet's own, and there are two more after them that nobody has printed:

*Item.* N-2214/K.

*Order date.* the first of a month, the year before he went in.

*Executed.* 10. 24. 27. —

*Held.* released, date, hour, by and at: *schedule clerk, fen drains.*

*[nothing printed]*

*[nothing printed]*

That is the whole of the page. **The register does not name months.** It carries a day and a month as two numbers under a printed head, and the head has no word on it, and a district form with a head and no word on it is the most ordinary object in the world, and it means that a person in a cold office writes a 1 and a 9 in two boxes and does not think about it and could not tell you in a year what the month was. That is how the district has kept this table for eleven years without a month ever having to be true of anything.

The third, fourth, fifth and sixth columns are three executions and a hold, and the extract carries three executions and a hold, and any clerk in the district could have filled all four of these out of the same table. The seventh column and the eighth column are filled in a hand, and there are no printed lines under them, and no column heads above them, and no instruction anywhere on the sheet saying what a hand in either of them is, and this building has named them both anyway. The seventh is *authorised by*. The eighth is *work description*. Those two names are ours.

And under the seventh column there is no legend. Under the eighth there is no legend either.

Dunmar Fitch read it out, both times, and put it in the cooperative's own book on the seed-house table where anybody may read it, with the hour, and then he stopped, and then he said the thing that is in the entry in the form he actually used, which cost him something.

"Twenty-sixth day, third hour, to the fifth hour. Produced at this table: district works schedule extract, six columns, ninety rows, a caption at the head of the sheet and no column heads upon it, being the district's own certified copy, unrecallable. Produced with it and not asked for: one certified page of a district register of authorisations, item N-2214/K, eight columns, a head printed over the first six and none at all over the seventh or the eighth, and both of those two filled in a hand, and no instruction on the sheet saying what a hand in either of them is. This building has named the seventh *authorised by* and the eighth *work description* and those names are ours and not the district's, and they are entered as ours and not as the district's. This building cannot say whose hand it is. The district has certified the form of the page and not the truth of the column. The register behind the page has not been produced and the applicant is not entitled to it, and the clerk has said so in writing, which is the first time a district officer has said that to this body in writing and I am entering that because it is the part that will matter in a year and not the part that matters today."

He read it back. Marek signed it. Lissa Vale entered the page.

---

They read the page again at the eighth hour with the lamp on, because two of them could not leave it alone.

"Order date," said Marek. "The first of a month, and it is in the year before he went in. Not the year he went. The year before. There is no month on this page to tell me which month it was, and it does not matter, because whatever month it was, it was in a year when he was walking about and could be asked about it."

"Which means the item was ordered before he went in," said Dunmar Fitch. "Which means it is on time. It was ordered and then it was executed, and the executions are the three stamped faces on the wood, and two of them are the district's and the third was cut on this holding with a pair of shears on the twenty-fourth, and the last of the three is the one a licensed reader put her hand on in the mud on the seventeenth, and they are all in the right order, and there is nothing in the dates themselves that is wrong."

"There is something wrong with the dates," said Marek. "It is in a book in my mother's press and I cannot show it to anybody."

He did not go on about it and nobody made him, and the not-going-on was in the room and everybody heard it.

"The seventh column," said Lissa Vale.

"The seventh column," said Marek. "A hand. A hand is not a name. A name is not a man. A man is not a decision. The district has given us three things in one column and certified that the three things are in the same shape as the third thing, and the third thing is the only one that could be a signature, and the only one a register clerk does not have to write."

"Who writes a column of hands," said Ilsa Kern, "if not a man."

"A clerk filling a column out of a table," said Gael Senn, from the doorway, and nobody had heard her come in. "And a clerk writes the same hand forty times in a day, and if the table is a good table the hand is even."

"It is an even hand," said Marek. "Every letter on that page sits at the same angle and it does not hurry anywhere. I have read seed orders for thirty years in a house that has taught me what thirty years of one man's hand looks like on a bad day, and I will tell you what I will not tell you: that is not a man writing a thing he has an opinion about."

"Then it is a copy of a man," said Dunmar Fitch. "Which is what I said in the entry. A register is a transcript, and the thing being transcribed is the thing nobody produces, and the reason this office has never produced it is a column about Crown-held work and the body asking has had a charter for five days and has not yet used it on the right document."

Marek read the page a third time, at about the ninth hour, and the thing he read was the thing nobody else in the room could see.

"There is no column for what the cut was for," he said.

The register has eight columns and this room has the names of six of them off the sheet and gave the other two itself. **There is no column anywhere on that page for the purpose of the work.**

A schedule of damage has a statutory purpose. A hazard measure has a form number and a reason and a place for the person who signed it.

A register of authorisations has an item and a date and a hand and a description of the work. It does not have a column that says what the work was for, and the reason a register has no such column is that a register is not a record of a reason. It is a record of an instruction.

"Then we have a document that says a piece of ground was cut," said Tova Reed, from the door, "and a document that says who authorised it, and nothing on this earth that says why, and the two documents do not sit in the same book."

"They do not sit in the same book," said Marek, "and the first one is nailed to our door and the second one is in a clerk's certified copy, and there is no third one, and I do not think one exists."

---

Eleven rows carry a dash in that sixth column, and none of them is at Lantern Reach, and the eleventh of the eleven is where this yard found out what a hold is.

Row thirty-one. A holding number, an order date in the fourth year, the execution columns carrying a mark and a mark and a dash, a hold in the fifth, and a hand under the hold. A dash in the sixth column. Nothing else. The holding is a colony cut out of a bigger holding eleven years ago and it was on the sheet from the year it was made, and in the fourth year the bigger holding failed and took it down with it, and the district put what was left into the Crown's hand, and the colony has been the Crown's for eight years, and the row has not been touched since.

"It has been dead for eight years and it is on a live table," said Dunmar Fitch.

"It is not dead," said Anneke Roos. "It is *held*. That is a different word and the sheet has it in the fifth column in the same hand and the same line as the dates, which is the thing that has been wrong in this yard's understanding since the sixteenth of a month, and I have been wrong about it and I would like it entered that I was wrong about it. A hold is not a job that has not started. **A hold is a job that somebody has decided not to finish.**"

Lissa Vale read the row out again with her finger above it and not on it.

"Look at the other ten rows that carry a hold and a dash in the sixth," she said. "Nine of them have a date under the hold and one does not, and the one that does not is this one, and there is a word in it and the word is *suspended*, and it is printed, and printed means it is the same in every copy."

"Suspended," said Marek. "For eight years. And nobody has ever written a word under it about why, or about who suspended it, and it has not been released, and it is in a column on a table this office keeps and does not explain."

Nobody wrote the word *concealment* and the reason nobody wrote it is in chapter fifty-six, and the reason nobody asked is that a person has to be accused before a person can be refused.

What Dunmar Fitch did instead was take out a sheet of his own and count, and he did it in front of everybody, which is the only way a count in this yard is worth anything.

"Thirty-one rows carry a hold. Eleven of them are a dash in the sixth column. Nine of the eleven have a date under the hold. Two do not, and both of the two say *suspended* in print and neither has been released. In the other twenty rows with a hold there is a date under the hold and a hand under the date." He put the sheet down. "So there is a form for a hold that has a date under it and a form for a hold that has not, and the one that has not is the one this district uses when the holder is Crown."

"And you have seen that before."

"Four times in nineteen years. I never asked about it and I was never the clerk of a body that could ask."

"Then we cannot tell a Crown suspension from a Crown abandonment," said Anneke Roos, "because the two are printed the same way, and a book that cannot tell those two things apart is not a record of a state, it is a record of a category, and this district has been selling us categories all season and has been right every time. Nothing here is a lie. That is the difficulty. **A district that lies can be caught. A district that prints a category cannot be caught at anything, because the category is true and the category is all you are given.**"

Gael Senn had the certified extract in her hand and had not said anything for eleven minutes, which is long for her, and what she said was about the sheet and not the row.

"It is dated at the bottom," she said. "The extract. Both copies. *Second issue, this season.* And ours is the second issue and it is stamped with a date, and the dash in the sixth column is the same dash in both issues, and the suspension is the same word in both, and there is still no date under it in either. So it is not that somebody has taken a hold off. It is that the thing has been printed as *suspended* twice, in two issues, eight years apart, with a gap, and the gap is where a colony went to the Crown and this district stopped writing about it, and then started again the same way it had before."

She put the sheet down exactly square to the edge of the table, which she does.

"That is not a state," she said. "That is a habit."

Nobody in the room had an answer to that and the room was not trying for one, because the sixth hour had gone and three of the people who had come up from the low quarter had nine miles of bad road in front of them in the dark. So Dunmar Fitch took a clean sheet, wrote one thing, and read it back, and it is the last line in the entry for the twenty-sixth and the first time nineteen years of being a clerk has put a question of his own into a book that goes out of this holding.

*That the district's own schedule, in its second issue of this season, prints in the same words a suspension entered in the fourth year, of which there is no release and no reason, and that this body has no way of asking, and that the asking is not the difficulty. The difficulty is that the words are the same in both issues and nothing in them has changed and nothing in them is required to change.*
