# Chapter 56

## Unmapped Root Line, Maintained In Schedule

Two maps went onto the seed-house table on the twenty-ninth at about the seventh hour in the morning and they were both put down by hand and both were carried to that table by a person who would not let a clerk do it.

The first was the Crown's. Form S/4, nine years old, a seed-route map of the Tarrow fen compiled for the use of seed holders and traders, in five colors that had not been bright in a long time, with a scale in one corner and a legend in a box at the bottom with nine marks in it and a sentence under the box that said the map was compiled for the use of seed holders and traders and was not a survey and did not show every line.

The second was about three feet by two, on a single sheet of lining paper, and it had been made by four men of the low quarter on the nineteenth and twentieth of a month with a stick and a bucket of lime and a knotted cord, at their own hand and at their own cost, and it had been carried nine miles up the fen road by a boy on a slate on the crest night, and it was pinned in a house in the low quarter and it is in a piece of paper in Fennmere, and it is the only survey of that ground anybody has ever had.

Marek asked for it on the twenty-seventh, going out with the Sunday post, and it came up the fen road inside a day and a half, and nobody charged for it, and the man who brought it said the low quarter wanted it noted that they had asked for it and it had been sent.

Anneke Roos said they should not have sent it. Then she read the sentence Tova Reed had written on the Crown map, *no line here*, in a strange hand with a pencil mark beside it, and changed her mind about about a third of what she had been about to say, and said so.

---

The branch is on the low quarter's survey and it is not on the Crown's, and it is not a matter of a line of drawing.

On the low quarter's own sheet the Weir Row branch comes out of the embankment foot in about nine places, exactly as the ground does, and runs north-west to the weir and on out of the sheet. It is in the same weight of chalk as the drain it joins. The men who made it were not surveyors and said so, in the corner, in a hand that is not a clerk's: *by four of us, at our own cost, for our own use, and anybody who wants it to be a survey can go and pay somebody.*

On the Crown's sheet there is nothing at the place where the branch is. There is a printed line in the same weight as the other lines, and it is a boundary, and it is the boundary of a holding, and the holding is Crown-held.

And in the legend, in the box at the bottom, between the mark for *watercourse reported* and the mark for *drain out of order, reported*, there is a ninth mark and a label under it, and the label is six words long.

**unmapped root line, maintained in schedule**

The mark is a small open circle with a bar through it. Marek counted the marks on that sheet nine times on the twenty-ninth and the ninth one is used four times on the sheet and one of the four is the Weir Row branch.

"They have drawn a symbol on it," he said. "They have not left it blank. They have not printed a line they cannot stand behind. They have put a mark in a legend and a legend has a name in it and the name is a category and the category is doing the work."

"A category," said Dunmar Fitch, "is not a concealment."

"It is not," said Marek. "I want to be careful here because I have been in a yard for six weeks where a man with a rule was the only honest man and I am not going to be the man who says Crown in a tone. That sheet is nine years old and it is a map for seed buyers and it draws a mark where there is a line and names the mark. A man who wanted to hide a branch would have left the paper white. Whoever made this did not leave the paper white. They spent a mark and a name and a place in the legend on it, and they spent them on a printed sheet that goes to a hundred and forty seed buyers in a winter, and the reason is in the name of the mark."

"Which is *maintained in schedule*," said Tova Reed.

"Which is maintained in schedule," said Marek. "It is on the schedule. It has three stamped executions on it. It is a live item in a district table with a hold on it that was released on a Monday. It is in your document and in mine and there is a Crown mark on a printed map saying *yes, and here is where it is not, and here is the class of thing it is in.*"

---

Nobody wrote the word into the book and Marek did not say it out loud, and both of those were decisions and one of them was a rule they made on the morning, out loud, at the table, in public, which is how this yard makes rules.

"Concealment is a word about a person," said Dunmar Fitch, with the pen in his hand and not writing. "It says somebody knew and did not tell. I have not got anybody who knew. I have got a form, with a form number, and a legend with a name in it, and a practice, and a practice can be old and can be reasonable and can still be the reason a thing is hard to see. I will write *the practice* and I will write *the mark* and I will write the six words off the legend, and I will not write a word in that book that says anybody lied, because the only person in this building who could be charged with lying about it is a printer in Auberron who was not in this fen and is not going to be asked."

"Entered," said Marek. "And put the sentence in that says we are not writing it, and put my name against it and not his, because I am the one who wanted to write it."

*Twenty-ninth day, seventh hour, to the ninth. Two maps laid over one another at this table and neither altered.*

*One: the district's own seed-route map, form S/4, nine years old, compiled for the use of seed holders and traders, not a survey, carrying four cultivation districts, the recognized holdings, and a legend of nine marks. The Weir Row branch does not appear as a watercourse on it.*

*Two: the low quarter's own survey of the line of the standing iron, made on the nineteenth and twentieth of a month by four men of that quarter, with a stick and lime and a knotted cord, at their own hand and at their own cost, sent up the fen road on request, uncharged.*

*The branch is on the second and not on the first, and the reason is printed on the first. The legend of the first carries, as its ninth mark, six words: unmapped root line, maintained in schedule. That mark is used four times on the sheet. One of the four is the Weir Row branch. The branch is item N-2214/K on the district's own works schedule, with three recorded executions, a hold marked in the same column and the same hand as a date, and the hold released on a Monday by schedule clerk, fen drains.*

*Therefore: a line of root may be worked, maintained, scheduled, ordered, held, and cut three times, and may not be on the public map, because it has no local holder; and a district may print that fact on a map in a legend and sell the map to seed buyers, and both of those are the same practice and the same fact.*

*The words concealment and conspiracy are not entered in this book and were proposed at this table and were refused at this table by D. Fitch and not written by him. M. Vale proposed them and asked that the refusal and the proposal be on the page together, and the clerk has entered it and initialled it and is not a party to either.*

*Entered by M. Vale, engineer of record, against the interest of the application.*

Lissa Vale read it back, and paused at the bottom, and did not read the last line aloud but read it to herself twice before she signed, and the page has her initials on it in a different ink from the body because the body was in a hand and the initialling was hers.

---

Marek went out to the gate afterward and stood there for about ten minutes with the sheet in his hand, and what he was looking at was not the branch.

He has written seven notes under section nine of the charter schedule in one book on the same pages, all of them unsigned by anybody but himself, and five of the seven are about a cut, and every one of the seven closes with the same seven words, and the seven words are *this is worth nothing against a cut.*

A legend on a Crown-printed map, in a box at the bottom, in nine years' type, says six words, and the six words are *unmapped root line, maintained in schedule.*

He said it out loud at the gate to a yard that was not listening, and it is in the book because Ilsa Kern heard it and made him repeat it.

"Say that again," said Ilsa Kern.

"You have said it three times this season and you are not a fool and neither am I, and it is the same sentence," said Marek. "A cut is worth nothing against a note. A note is worth nothing against a cut. And a line with no holder is worth nothing against a schedule, and that is what the legend says, and the legend is printed, and the legend is right, and the legend was printed by somebody who has never heard of section nine of a charter schedule and does not need to have."

"That does not make it fair."

"It does not make it fair. It makes it *precedent*," said Marek. "I have spent a season being the only man in this district writing down things that cost me, and there has been a Crown map in a drawer in a seed house for nine years that says the same thing about nine miles of channel, and neither of us knew the other one existed, and I want that written down too, because in four years somebody is going to find one of them in a bundle of paper and think it is the discovery, and it is not, and I am the engineer of record of a holding whose whole argument is that a book anybody may read is worth something, and my own book is worth nothing against a cut, and I have said so in my own hand seven times, and the district has said it in a legend and got it printed."

---

Tova Reed had been at the table with her hands in her sleeve since about the seventh hour and she had not said a word, and at about the eighth hour she said:

"I have been selling seed to a market for eleven years," she said. "I have taken the March's wet-line reserve four hundred miles north on somebody else's cart and put it into ground that had never grown it, and I have taken four lines of dry-land out of the trust's own book and let a printer set them, and I have done all of that, and I have done it in a market where the price of a line of seed is set by a book, and I have never once in eleven years asked what the book is."

She put her hand on the legend box.

"That is the book," she said. "That is what I have been selling into, and it prices a line of root by whether a Crown map knows about it, and it has been doing that for nine years, and I have stood in front of four hundred people and said the words *long-term preservation of regional varietal knowledge*, and I have never once asked the map whether it knew about the ground I was talking about."

She took the pencil — she asked for it, she did not take it — and wrote under the line she had already written, in her own hand, on the face of a Crown-printed map, in letters about the size of her own.

*Nothing on this sheet about this ground. Nine years of it. And I have sold into it eleven years.*

And under that, which is the seventh line Tova Reed has entered against the trust's own interest since the ninth of a month:

*A map for buyers is not a survey and says so on its own face, and the district has printed the class of line it will not draw rather than leaving the paper white, and I want both of those in a book together, because in nine years one of us will need the second half and nobody will remember the first, and the half that makes us feel better is the half that is more dangerous.*

She read it back before she let Marek sign it, which she does, and then she squared the sheet to the edge of the table, which is a thing Lissa Vale does, and which she noticed, and which neither of them said anything about.

---

It was Ilsa Kern who asked the question that made it a tool instead of a fact, at about the ninth hour, standing at the table with her coat on because she had a cart waiting at the gate and no time for a lamp.

"Then what is the mark *for*," she said. "You have all been treating that thing like a piece of news. News is what it is. What is the mark for. Who is it for. What is a man supposed to do with a mark that says a line is unmapped and maintained in schedule."

Nobody had a good answer for about a minute.

"It is for the district," said Marek. "It is for whoever is responsible for a line with no local holder, which is by definition not us, and the mark is how they say so on a map that goes out for free to anybody who asks for it. It is a way of *giving a line away* on paper."

"Give it away to whom."

"To whoever comes next with a form," said Marek. "That is the answer. The mark is not a warning. It is a handover. It says: there is a line here, it is not ours to keep, it is in the schedule, and here is the class of work you are being handed."

Marek looked at the sheet for a while and then he wrote it down on the face of the map in the space under the legend, because it is the only place a person would ever look, and because he wanted it where the legend was, and because he wanted the legend to have somebody's handwriting near it.

*Unmapped root line, maintained in schedule. A line with no local holder is maintained by the district, on a schedule, and it is drawn differently on a public map so that anybody can see that it is not the holder's. This is not a warning to the holder. It is a handover to whoever is not the holder, and the mark is how a district says out loud on cheap paper that it is not maintaining anything itself.*

*Entered by M. Vale, engineer of record, against the interest of the application. I want this in the margin of a Crown form. If a district is going to print a line, it can print the sentence that goes with it, and a handover that is only visible to a person who already knows what a handover is is a handover and not a fact.*

And then he took the pencil away and squared the sheet to the edge of the table, and read back what Lissa Vale had written, and the two of them were at the same edge of the same table at the same moment from opposite ends of it, and neither of them noticed, and the room was too tired to have noticed anyway.
