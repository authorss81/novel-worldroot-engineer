# Chapter 52

## Sixty Pounds

He came up the lane at about the second hour of the afternoon on the twenty-fifth, on foot, with a satchel and a good coat and no cart, and he stood at the gate long enough to be asked.

Ruben Ashe. Forty-eight. A charter broker, of Fennmere, which meant that he did not hold charters and repair wells and keep a pump running, and did not hold the Assembly's license, and had never once in his life had to go down four feet in the dark, and bought conditional charters off people who had been holding them for a season and sold the conditions on to whoever would pay for the compliance.

He said all of that in the first four minutes, unasked, and he said it in the tone of a man giving a man his own card, and then he asked whether he might sit at the seed-house table, and Lissa Vale said he might not, and Marek said he might, and the vote did not happen because there is no vote for a stranger at a table in this building and the person who keeps the table decides.

He had the charter in a folder. Four clauses and a fifth sheet, and it was not his copy, and he said so before he opened it.

"I am not going to pretend to you that I own a piece of paper with a district seal on it," he said. "What I have is a document that says a body may hold this for the term of a season and may not hold it after the term, and what I have bought thirty-one times in nine years is the term. That is the whole of my trade and I would rather you had it in the first minute than in the fourth quarter of an hour."

"Go on," said Marek.

"Sixty pounds for the charter. Forty pounds a year for the compost, for as long as I hold it, which I would pay quarterly and in advance and without a receipt, because a receipt is a document and I do not want a document. And the schedule of damage." He turned the folder round. "Sixty pounds ten, entered, not discharged, not remitted, and not reduced, and standing in a file that the Directorate has reserved the right to reopen on any new report. I will pay that on your behalf and you will never see it and there is no mechanism by which anybody can serve it on a body that has one. It is the best four pounds in this transaction and I am aware how that sounds."

"Go on," said Marek, and did not move.

"The argument, and you are entitled to it before the number, not after." Ashe put his hands flat on the table, which he had clearly watched somebody else do first. "Your charter expires at the end of the season. Not at the end of next season, not on renewal, at the end of this one, without notice, and I have read the minute and there is no machinery in it for anybody to say no to that. In nine years I have bought thirty-one of these and I have never once had a holder thank me, and I have never once been wrong. A thing that cannot be renewed is not a holding. It is paper with a date on it, and paper with a date on it is worth money for exactly as long as the date."

"And after that?"

"After that somebody with capital holds it or nobody holds it," said Ruben Ashe. "That is the whole of the market and I am not going to dress it up. In nine years of buying expiring charters I have watched eleven holdings go to nobody. I have bought four of them. I did not save them and I will not tell anybody I did. I buy the paper and I hire the crew and I pay the compost, and when the money stops the water stops, and the thing I want you to understand is that the water stopping is not on me. It is on the end of the season, and the end of the season was in the document before you signed it, and the document is nailed to your door with a copper tag under it, so you have had it in front of you since the twenty-first and you have had a week."

He stopped. He was not a bad man and the room could tell.

Lissa Vale spoke first, and she spoke from the end of the table, standing, with her hands not on anything.

"You have made an offer to buy the right to water a seed bed out of a mill floor," she said. "You have not said the word *seed* once and you have not said the word *bed* once and you have said *compost* four times, and a man who is talking about compost is talking about what goes into a wound, and a wound is a thing a body of sixteen households is holding open for a district that has forty-one houses off in its own low quarter."

"I have said compost four times because compost is the line item that kills you," said Ashe. "Mrs Vale, I have read the file. I have read the whole of it, and I have read it because it is a public body and everything about it is a copy somebody can buy. I know the return main is a third of specification. I know your bed is at three and a half degrees and you are buying a fifth of a load. I know the one that kept your field alive is a long line two hundred yards under a windbreak belonging to ninety people with no charter, which means the day a fire takes that windbreak you have no return at all, and I know that because it is in the annex and the annex is in a public ledger in Cray."

He looked at Tova Reed when he said it, and Tova Reed did not move, and after a while she said: "That is the seventh time somebody has read that annex and it is the first time one of them had it in their hand before I did."

---

Tova Reed's refusal was short and it was the second thing in the room that week that nobody could argue with.

"A buyer does not own a graft," she said. "A buyer owns a thing that has been in the ground and cut. I will not sell a charter on the basis that the water under it can be resold, because the water under it goes into a bed that holds seed, and the moment a man owns the water and not the seed, he does not have to ask me what the seed is for."

"And if I said I would buy the seed too?"

"Then I would refuse you twice as fast and for a longer afternoon," said Tova Reed, "and I would put the second refusal in writing, and the second refusal would be the one that matters, because the first one is about a document and the second one is about a thing you have not got."

Gael Senn's refusal was the arithmetic and she gave it to Dunmar Fitch to write down because she wanted it written and not spoken.

"Sixteen households can raise four pounds five in a fortnight, twice, in a season, and the second time was the same as the first time and the first time was two shillings a fortnight better than the fortnight before it. Forty pounds a year is eight times what this holding raises in a good month. In a year I have to be able to say to a woman at a gate how much compost went into that return main and out of which sixteen houses, and I am not going to be able to say it, and there is no wording of the charter that fixes it, because the money does not arrive in the yard. It arrives in a counting house and then it arrives in a cart, and a cart is a fortnight."

Marek's own reason he entered in the cooperative's book in his own hand, in the six lines he uses, and it is not the best of the four and he says so.

"Three of you have refused on things I agree with and one of you has refused on a thing I do not understand properly yet, which is hers and not mine, and I am not going to argue with it. Mine is a number." He turned the page round for Ashe, who had not asked to see it and looked at it. "Sixty pounds is the sum the district entered against this holding on the twenty-eighth for water four wells in Fennmere did not get, and it stands at sixty pounds ten and it is not remitted and not reduced and it cannot be served on anybody, because a debt is enforceable against a body and a body is a thing with a charter, and this body had none for nine hours on the eighteenth of a month."

"Yes," said Ruben Ashe.

"So you are offering me the same money twice for the same reason, and neither of those numbers is worth anything to you, and you have known that, and I have known that, and I have said it out loud in a yard with a clerk in it." Marek put his hand flat on the page. "I would also like to say the other half. I have no money. I have four pounds five and a box that was counted twice this week and is empty, and three acres of my own ground that will not crop until the third season after this one, and a charter that is the only reason there is a seed house. I cannot be bought and I would look ridiculous if I could."

"Everybody can be bought," said Ashe. "I am not going to insult you by pretending otherwise. Some of us are simply bought in a currency you can hold."

He put the folder away and stood, and at the gate, with his hand on the latch, he stopped and asked a question that had nothing to do with money and that Marek has thought about every day since.

"Have you had the schedule extract yet."

"No," said Marek. "It is a courtesy. It comes with the renewal."

"It is not a courtesy," said Ruben Ashe. "Not any more. Not to you."

He said it the way a man says a thing he has decided not to finish, and he went out through the gate and up the lane on foot, and there was a Crown courier on the fen road behind him with a satchel the same shape as his own, going the other way, and neither of them looked at the other.

---

He had not gone up the lane yet. That was the other thing about the twenty-fifth, and it took Lissa Vale until about the eighth hour to say it and she said it to nobody in particular while she was putting a lamp out.

"The man read the books."

"Which books."

"All of them. The standing pressure log, the use log, the return line, the section-nine notes, the entry about the well, and the entry about the man of about seventy, and the three names in a strange hand on a Crown map that had been on the table for two days." She set the lamp down. "He had the folder open on the twenty-fourth's answer to a form I have never seen and he was reading our own book with his thumb on the line he was on, and he was not reading it the way a man reads a thing he has been given. He was reading it the way a man reads a schedule, which is to say he was looking for the column that says what it is worth."

"That is what he is," said Marek. "That is the whole of what he is."

"It is," said Lissa Vale. "And he came into this building and he asked to sit at my table and I said he might not, and Mr Vale said he might, and there was no vote, and I have thought about that for six hours and I have decided that I would have let him sit there anyway and that this is the sort of thing I am not going to think about twice in a season."

She went in. Marek sat on the step with the log on his knees and the fact in his hands, which is this: a broker is the first man outside this holding who has read every book in it and used them correctly, and the reason he is the first is that everything in this holding is now readable by anybody who comes with a folder, and that has been true since the second day of a month and he is simply the first one to have come.

---

Dunmar Fitch wrote the refusal at the seventh hour, and then, on his own initiative and without being asked, he wrote a second line under it, which is in the book and which nobody in that yard had suggested.

*Refused. And the offer was named as made, and was named as made again at the end of the season, which is the expiry of the charter this body holds.*

He read it back before he initialled it, which he does.

---

The other thing about the twenty-fifth is that he asked about the instrument on the middle of the table, and he asked it at about the sixth hour with the folder shut, and he did it the way a man asks for something he has already decided he is not going to get.

"There is a book on the middle of that table that is not in the press," he said. "I am not going to ask to open it. I have been in this district nine years and I know what a use log is and I know what happens to a man who puts his hand on a thing like that without a license in the room, and I have watched two of my own crew lose four years of work over a copy of a form."

"Then why ask."

"Because in nine years nobody in this district has refused me anything in a sentence shorter than that," said Ruben Ashe, "and I wanted to know whether the woman who keeps this table was going to make me say it, and she did, and she has been doing it to me since about the fifth hour, and I have enjoyed it more than I am going to say to her face."

Lissa Vale said nothing to that and Marek said nothing to that, and the broker put his hand flat on the table for a second — a thing he had watched somebody else do first and had now done twice in one afternoon — and then took it away again.

"It is a good table," he said. "That is all I am going to say about it. It is the only table in this district where a man can find out what is actually being done to a node, and I have been buying paper off nine holders in nine years and not one of them would have let me read a page."
