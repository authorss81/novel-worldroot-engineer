# Chapter 514

## Three Wheels, And Nobody Describing Them

The first line of the rota came round at the second hour and was worked, and it was the water: the north end of the ditch, the trough at the head of the four-mile road and the standpipe in the yard. It was the first morning in three weeks the ground had been soft enough to get a barrow up the four-mile road without cutting, and the man of about thirty-one of Silling took the barrow up it loaded and came down with it and did not say anything about that either, because there was nothing to say.

Seven came in at about the ninth hour and came back out of the other book, and the two of them were the same.

**One thousand and twenty-three hundredweight.**

Friday gave five back off Thursday and the fall went onto the falls, and what stands on the launder is the two hundred and seventy-seventh figure of a run in which no number has ever been entered twice. The window this office keeps behind it reads a hundred and twenty-seven mornings one way and a hundred and seventeen the other, and the two of those add to two hundred and forty-four. Four years of setting one end of that window against the number printed over the other and it has never once been done in this building.

This morning's sheet carries the four bodies of households at two hundred and seventy-five days and the clause under it takes the two hundred and seventy-fourth of those mornings out of two hundred and seventy-sixth. One under and one over, and the two figures have never been added to the four launders and a signature on a subscription is not a signature on a charter.

The two men who read the boards read the pair to each other at the seventh hour and neither of them read the other's half, and the whole of it went into the second book at the ninth hour in the ordinary place, with a line under it and nothing at the head of it: **one hundred and nine mornings of two hundred and one**, of a month seven months back.

Six hundred and sixty-one days on the near board, **seven hundred and eight** on the far one, forty-seven between them, and the drawer behind the near board was not opened. The two men who read them have read them every morning for four years and neither of them has ever asked the other what the forty-seven is.

Six went out of the gate at the fourth hour and were inside the machine house by the seventh, and the great door was open again and had been open every hour since it was last seen open, and nobody in either building had said a word about the fact that it was still open.

The three wheels are wheels. Each of the three is about the size of a cart wheel and more than a cart wheel, and they stand in a line along the north side of the floor about three hundred yards apart, with the tanks behind them and the line of gauges on a frame above the tanks. That is what they are, and it is all that anybody in this holding has been able to say about them after standing in front of them for an hour and a quarter.

The man who keeps the gauges walks the line from the second end to the tenth and back with a cloth in one hand and a card in the other, and he does not write on the card. He reads a needle, he looks at the card, he goes on to the next one. On the way back he does the same ten in the same order. Twice an hour, and he had been doing it before any of the four of them were born in this part of the county, and there is no name over the door and no date cut in the concrete, and Marek looked for both of those again on Monday, standing in the road in front of an open door, and neither of them is there.

Marek went through the great door at about the eighth hour and put a foot on the floor of that building and did not stop in it. He went down the rail as far as the second wheel and stood at the third wheel with his hands behind his back for about forty minutes while a change of men came off and went on, and nobody in that building asked him a single question while he was standing there and nobody avoided him either.

The tanks hold water and the water in them is at a level that four of the five gauges agree about and one does not, and the man who keeps the gauges looked at that one for longer than he looked at the others and then went on to the next one and did not write anything down and did not say anything to anybody about it.

**"That one has been a half low since the turn of the month."** He said it to the man of about fifty when the man of about fifty stood next to him, which is the only person he said it to in three hours, and neither of them said anything else about it, and the man of about fifty did not put it in any of the four books.

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden asked the man who keeps the gauges one question at about the tenth hour and she asked him what she wanted it for out loud before she asked it, which is the way people in this county do things and is not a thing anybody taught her.

**"I want a figure in my own hand that I did not get from a sheet in a satchel, and I am going to be arguing with a council about a sowing date and I cannot argue with a council with a thing somebody else wrote."** She said that and then she asked him what the sixth gauge off the end was.

He told her it was a level and not a figure of anything, and told her that a level is a level and she could have one written down and it would still be a level in the morning. Then he told her, without being asked a second time, that if she wanted a figure of what the machine was holding she would have to have it off the wall and not off him, because he read needles and the wall was not his.

**"I have been asked that once in eleven years,"** he said, and went on to the next gauge.

Soren Rill spent the first two hours at the tank end and did not say one word to anybody. He walked the length of the line twice, counted the tanks twice, and counted the gauges twice, and wrote the two numbers in the back of a small book he carries and put the book away. He said at the end of it, to nobody, that he had got two numbers out of that building and that neither of them was anybody's, and that he had carried seed up a road for nine years and never once had a number out of the far end and now had two and no use for either of them.

The schedule on the wall is a schedule. It is the eleventh of the ones that has come up a road and every one of them has been kept. It has three columns at the head of it and a draw figure at the foot of the first one, and a column headed for the things this holding does not know with six rows ruled in it and no unit against any of the six and the seventh row not ruled. The man who reads it off the wall copies it onto a sheet, and the sheet goes out on a road, and the copy is the same as the wall in every particular that anybody has ever compared, and nobody in this holding has ever compared it and got a difference.

The two men on the wheel took it through in fours and stopped and took it through in fours and stopped, and the stopping is not a fault and there is no sound of a fault in it, and it is the third wheel that stands still with the bar laid across it, and the bar has been laid across it every morning this holding has ever heard about and nobody has ever asked why that one is not going.

The clerk of the machine house came off his stool once in three hours to put a sheet on the desk in the smaller door, and the woman at that desk took it and put it in with the others and did not look up at four people standing in a building that has nine hundred yards of rail in it.

There are six people in that building and this page is not going to give any of them a name, and a building with a duty in it does not need one. What it does is hold. The water in the channel down the middle of the floor is warm. The gauges are read twice an hour. The schedule is copied and sent. The wheel goes round in fours and stops. Four hundred thousand people eat out of it in an ordinary winter. All four hundred thousand of them have signed something, and not one of them was asked to.

Iona Vey was in the room from the second hour and she was the only person in that building who was not doing a job while the four of them were in it, and nobody in this holding has established that as a thing she is doing on purpose.

**"You are all looking at the third wheel."** She said it from the second wheel and she said it plainly and without any weight on it. **"It is not broken. It is a wheel that is not wanted on this morning and it will be wanted on another one, and the bar across it is not a fault and it is not a mystery and it is not a thing I have kept back from anybody, and I would sooner tell you that than let four of you spend a morning inventing a reason."** Nobody in that room told her she was wrong and nobody thanked her for telling them.

**"You will want to know why I am in this room."** She did not wait to be asked it, and she did not sound as though she had been waiting to be asked it either. She put her thumb on the frame of the gauge above the second wheel and looked at the needle while she spoke. **"There are six people in here and four of the six of them have been doing this longer than I have, and I am going to be asked today why I stand in this room and I would rather say it than be asked it. I am going to be here as long as there is a window, and I will tell you what a window is when I am asked and not before, and I am not going to trade one for the other."**

Nobody asked her what a window was. The man of about fifty opened his mouth about a quarter of an inch and shut it again and stood there, and the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden did not write it down, and Soren Rill was not in the room and did not hear any of it.

There is a page that could say here why a woman would stand in a room like that on a morning like that, and it is not going to. She is in the room and she is in the room for a reason and the reason has not been given to anybody in this holding and it is not going to be given today because she has not given it and nobody in this building has earned it and nobody has asked her for it.

Two people hold the far end of a thing and this body is not one of them, and there is no instrument anywhere in four counties that says whether it is on or off, and nobody has ever said it is on. Three wheels behind about nine hundred yards of rail are not that thing. Neither is a wheel with a bar laid across it, and nobody in this building is going to put the two in one sentence.

Paid at twenty-five, and it has not moved. The word goes in in front of the figure, where it has gone in for eleven years, and neither the man who keeps the use log nor the clerk has ever been asked to move it behind. The register form is a hundred and seventy-one days old on the middle table and is not filled and is not refused and is not going to be read out a fifth time.

The second of the four lines in the man of about thirty-one of Silling's own book is a hundred and sixty-five days old and has nothing on it, and he brought the barrow down the four-mile road this morning and did not have to cut a rut for it, and he did not tell anybody that either.

Nothing went into this holding's book as a session today and the number of entries in it is unchanged at seven. The four ruled lines under two words in the corner of the one room are still bare and nobody has put a date against them, and the answer is not due. The requests are fifty-three and the section-nine notes are fifty-three, and a morning of six people going nine miles went into neither of the two.

The barrow is at eleven journeys and eleven is a floor, and four miles is not nine miles and the nine hundred yards is not either of them and nobody in this building has ever added the three of them together.

The use log has fifteen lines and got no sixteenth for a morning when six people went nine miles and stood in a building and came home.

The third column of the sheet of terms on the long wall has been ruled and empty since the twenty-first of a month now three months back and it has a heading at the head of it, and the heading is the heading and has not been improved. The five terms were declined as they were offered in a room in another year and nothing has been withdrawn and nothing has been added to them and a sixth is not going to be written this month by anybody.

The two sheets about the two declinings were still nine inches apart where they have been for a month and the shutter being up for six days did not move either of them and did not put them closer together.

The old man with a stick was on the north wall from the third hour to the sixth and the wind was behind him for the first time since the sleet and he worked faster than he had all month, and nobody in this yard said so and nobody asked him about it.

The man of the north row with the cough was out on the low field gate with a bar and the bar came off its pin without any trouble at all for the first time in a month, and he said one word out loud to nobody and it was the word for a thing that has finally gone right, and nobody in this holding asked him which thing.

Nobody touched the arm. The mark under Marek's sleeve is four inches and it branches twice and there is no second mark on it, and the four accounts this holding has of that arm are four and this morning did not make a fifth, and he had his sleeve down the whole of the three hours he spent in that building.

There was no rain and a wind off the fen all morning that took the last of the dripping out of the hedges by the ninth hour. The middle road neither took nor gave, the north end of the ditch is running full off the first line, and the trough at the head of the four-mile road was full by the seventh hour and stayed inside its own lip.

Nobody thanked anybody. The clerk of this holding wrote the seventh in at the ninth hour and read it back once and did not read it back twice, and a man put a foot on the floor of a building nine miles off and stood at a wheel that was not going for forty minutes and a woman told him why she was in the room and did not tell him what a window was, and nobody in this holding has asked her.
