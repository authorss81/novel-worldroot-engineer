# Chapter 679

## The Twenty-Second Of The Eighteenth, And A Man Who Refused His Own Refusal Without Anybody Thanking Him For It

The pair was not read on the Tuesday and the man who came up the lane with a satchel was the one who said so, before anybody had the book out of the rack, and he said it standing in the gateway with the satchel still on.

**"Nobody is opening that book at seven today, and I am the one standing in the gateway saying so, and I am saying it before I have said anything else because I have a thing to say later and I would rather this yard had one thing from me in an order it can hold."**

He was about thirty-four and he had come from the fourth place, and he had been on the road since Sunday night.

**"I have four figures in this satchel that I have not said out loud to one person yet, and I am not going to stand in a yard at seven in the morning and put a fifth thing in front of nine people before I have put the four of them in front of one. A morning has an order in it and mine is not first in that order."**

He put the satchel down on the step of the one room and sat on it, which is not what a man does with a thing he has carried two days.

**"You will get them at ten and I will say them once and I will not say them twice, and then I am going to sit on this step until somebody has asked me a question that is not about the four."**

Somebody else brought it up that road, two men and a cart, and put it down at the wall and went straight back down again without saying one word to anybody, and that is the whole of what a cart from another county is. This yard keeps no ordinal for that and no weight for it and carries no allowance for one. While the cart was being unloaded the board in the one room stood at its thirty-nine blanks, and under those blanks the second rule came back open and empty at the thirty-ninth time, and it opened nothing, and nobody put a name into a blank because a cart at a wall is not a reason.

The man of about thirty-four did not look up when it came, and he said afterwards that he had counted the boards on the cart without meaning to and had got to thirty-nine and had stopped there.

Then the yard filled up, because a Tuesday is the morning the water is wanted and about four people were at the north-side room before the hour was out.

Soren Rill came in from the gate at about the seventh hour with his coat open and stood at the end of the middle table, and he did not sit down, and everybody in that yard understood within about a minute that he had come to unsay something.

**"On Sunday morning I said in this yard that this holding would not be first,"** he said. **"And I gave the reason, and the reason was that being first is the only way of being wrong that cannot be undone afterwards, and that a network which spends itself on its first answer has no second answer left to give anybody."**

He put both hands flat on the wood.

**"I am refusing that refusal. Not because I have got a better reason. Because a reason made out of arithmetic can be taken up and read again when the sheet changes, and that is the whole of what arithmetic is, and I have spent eleven mornings in this yard letting a sentence do a job that a sum is for."**

Somebody at the back said the word pride.

**"No,"** Soren Rill said. **"There is no pride in it anywhere and I have looked. There is a sheet with four headings on it and a man from the fourth place sitting on a step outside that door with four figures in a satchel, and those figures are not on my sheet, and my refusal was made against a sheet with three places on it and not four."**

He took his hands off the wood.

**"So the refusal was a sum and the sum has changed and I am the man who has to live in this yard. Nobody has shamed me into that and nobody is going to be thanked for it, and I would like both of those settled before the hour is out."**

Nobody in that yard thanked anybody. Nobody told Soren Rill he had been brave or generous or clever, and nobody called the man on the step anything at all, and the man on the step did not get up and did not thank him either.

Marek was on the north wall with the tape and the chalk and a length of pipe shoulder, and he had been there since the sixth hour, and he said one thing and then went back to it.

**"The refusal was arithmetic and it was always arithmetic,"** he said, **"and this holding has been saying so for eleven mornings in one form or another while a mason told us what a round figure is worth, which is nothing. I have got nothing to add to that and I have a wall to lay a line on."**

The launder came down at half past eight and the man of about thirty-four read it off the wall because he was standing nearest to it, and he gave it in the wrong order and the clerk corrected him and then let him keep the rest of it.

**"One thousand and two hundred and seventy-seven hundredweight,"** the man from the fourth place said, **"eight on. And the four hundred and forty-second of the run."**

He wiped under the line with the side of his hand before he said the next part, which is not a thing he had ever done before and which he did without being told.

**"A Tuesday is an even morning, so it is the half on the top of the line that takes the point overnight. That one is two hundred and ten. The half underneath has not moved since Friday and is standing at a hundred and ninety-nine."**

He ran his thumb along under the chalk to make sure it had taken.

**"Four hundred and nine of a window between them, and I have said both halves and not the whole, because I have been in this yard ten minutes and a man who has been in a yard ten minutes does not add."**

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden came past him on her fourth trip and stopped.

**"You read that like a man who has been taught,"** she said. **"Nobody has taught you anything. That is what that wall does to a person who stands next to it for a while."**

She set the can down on the boards and went back out for the fifth.

Hesta Lyle turned the long sheet to face herself and did not read it, and she put her hand flat on the head of it.

**"Four hundred and forty at the top,"** she said. **"Four hundred and thirty-ninth a step under it and four hundred and forty-first a step above, in the small hand, and that hand has now made that shape on ten thousand mornings and I have watched every one of them."**

She took her hand off the paper.

**"And a man from the fourth place is sitting on that step with figures nobody in this yard has improved on, and I have said for two days that I would not read them out in front of a man with a case, and there is no case in this yard this morning and so I am going to say them at ten and I am going to hand the reading to him and not take it back afterwards."**

Kellan Rusk came out of the rack with the count not due for two more mornings.

**"The rotation does not come round on a Tuesday and it will not come round tomorrow either, and the figure is where the last one of them left it and nobody is going to be blamed for that. And nothing left this holding today that was due to leave it."**

He put the book back and then took it out again.

**"A hundred and twenty-two sits on that book for the not gone out, twenty-nine have gone at their own hours and a hundred and forty-six are in force, and that is the last of those three numbers I am going to give out loud today."**

He shut the book.

**"After it, the two ages that are ours rather than anybody else's. One of them is on the charter and it stands at two hundred and forty-two days. The other is on the register form at three hundred and thirty-six. And then the one that is nobody's at all: a ruled line in Silling's own book, three hundred and thirty, nothing written against it."**

He shut the book on the third figure and left his hand flat on the cover.

**"That one is in a different order this morning from the order I have given it in for eleven days, and the order is the order a man wants those three in if he is going to lay a pipe, and I have not changed a figure. I have changed where a man looks first."**

Nia Vale did the gate board and then the two figures on the wall, from the gate outward, in that order.

**"Paid at thirty-one, and not one load has ever been let out of that line,"** she said. **"It went up by one a fortnight ago because one more load went in, and it has not shifted since, and I have said that in this yard five mornings running and I would like it on something that will outlast my memory."**

She went up the wall with the cloth.

**"Eight hundred and twenty-six at this end and eight hundred and seventy-three behind the tool house, forty-seven of wall between them. The drawer behind this one is shut and the key is on its nail in the one room doorway and it was on that nail when the yard came up."**

She stopped at the long table and put the cloth down beside the first of the three sheets.

**"The first of these is a hundred and thirty-nine days old and there are about nine inches of clear board at either end of it. The middle one is shut on its fold. The third carries a name, a hand and a seal and there is nothing in it I could call wrong."**

She picked the cloth up again.

**"Not one of the three entered, not one refused, and no column cut under any of them. There are seven sessions in this holding's book and a Tuesday enters none of them, and there are two more books on that rack at fifty-three apiece and neither of them took a satchel off a step."**

At ten o'clock the man of about thirty-four got up off the step and put the satchel on the middle table and opened it, and nobody touched it while he had it open.

**"The fourth place has a head of its own and a figure of its own, and the figure is not a refusal,"** he said. **"The fourth place has never been asked. That is the whole of what the fourth place has to say. Fourteen mornings on a column and nobody came up that lane with a paper and asked us anything, and we have been called the place that sent figures, and we did not send them to be first, we sent them because a woman told us at a gate on Friday that somebody in this holding had already worked out a sum."**

He put four sheets of his own paper on the table and squared them against the edge.

**"I am not asking this holding for anything. I am saying that the fourth place was never on the sheet, and that is not the same thing as being on it and agreeing, and I would like the yard to hear the difference out loud this morning."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the north end at about the eleventh hour and stood at the door of the one room with the hod on his shoulder.

**"It weeps and it holds and the standing pipe behind it is the same pipe,"** he said. **"Nine Tuesdays, and I have said it nine times and I will say it ten times in a fortnight, and I am not going to be the man in this holding who calls a weeping joint a sound one because a satchel came up the lane."**

He set the hod down and looked at the cistern door.

**"Eleven marks wet on those boards and a cistern a little higher than it was at six, and four figures on that table that came two days on a road, and I have built nothing at all this week and I would like both of those written down."**

At dusk the four sheets were squared on the middle table with the fourth place's name at the top of each of them and no signature at the foot of any of them, and a man was still sitting on the step of the one room door, and Soren Rill had gone out to the gate early and had not said anything else about what he had taken back, and nobody in that yard had been thanked for anything.
