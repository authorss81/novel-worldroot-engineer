# Chapter 640

## The Thirteenth Of The Seventeenth, And A Hundred Days Exactly

A hundred days is a figure with nothing on either side of it, and it was on two things in this yard this morning that are not the same thing, and the clerk wrote both of them out in the one line because he wanted the yard to see that a figure can stand on two papers and still be one figure.

He put them side by side on the edge of the long table with a knuckle between them. The letter, a hundred days. The first of the three sheets, a hundred days.

**"They are not added,"** he said. **"They are one number wearing two names, and the second of them is the letter under another description, and a yard that starts adding its own ages together is a yard that has run out of paper before the winter. I have written both because the day they stop agreeing will be a day worth a line, and today they agree."**

The wall came at nine with the frost off the bottom of it this time and the two halves already on the slate.

**One thousand and two hundred and twelve hundredweight.**

**"Five off, and the four hundred and third,"** the clerk said. **"The rising half has not moved since yesterday morning and is a hundred and ninety. The falling half has moved and is a hundred and eighty. That is the second morning this week the same half has moved twice running, and I have told the yard about it once and I will not tell it again."**

Renn Ashby read the pair out of the doorway at the seventh hour, four voices on the last sentence of it, and the heading over the pair is of a month nine months back and did not move.

**"One hundred and seventy-two of three hundred and twenty-seven,"** he said, and then stopped, and started again, and the yard heard him do it.

**"I have said that number the way I say it every odd morning and I have said the one on the wall the way I say that one, and they are two different spellings of two different figures, and I have watched two people in this yard this week reach for one of them when they wanted the other. The pair is one hundred and seventy-two. The half is a hundred and ninety. Neither of them is the other and a man who cannot tell them apart cannot run a wall."**

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden set both cans down on the slab by the tap without spilling a drop out of either, and said what she had to say from up there and not from the table, which is the way she has said everything since anybody in this yard first heard her say it.

**"Right,"** she said. **"And I will say the other half of it. He has just told you the spelling of one figure and the spelling of a different one, and the reason he can do it is that the two of them have never once been the same figure on the same morning. Put them in a list together in your own head and you have a book nobody can check, and a book nobody can check is a book that gets quoted."**

Then the long sheet, which the clerk turned round to the light because the fourth line comes round on Fridays sometimes and he wanted the light on it before the room filled, and the four bodies of households stood at four hundred and one with the four hundredth out of four hundred and second written under the line in the small hand, and the two of them were not on the wall and were not in the pair in the doorway and were never to be added to either.

Sera Quill had the covering letter in both hands and had been in it since before the clerk came out.

**"I have read that paper four times today and I am going to say why, because Harlan said his reason yesterday and mine is not his,"** she said. **"He read it to find the thing wrong with it. I read it because I am a reader and I have spent forty years being paid to know what a hand means before anybody signs, and that page has a hand on it, and the hand is not in a hurry, and the hand is not doing anything to look as though it were not in a hurry."**

**"That is the first useful thing anybody has said about that paper,"** Harlan Vetch said, from the north end.

**"It is the first true thing,"** Sera Quill said, **"and they are not the same, and I am sixty-eight and I have stopped being interested in the difference between useful and true, and you have not."**

**"I have not,"** Harlan Vetch said.

Nobody answered the three sheets, nobody refused them, and nobody moved them. About nine inches of bare board stands either side of each one on that table, and the yard has been reading those inches for four years as a place where a fourth thing would go, and no fourth thing has gone there and the paper that came up the road on Wednesday is at the end of the table with no inches at all marked out for it.

Sennik Vaul read the three ages off the rack at half past ten in the order a person reads them when the person reading is not the person who keeps them, which was the charter first, then the form, then the ruled line. Two hundred and three on the charter. Two hundred and ninety-seven days on the register form behind it. Two hundred and ninety-one on the second ruled line in Silling's own book, and he read that one with a finger laid on the shelf beside it and not on it, and he said afterwards that he had not wanted his hand anywhere near the figure and had wanted the yard to see him not putting it there.

**"You have said that figure out loud in this yard twice in a week,"** the clerk said.

**"And I would say it again tomorrow,"** Sennik Vaul said, **"and the day the three of them stop being a morning's work and start being a habit is the day this holding stops being able to tell anybody what it does not know."**

Harlan Vetch was on the wall at the north end at eleven with a chisel in one hand and a green thread of yarrow still in the second joint of the third course, and he said the thing out loud to the wall rather than to the yard, and then said it louder.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning, and I will tell you why, and it is a narrow reason and it belongs to me. That schedule took a branch off a hedge two miles outside this basin on a day when nobody in this yard was told about it, and I cannot put my hand on a sentence in this yard that says a day is gone for good while I have got a piece of paper in my pocket that says a date did come, and it came, and somebody wrote it down."**

He turned the chisel over once in his hand and did not put it to the stone.

**"One of those two is about a person. The other one is about a day, and a day is the thing that paper is good at."**

Then the boards, which the man of about fifty read at the gate in the order of the near one first and the far one second, and did not add the two and did not look at anybody while he did it.

Seven hundred and eighty-seven at this end. Eight hundred and thirty-four at the other. Forty-seven between them, and the three hundred and seventy on the slate above the clerk's head is a different figure from either of those and has never been added to either of them.

Behind the near board there is a drawer let into the masonry, and on the frame of the one room door there is a nail, and the one key to that drawer has been on that nail since first light and will be on it at dusk, and a man could say the same sentence about that drawer on every morning of this book without once being wrong.

The count in force did not rise on a Friday and stands at a hundred and thirty-nine, twenty-nine taken and a hundred and ten not. Thirty-nine blanks and the second rule open and empty under them, and it opened nothing. Paid is in front of thirty and has been let out in nothing. The barrow stands at eleven journeys and eleven is a floor. Seven sessions in this holding's book and a Friday is not an eighth. Fifty-three in the requests and fifty-three in the section-nine notes, and neither has taken a sheet off that table or a page out of a coat.

Nobody went out to the man of about seventy today and nothing was said to him. Nine hundred yards off there is a door with a number scratched into its seventh column, and this holding did not read that number this morning, and the number has not gone back one notch since anybody last looked at it. No rung of the ladder was climbed, and no foot went onto the ring of bare ground at the top of the low field, which is four years unwalked.

On the low board an offer lies with nothing written on it for a date. Not one of the four ruled lines under the two words carries anything. The third column of the sheet of terms is ruled and empty and the heading over it is one nobody has ever thought to improve. And inside the tool house there is a mark cut into the plaster that measures four inches and forks twice and has not acquired a third fork.

There is a hundred days on the letter and a hundred days on the first of the three sheets, and both of them are the age of a thing that arrived at the ninth hour and has been lying face up ever since, and about nine inches of bare board stands either side of it, and the yard got through the whole of a Friday without once asking anybody what it was worth.
