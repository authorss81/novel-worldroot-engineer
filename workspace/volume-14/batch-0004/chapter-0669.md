# Chapter 669

## The Twelfth Of The Eighteenth, And A Sheet Read Out Loud In A Yard By Two People Who Were Not Going To Pay For It

The pair was not read on the Saturday, and the clerk put it out of his own mouth before anybody had the book out of the rack, which he does now and then.

**"Renn Ashby will open that book at the seventh hour and he will not open it, and he is not the one saying so, I am,"** he said. **"In about ten minutes there is going to be a sheet read out at this table with four headings on it and a figure under each heading, and the reading of it is going to be my job because I keep the copies. That is already one morning's worth of figures in front of nine people, and I have been at this rack every morning for four years and I have never once read two things out of it in the same morning."**

He turned the book round on the rack so the spine faced the wall, which nobody in this yard had seen him do before.

**"One pair a week is what I can stand. One pair, and one paper. If I read that pair at seven and read that sheet at eight then everything said in this yard today will be something I said, and I would rather this holding had at least one morning this week where the numbers came out of somebody else's mouth."**

Then the rotation came round, because Saturday is one of the twelve mornings it comes round on, and it came round in the middle of a yard that was already arguing about a pipe.

**"The book has a hundred and forty-seven on the in-force line this morning,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"Twenty-nine of them have already gone out at whatever hour their own hour is. A hundred and eighteen are still on the books, and that half has been above a hundred all season and will be above a hundred on the morning after next."**

He had the second book open on his forearm and did not put it down when he said the next of it.

**"It has gone up by one and by one. Nine mornings of this kind and never by two. And this holding gave nobody out today, because nobody was down for it, and I will not stand here and point at a man over ground that never had to be given."**

He came round the end of the rack and set the second book on the middle table with his hand flat on it, which put the rotation in the middle of the room where the costing was about to be.

**"There is a woman from the second place who walked nine miles yesterday to say a word, and a first place that has not said a word at all, and now this yard is going to hear what the thing costs. I have never read a costing out loud at this table and I am not doing it because I want to. I am doing it because a sum that only ever gets said by the person who wrote it is a sum nobody has checked."**

Hesta Lyle put her own folded sheet on the table and opened it at the first fold, and it was not the long sheet and it was not one of the three papers on the table.

**"Four headings and one figure under each of them,"** she said, **"and the figure under the first one is a pipe and the labour to lay it, and under the second the same, and under the third the same, and under the fourth the same. That is everything the column's money has in it for this season, and nobody in an office gave me that figure. Two separate men gave it to me and neither of them knew the other had."**

She squared the sheet with the back of her hand.

**"And before anybody in this yard gets pleased with itself, this is not a vote and there is no page here with anybody's name on it. One of the four pays the whole of it and gets the water. The three that are not first spend nothing and have nothing, and after that they will have no argument left to make, because the money is gone and it went into a pipe somebody else is drinking out of."**

Kellan Rusk looked down the sheet and read it out in the only order he reads anything, which is the order it was written in.

**"Four places, one figure each, and the total is one pipe and one laying,"** he said. **"The second place cannot lay a loaded barrow across that ford this month. The third place shut its head sluice at the fifth hour and its water comes round the long way now, and a long way takes its own time whatever the weather does."**

He turned the sheet back the way she had it.

**"I have read that out and I am not accepting it. I want it on the record that I read it and did not accept it, because in about a week one of the four of you is going to tell me that this yard agreed to it, and I did not, and you did not, and I am writing that down now while we are all standing here."**

Marek had his sleeve rolled to the elbow and the chalk still in his pocket and he was the one who had to say what the figure was for, and he waited until the sheet had been read and then he said it.

**"It is for a cistern and a stopcock and four inches of pipe along a skirting,"** he said. **"The room at the north side has a trough at the end of it that is empty by the second hour on three weeks out of four and on the fourth week lately it has been empty by the first. That is the whole of what the pipe is for and I am not going to dress it up into anything about a basin or a district."**

He put his hand flat on the table, and not on the sheet.

**"A man and a day to lay it. That is the labour under the figure and I have never disputed the figure and I am not disputing it now. What I am telling this yard is what it would be spent on, because in about ten days four people are going to ask me what our money went on and I would rather answer that today than in a fortnight."**

Nobody in the yard said anything to that, and the woman from the second place, who had stayed the night at a house down the track and had come back up the lane on foot, put her hand over her own mouth for a moment and then took it away.

**"Then the second place will say the same thing back,"** she said, **"because it has a trough too, and the sum is the same under every one of those four headings, and nobody in this yard needs a fifth figure."**

Sera Quill turned the slate face up at the end of the long table and put her thumb under the bottom line of it, where there were two figures and a third line under the two.

**"Ninety-fifth,"** she said, **"and eighty-second. The two are thirteen apart, and in four years of carrying this slate that gap has not once closed."**

She moved her thumb down onto the third line and kept it there.

**"And the third line under those two says the second one is carried forward, and that nothing printed in this holding ever moves it. I have read that line out loud in this yard five times and it has never once been what anybody argued with, and today I would like it read out loud a sixth, because there is a yard in here at this moment that is about to decide something with a sum on a piece of paper, and this slate is the only object in the room that admits out loud that it is carrying a figure it cannot account for."**

Kellan Rusk took the slate from her without asking and read the third line off it himself, and gave it back, and nobody in that yard said a word about it.

The launder came down the middle road at half past eight, and it was Nia Vale who read it, because she was at the wall with her cloth when it came and the clerk was at the table with his copy sheet.

**"Six on, and one thousand and two hundred and sixty-two hundredweight, and that is the four hundred and thirty-second of it,"** she said. **"I read the unit off the chalk and he copies it off the chalk and neither of us has ever once said so out loud."**

She wiped under the line and left the figures where they were.

**"The half that took the overnight is the upper one at two hundred and five. Saturday is an even morning and the upper one moves on an even morning, and the lower one is standing at a hundred and ninety-four where it stood yesterday. Put the two together and you have a window of three hundred and ninety-nine, and I have only just done that, and I would rather nobody said anything about it."**

Kellan Rusk read the back of the long sheet, which was face down because Hesta Lyle had turned it over and laid her forearm across it and then taken her forearm off it and told him to.

**"Four hundred and thirty at the head and four hundred and twenty-ninth out of four hundred and thirty-first underneath, in the small hand,"** he said. **"One step under and one step over and nine years of it, and I have read it off the back of that sheet this morning because she told me to and because a figure on the front of it can be given to a man in another county and a figure on the back cannot."**

He turned it the right way up again and put his own copy sheet on top of it.

**"Four places on that column and no fifth, and the second place said its word yesterday and the third place shut its sluice at the fifth hour and the first place has not said a word at all in eleven mornings. I have stopped expecting one out of the first place and I go on checking, and those two habits do not contradict each other."**

Nia Vale stopped at the gate post, where the compost line was in its own chalk.

**"That line is paid at thirty-one, and not one load ever let out of it,"** she said. **"It did not fall by one on any morning and it did not drop. It came up by one a fortnight ago because one more load was paid in, and I have said that four mornings running and I am going to keep saying it until it is written down somewhere."**

She came back into the yard and left the cloth over her shoulder.

**"Eight hundred and sixteen at this end of the wall, and eight hundred and sixty-three behind the tool house, and forty-seven of wall between them."**

She came back into the yard and left the cloth over her shoulder.

**"A drawer is let into the stone behind the first of those two numbers. It is shut. The key hangs on a nail inside the doorway of the one room, and it will still be hanging there at the ninth hour tonight. There are three papers on the long table and the first of the three is a hundred and twenty-nine days old, and I did not touch it. I wiped round it."**

She looked down the wall to the drawer and then back at the gate.

**"And I am not saying either of the two sentences this place has on it this morning,"** she said. **"That is not a mystery and it is not modesty. If I stand in this yard and say a permanent thing at ten, then by eleven somebody here will ask me to write it down, because that is what people do with a thing like that. And there is a piece of paper folded in my coat that nobody in this holding has ever seen, and if a sentence I said once went onto a page it would go onto that one, and I am not having it travel with me."**

She looked at the gate while she said the last of it.

**"It goes down that lane at the end of the week in a cart with a tray under it and I am not going to be the woman who put it there."**

Odile Vray came in at the gate just after the middle of the day and asked for the shelf, and Kellan Rusk gave her the register form and the charter and not Silling's book, which is what he does with anybody.

**"Two hundred and thirty-two on the charter,"** he said. **"Three hundred and twenty-six days on the form. And the line in Silling's own book is at three hundred and twenty with nothing written against it. That one is not yours and it is not mine and it is the one thing in this yard I have never been able to hand anybody without saying that sentence first."**

He put the third one back the way he takes it out.

**"And since you are standing there, here is the whole of what that rack holds and none of it is going into a case of yours. Eleven journeys on the barrow and no twelfth, and eleven is a floor. Fifteen lines in the use log with nothing above the fifteenth. Seven sessions in this holding's book, and a Saturday takes none. The board in the one room has thirty-nine blanks cut in it and the rule under them stands open and empty, and it is the thirty-ninth time. Six ruled rows for what we do not know with nothing cut above the sixth. And the other two books are each at fifty-three."**

He drew one more line under the last item and left the pencil on top of it.

**"The last item on that rack is a man of about seventy whose figure is twenty-nine fetchings, and he was not fetched this month and nobody has put a question to him, and I am not going to be the first."**

He shut the rack on the last of it.

**"That is eleven things and not one of them is a figure anybody could enter against a standing, and you asked for the shelf and I have given you the shelf."**

Soren Rill came through the gate with mud to the ankle about the second hour and came the whole length of the yard without sitting down.

**"Nobody in this holding has climbed anything. There is a ladder standing against the tool house wall and the gap between the ground and the bottom rung is the gap it was in the spring. There is a thing on the low board at the end of this yard that has been sitting there so long that nobody under forty could tell you which hand put it there."**

He put his hands back in his coat.

**"And inside the eleven acres there is a ring of bare ground that has taken no boot and no tape and no price today or on any morning of the four years, and I walk the inside edge of those acres for a living and I still do not know what is in the middle of them."**

He stopped in the middle of the yard.

**"And a yard that has just heard its own sum read out at its own table has not accepted anything, and I would like that written down in the same hand as the sum. There is no paper in this holding with our name on it and there is not going to be one, and the four places on that column are still four places and not five."**

Kellan Rusk shut the rack at dusk with the sheet of four headings back inside Hesta Lyle's coat and the slate face down at the end of the long table with two figures and a third line under them, and the count in force stood at a hundred and forty-seven with a hundred and eighteen of them not gone out, and the yard had heard its own arithmetic said out loud twice and had accepted none of it.
