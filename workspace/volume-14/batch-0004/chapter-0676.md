# Chapter 676

## The Nineteenth Of The Eighteenth, And An Offer Of A Room That Was Refused For A Reason About Being Believed

The pair was read on the Saturday at the seventh hour by Renn Ashby, and there were about a dozen people standing round the middle table because a Saturday is the morning the least work gets done in this holding and everybody knows it.

**"One hundred and ninety of three hundred and sixty-three,"** he said. **"Of this month. That is the largest it has been since the trough went in and it is a number and not an event and I have said it the same way four hundred times."**

He got off his heel and put his hand on the back of the chair for a second before he let go of it, which he has not done in this yard before.

Nobody in that yard congratulated anybody on the pair, and nobody read anything into it, and the morning carried on the way Saturdays do.

The cistern in the north-side room had been filled by hand eleven times in five days, and at about the ninth hour Tova Reed put the last tray on the floor, wiped her hands on her skirt, and came out into the middle of the yard and asked to speak.

**"I am not asking anybody anything,"** she said. **"I am putting something on the table so that it has been put on the table and not put on it later by somebody else."**

She stood with her back to the north wall.

**"This room goes without the pipe. I want it said in this yard today, in daylight, out loud, and I want it written on nothing at all. Not on the long sheet, not on a form, not in a request, not in the section nine notes. I have been four days deciding whether to say it and I have decided to say it, and I said it because the second place cannot be reached this month and the third place shut its sluice at the fifth hour and a person is not going to carry eleven cans a day into a full cistern for the rest of the season."**

She wiped her hands on her skirt again although they were dry.

**"The seed dries slower on a floor than on a shelf and I have proved that over five days, and a room with no water in it in the middle of that week is a room I have to move every tray out of and put back when the water comes, and I know that because I have done it in a bad year."**

Somebody in the yard said something about it being a hard thing to offer and she did not let it stand.

**"It is not a hard thing. It is a room with a trough in it and a cistern I did not ask for. I would like the offer taken and I would like it taken quickly and I would like nobody to thank me."**

Marek came in from the middle road about the tenth hour with the tape on his belt and had been gone long enough for nobody to interrupt him, and he stood at the cistern door and did not go in.

**"No,"** he said.

He said it before anybody had argued for it and he said it flatly and he did not soften it afterwards and he gave the reason in the same breath, which is how he gives a reason.

**"This holding is the only place on that column that anybody can check. Two boards on one wall with a number at each end and a drawer shut behind the first one and a key on a nail, and a window on it with a figure that moves every morning and two halves that move against each other, and a count that comes round every fourth morning and is written down. That is the whole of what this holding is for to anybody up that road."**

He put his hand on the door frame and not on the cistern.

**"A place that is left has to be a place nobody can see the inside of. That is not a kindness and I am not doing it as one and I am not going to be talked into it by anybody in this yard including the woman who made the offer. If the room at the north side goes without water then this holding stops being checkable in the only way it has ever been checkable, and the four places that are still in the column will be holding a claim that nothing on this earth can look at."**

He looked at the cistern and then at the yard.

**"And I am not going to tell you who should have it instead. That is not mine to do and anybody who tells you otherwise is using this yard to move a figure from one page to another."**

He came off the door frame and stepped back into the yard.

**"The offer was refused. It is not going to be softened tomorrow and it is not going to be turned into a later. Somebody else in this yard is going to feel bad about it by the middle of the week and they can feel bad, and feeling bad does not put the pipe back on this sheet."**

Somebody at the gate said, quietly and to nobody, that Marek had not had to say no that fast, and Soren Rill turned round from the low board and said one sentence into the yard without raising his voice.

**"The man who said no gave the reason inside ten seconds and the reason was the reason and none of us has a better one. Nobody in this yard is going to be called anything for it and I would like that settled before the hour is out."**

Nobody in that yard was called anything, and nobody put a hand on anybody, and nobody apologised to her, and the morning did not go anywhere else for the rest of it.

The launder came down the middle road at half past eight and Marek read it from the tap house door, because he was the one standing nearest the channel and because nobody else in that yard had asked him to.

**"One thousand and two hundred and sixty-six hundredweight,"** he said, **"and it is the four hundred and thirty-ninth of the run. Five off, because a Saturday is an odd morning and on an odd morning in this holding it is the lower half of that window that goes down first."**

He did not wipe the line and he did not put his thumb under it.

**"The lower half is a hundred and ninety-eight. The upper half stood where it stood yesterday at two hundred and eight, and the two of them make a window of four hundred and six."**

He went back inside, and the yard said nothing about it, and no figure of it was written on anything anywhere that morning, which is the first time that has happened in this holding in four years.

Nia Vale came through with her cloth and did not say anything about the cistern and did the gate board instead.

**"Paid at thirty-one,"** she said. **"Nothing has ever been let out of that line and it came up by one a fortnight ago because one more load went in, and it has not moved since."**

She wiped round the figures and not across them.

**"Eight hundred and twenty-three is cut into the stone at this end of that wall and eight hundred and seventy is cut into it at the other end, and there is forty-seven of wall between them."**

She wiped round the near one.

**"The drawer behind it is shut, and the key is hanging on its nail inside the doorway of that room, and it will be hanging there at the ninth hour tonight."**

She wrung the cloth out on the step.

**"Three sheets, and I have counted them again this week because a man stood where I was standing. The oldest is a hundred and thirty-six days and there are about nine inches of bare board at either end of it. The second is folded shut. The third has a name and a seal on it."**

She stopped with the cloth in her hand.

**"Not one entered, not one refused, no column cut under one, and I have heard four refusals in this yard in four years and have never once been asked to write one of them down, and I am not going to start because this morning's was said out loud at a cistern door."**

The clerk had the copy sheet out at the middle table and had been writing the second place's word on it since Friday and did not look up while the offer was made.

**"The second place's word is on my sheet and on my sheet only,"** he said. **"It goes up to the office on the ordinary day with the rest of it and it arrives as a fact about the second place. The office's own day is the twenty-ninth of this month and it has not shifted one hour since anybody put it there, and a refused offer in a yard in this county does not move a day that four places have already written round."**

He turned the sheet over and put his own hand flat on the blank back of it, and said the next of it to the back of the sheet rather than to the yard.

**"The top of that long sheet is four hundred and thirty-seven. A step under it is four hundred and thirty-sixth and a step above it is four hundred and thirty-eighth, in the same small hand, and I read it off the back this morning so that a figure another county can be handed stays on the front where it started."**

He turned the long sheet over and read the two figures again off the back of it.

**"Two hundred and thirty-nine days on the charter and three hundred and thirty-three on the form and three hundred and twenty-seven on the line in Silling's own book with nothing written against it. I have said that last part out loud five mornings running at the same volume every time and it does not get quieter or louder."**

He put the sheet down the way he puts it down, square to the edge.

**"And I am not saying either of the two sentences this place has on it this morning,"** he said. **"The reason is that I have this holding's words in a case and I am going to carry them to an office on Wednesday, and a man carrying a yard's words to a room where they will be read over should not also stand in that yard and say the last sentence anybody is waiting for."**

He turned his own copy sheet over and put his hand flat on the blank back of it.

**"That is the whole of it and I have said it before I get asked."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the north end about the eleventh hour and put the hod outside the one room door and stood looking at the cistern for a while.

**"Eleven marks wet on those boards,"** he said, **"and a cistern a little higher than it was, and a woman just offered to give the whole thing up and a man just said no inside ten seconds, and neither of those two people is a fool and I have worked with both of them."**

He put his thumb on the brick and took it off again.

**"The joint weeps and holds and the standing pipe behind it is the same pipe. And a cistern that is full by hand is a cistern somebody is standing next to all day, and that is a worse way to hold water than a pipe and I am not going to pretend to anybody in this yard that it is a better one."**

He went to the tap house and came back.

**"Nine Tuesdays. I am not chalking it and I am not the man who says it has settled."**

Soren Rill came the inside edge of the eleven acres at dusk without going near anything in the middle of them, and stopped at the low board, and did not touch it.

**"Nobody has gone up that ladder and not one figure in this holding is set against either of the two people it belongs to,"** he said. **"What is on that low board has no date written on it and no hand near it, and I have asked nobody in four years what it means and I am not going to start on a Saturday."**

He looked across at the cistern door and then back at the low board.

**"That bare ring inside those acres has taken no foot today, no tape and no price. The other two books in that yard are each at fifty-three and they have never once been the same list."**

He put his hand flat on the gate post.

**"The barrow in there is marked at eleven journeys and nobody has ever put a twelfth beside them. The use log is fifteen lines deep with a clean space above the fifteenth. There are seven sessions in that holding's book and a Saturday enters none, and a shelf in that room carries six ruled rows for what nobody there can say with nothing cut above the sixth. And the board in the one room has thirty-nine blanks cut in it, with the rule underneath standing open and nothing behind it, and the standing on that rule is the thirty-ninth time."**

He looked over at the cistern door.

**"Before the month is out somebody is going to fetch the man at the end of the north row and put a figure in front of him. That figure is twenty-nine fetchings. He is not fetched this month, nobody has asked him anything about it, and I would like that in this holding's own hand before it is in somebody else's."**

Whatever else that Saturday settled, it settled one thing: a woman offered a room and a man refused her inside ten seconds with the reason attached, and the cistern in the north-side room stood full at dusk with eleven marks wet on the boards beside it and the long sheet lying flat on the middle table with nobody's hand on it.
