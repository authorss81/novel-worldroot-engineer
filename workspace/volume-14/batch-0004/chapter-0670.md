# Chapter 670

## The Thirteenth Of The Eighteenth, And Four Hundred With Nothing Round About It

The pair was read on the Sunday at the seventh hour and a return came up the fen road at the same hour, which nobody in the yard had any way of arranging.

**"One hundred and eighty-seven of three hundred and fifty-seven,"** Renn Ashby said, with the book propped open on the middle table and his thumb where it is. **"Of this month, and I am not standing here to argue with anybody about whether I have said it right."**

Then the cart came off the fen road and turned in at the gate, and the yard went to it, and the book stayed open on the table with a thumb in it for about a minute and a half.

There is no ordinal for the return and no weight and no allowance, because none of those things exist for it. What exists for it is a board in the one room with thirty-nine blanks cut in it, and a rule underneath those blanks, and the way the second of those two rules comes back.

**"Thirty-nine,"** the man of about fifty said, from the doorway, counting them without touching the board. **"Same as Friday. And the rule under them is open and it is empty and that is the thirty-ninth time it has come back that way."**

He put his hand on the frame of the door and not on the board.

**"I have watched that rule for six years and it has never once opened anything. I am not going to stand here and say what it is waiting for, because a man who says that in a yard on the morning a return comes up the road has said it about the return and not about the rule."**

Then the launder, at half past eight. The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden had the trough side that morning and read it from there with the water still coming down, and the clerk wrote it out on his copy sheet at the wall and said it after her.

**"Five off, one thousand and two hundred and fifty-seven hundredweight, and the four hundred and thirty-third of it,"** she said. **"I read it off the trough rim because I was standing at the trough."**

**"That is what she said,"** the clerk said, and wrote it down, and then read the halves himself in the order he reads everything.

**"The half that fell in the night is the lower one at a hundred and ninety-five, because a Sunday is an odd morning and on an odd morning in this holding it is the lower half that goes first. The upper half stood where it stood yesterday at two hundred and five."**

He wrote the two figures side by side under the line in the ordinary way and then stopped with the chalk still against the wall.

**"And that is four hundred,"** he said.

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden came round the front of the trough and looked at the wall and did not touch it.

**"Two hundred and five and a hundred and ninety-five,"** she said. **"That is four hundred. I have been carrying water past this gate for four years and I have never once in any of those four years added those two figures together on that wall, and I have just done it twice this morning, and it came out four hundred both times."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the north end with the hod still on his shoulder and read the wall from where he was standing, from about eleven feet off, the way he reads everything.

**"Four hundred,"** he said. **"So it is a round number."**

Nobody said anything to that either, and he went on with the hod on his shoulder.

**"Four hundred is a round number and it means nothing at all,"** he said. **"I have built four walls in my life that came to a round number of feet and not one of them stood up because of it. This one has been coming up the same channel since the trough was put in and it will come up the same channel next Sunday whether it is four hundred or four hundred and one."**

Kellan Rusk came out of the rack and did not say anything about the round figure either, and he said afterwards that he had thought about saying something about it and had decided not to.

**"Three hundred and ninety-nine on Friday and four hundred this morning,"** he said, **"and one up, and that is a Sunday and the same as every other Sunday, and there is nothing in this holding that keeps a mark of a round number. This yard does not keep marks of anything."**

The clerk put his hand flat on the wall under the two halves and left it there.

**"I have said the sum out once and the two of you have said it out twice and my friend the mason has told us what a round number is worth, which is nothing, and he is right, and I am not going to argue with a mason about a wall in his own trade."**

He wiped the wall under the line with the side of his hand.

**"What I will say is this. That window has been on that wall since before I came and it is on the wall because somebody put it there and nobody has ever taken it off. It has not been four hundred before and it will not be four hundred again for a long while, and neither of those two things happened to anybody in this yard, and I have written nothing against it and I am not going to."**

Sera Quill had the slate in her hand and did not put it down all morning, and she used it once, about the fourth hour, when the yard had gone quiet.

**"Three hundred and twenty-seven days on the form,"** she said. **"Two hundred and thirty-three on the charter. And the third of those ages, three hundred and twenty-one, belongs to a ruled line in Silling's own book and there is nothing written on it."**

She turned the slate round so the yard could see the back of it as well, and on the back of it, in her own hand and smaller, there were two more figures.

**"Four hundred and thirty-one at the head of a long sheet I have never seen the front of, and four hundred and thirtieth out of four hundred and thirty-second under it,"** she said. **"I copied those two off the back of it on an ordinary morning when Hesta Lyle was reading it out in this yard and did not notice, and I have never had a use for them until this morning."**

She turned it back round.

**"The slate has a round nothing on it too. It has been blank since it came out of the printer with the maker's name on the back and I have had it in this yard for four years and I have never once had a reason to put a figure on it, and I am not going to start because a sum came out even on a wall."**

Marek was not in the yard for most of the morning. He had gone down the middle road at seven with the tape on his belt to look at a place where the channel has been wanting a stone since before he came, and he came back at about eleven and put the tape on the hook and did not say anything about where he had been.

**"The two figures on that wall are the two figures,"** he said. **"They are not the whole and they are not the sum. There are two of them because the thing comes in from two directions and one of the two has been bigger than the other every morning since the trough went in, and on the mornings the falling one moves it takes a point off the rising one and not out of it."**

He wiped his hands down his trousers.

**"A round number is a number a man can remember. That is the entire use of it and I have never known any other use and I am not going to invent one for it in a yard on a Sunday."**

The man of the north row with the cough had got up off his bench at the seventh hour for his paper and had gone back and had heard the whole of the four hundred from where he was sitting.

**"I want to say the thing I have not said for a while and I would rather somebody else said it,"** he said. **"This yard has two sentences of its own and I am leaving both of them where they are this morning."**

He put his hands on his knees.

**"The reason is that cart. A return came up that road at the seventh hour and it will go back down it on Wednesday, and if a permanent sentence is said in this yard before Wednesday then the two of them will go down the same road in the same cart and be opened in the same room, and there is nobody in the room they will be opened in who will be able to say which of the two was which."**

He looked at the middle of the yard and not at anybody in it.

**"A return is not a small thing and a man who says a hard sentence on the day one arrives is telling me he thinks they travel together. They do not travel together. I have had four years to be sure of that and I have never needed to say so out loud before."**

Nia Vale did the gate board after the return had gone up to the one room and the yard had gone back to what it was doing.

**"Paid at thirty-one and nothing let out of it,"** she said, wiping round the line and not across it. **"It came up a week ago because one more load went in. It has not moved since and it will not move tomorrow."**

She stood at the gate with the cloth and looked up and down the fen road.

**"And the door nine hundred yards off went past at the tenth hour with the cart and it is on nobody's paper and it is not going on anybody's, and I did not offer it to the man who came up the road with the return and he did not ask me for it, because a figure a man drives past is not a figure the man has read."**

She turned from the gate and started along the wall.

**"This end of that wall says eight hundred and seventeen and the end of it past the tool house says eight hundred and sixty-four, with forty-seven of wall between the two ends and nothing else between them worth the name."**

She put the cloth on the trough step.

**"A drawer is let into the stone behind the first of those two figures. It is shut, and its key is on a nail inside the doorway of the one room, and it was on that nail when the yard came up at six and it will be on it at dusk."**

She folded the cloth over her shoulder.

**"And the first of the three papers on that long table is a hundred and thirty days old today with about nine inches of bare board at each end of it. Thirty is a round number too and nobody in this holding has ever said a word about the bare board at either end of it."**

Harlan Vetch came back down the yard about the second hour and stood at the one room door with the hod on his shoulder and listened to the joint at the back of the tap house without going to it.

**"It weeps and it holds and the standing pipe behind it is the same pipe,"** he said. **"That is five Tuesdays now and I have said it five times and I am not going to be the man who says it has settled."**

He looked along the chalk line on the floor of the north-side room as he went past and did not step over it.

**"Four inches off the floor is right and I would have chalked it the same. A pipe is my work and a leak is my work and a mason who starts mixing the two of them together ends up doing neither of them properly, and that is the whole of what I have to say about the north end today."**

The man of the north row with the cough got up off his bench at about the fifth hour and came and stood at the one room door where the board was, and read it the way he reads everything, which is from the bottom up.

**"Thirty-nine blanks,"** he said, **"and the rule underneath them open and empty at the thirty-ninth time, and a cart came up that road at seven this morning and put no fortieth one there. Four feet four inches above the floor and forking twice, and four accounts of it that reconcile none of one another, and that mark of mine is not a debt and I have never once said it was."**

He put his finger on the space above the thirty-ninth blank and left it there.

**"Six ruled rows on the shelf against what we do not know, with nothing cut above the sixth and no seventh cut today for a cistern or a pipe or a man with a case. Fifteen lines in the use log with nothing above the fifteenth. Eleven journeys on that barrow with no twelfth, and eleven is a floor."**

He took his finger off the board.

**"Seven sessions in this holding's book, and a Sunday takes none of them. The other two books are each at fifty-three and neither of them took the cart that came up the road this morning. And there is a man of about seventy in this yard, and the figure against him is twenty-nine fetchings. He was not fetched today and nobody has put a question to him about it, and this place has never once gone over to ask him which number it is."**

He went back to the north row and sat down.

Soren Rill came in at dusk from the far end of the eleven acres and came the inside edge back without going near anything in the middle of them.

**"The ladder has gone up no rung. The offer on the low board is undated and unpicked and not taken back. And the ring of bare ground inside those acres has taken no foot today and no tape and no price."**

He stood at the gate.

**"And a sum came out even on a wall this morning and a mason told us what that is worth, which is nothing, and I am going to carry that sentence into the middle of next week and use it there, because by Wednesday somebody on that column is going to want a round figure out of somebody and I would like the yard to already have the answer ready."**

Sunday went by in that yard with a return on the board and a rule under it standing open at the thirty-ninth time and a window that had come to a round number for no reason that anybody there could put a hand on, and nobody in it was asked anything.
