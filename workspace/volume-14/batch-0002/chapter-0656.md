# Chapter 656

## The Twenty-Ninth Of The Seventeenth, And A Man Who Was Very Good At His Work On The Sunday

Renn Ashby read the pair at the seventh hour on the Sunday and the heading over it is of a month nine months back and had not moved.

**"One hundred and eighty of three hundred and forty-three,"** he said, **"and I am back after Saturday, and I read the number, and there is nothing to report about the day before it because nothing in this yard happened that anybody has told me about."**

The yard laughed at that and the clerk, who was the only person in it who knew there was something to tell, did not laugh, and did not look at anybody in particular, and got on with the wall.

Marek did the whole of the Sunday in the yard and he was very good at it.

He read the three ages off the rack before anybody else was up and left the book on its nail. He found the weep at the tap joint at about the seventh hour and put a saucer under it and did not pack it and wrote nothing about it in any book. He went down to the ford with Soren Rill at the ninth hour to look at the road after six days of rain and came back with a fact about the road and no opinion about anything else, and he gave the fact to Hesta Lyle and not to the yard.

He asked the man of about fifty about the six weeks and did not advise him about the six weeks and did not offer to write anything down for him.

At about the tenth hour he stood at the middle table with the slate and said the launder, and it was not his figure and he had been saying it for four years.

**One thousand and two hundred and thirty-six hundredweight.**

**"One thousand and two hundred and thirty-six hundredweight, five off, and the four hundred and nineteenth of it,"** he said.

Then the window, in the order of the wall.

**"The falling half is a hundred and eighty-eight and it went up in the night. The rising half is a hundred and ninety-eight and it has stood where it stood on Saturday. Together, three hundred and eighty-six."**

He put the slate on the ledge.

**"I have said more figures out loud this week than in any week I have stood in this yard, and I would like it understood that saying a figure a great deal is not the same thing as having something to say."**

Nia Vale was at the seed house end of the yard all morning with her back to the long table, and she gave the three ages to anybody who asked for them and she did not write on anything at all.

Nobody in that yard said a word to Marek about Saturday. Nobody asked him how he slept and nobody told him he looked well and nobody put a hand on him, and nobody thanked him and nobody said they were sorry.

That was not kindness. It was that a yard of nine people had worked out inside about four hours on Saturday evening that there was nothing they could do, and had the good sense not to do it, and that is a different thing from kindness and probably harder.

He was not different in front of anybody. He was slower to begin a thing by about a second and a half and he missed one step on the ladder at the north end at about the eleventh hour which he did not miss on Saturday and which he noticed himself doing and went back and did again.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning,"** Kellan Rusk said, in the one room door at about the seventh hour, **"and the reason is that I read three ages out of a book yesterday with my hand flat on the shelf to steady me, and I told that room I was going to stop needing the hand."**

He was holding the form in his left hand and the charter in his right and neither hand was on the shelf.

**"So if I put those two sentences on top of the three ages this morning then the three ages stop being three figures and turn into an argument, and the ages have been a book of plain arithmetic in this holding for four years and they have never once been about anything. A man who has decided to stop leaning on a shelf does not get to change what is on the shelf."**

He read them out again without the hand, for nobody.

The line in Silling's own book is at three hundred and seven and has nothing on it. The charter is at two hundred and nineteen. The form is at three hundred and thirteen days, and that is a different figure from the one the sheet on the table has been lying there for, and the two of them are never to be run together.

**"That is three mornings running that a line with nothing on it has been given out loud in this yard,"** he said, **"and every one of us did it differently, and the line has still got nothing on it, and I would like that written down because somebody in a hurry is going to decide at some point that three people saying an empty figure is a start."**

Hesta Lyle had the sheet with the ruled line down the middle of it in front of her and did not show it to anybody, because a Sunday is not the morning for it and she had said on Friday that she would say it out loud and she had said it out loud.

The long sheet was read out from the gate end by Harlan Vetch on his way in, standing up, the way a man reads a thing he has no part in.

**"Four hundred and seventeen, and the clause underneath is the four hundred and sixteenth out of four hundred and eighteenth,"** he said. **"Four bodies of households. Four lines. Two signatures wanted on each."**

He went past it.

**"No signature on any of the four and no column cut anywhere in this holding for what a signature agrees to, and I have been building a wall four feet from that table for four years and I have never once been asked to read the thing on it."**

Marek gave the two boards in the order a man reads them when he has just come in from the far side of the yard, which is far first. Eight hundred and fifty at the far board. Eight hundred and three at the near one, forty-seven of wall between the two, and a drawer behind the near one that is shut, with its key hanging on a nail in the door frame of the one room and no other key anywhere in this holding.

Marek did the standing round as well, because nobody else was going to and because it is the sort of thing a man does with his hands when his hands are the only part of him that can be useful that day. He put his thumb in the compost line at the gate and found it paid at thirty and empty. He counted the barrow's eleven journeys and left the twelfth unmarked, because eleven is a floor rather than a figure. He counted fifteen lines in the use log behind the glass and did not go on to a sixteenth. Seven sessions were entered in this holding's book and Sunday took none of them, because Sunday never does. The requests were at fifty-three and the section nine notes were at fifty-three, and between them they had taken nothing off the long table this morning and nothing out of any coat in this building.

**"None of it moved and none of it is anybody's fault,"** he said, to the yard rather than to a person, **"and I have been in this yard four years and this is the first Sunday on which I have wanted it to be somebody's fault. That is the thing I would like somebody to check."**

Sunday does not bring the rotation round either, so the number in force had not moved since Friday: a hundred and forty-one of it, twenty-nine taken, a hundred and twelve not. No field went out, and nobody was blamed for one that stayed where it was put. In the one room the board carries its thirty-nine blanks and nobody put a fortieth name into any of them this morning, and the second rule under those blanks is open and empty and opens nothing, as it has every morning of the four years anybody here can remember.

Tova Reed counted the six rows on the shelf with the back of her hand on her way to the bench and did not find a seventh.

On his way round he stopped under the four inches and the two forks cut into the tool house wall and did not put a hand on it, because a mark a man did not cut is not his to answer for. The ladder went up no rung. The offer on the low board was where it had been put, undated, untaken, not taken back, and the ring of bare ground had not had a foot on it. Nothing was fetched from the man of about seventy that Sunday and nothing was asked of him anywhere he was seen. And the door nine hundred yards off went past at about the eleventh hour and was not read.

**"A hundred and sixteen days,"** the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden said, from the tap, without coming over. **"That is how long the first of them has been on that board, and about nine inches of bare board either side of it, and the second is folded back on a crease and shut, and the third is the one with the seal on it."**

She filled a can and set it down.

**"Nobody here has entered one, refused one, or ruled under one, and there is no column in this holding for any of the three. I have looked at the third of them more times than I have looked at my own book and there is nothing wrong with it, and that is the whole difficulty and it does not go away by being looked at."**

That Sunday went by in this yard with a man who had given something away on Saturday and nine people who did not ask him what it was, and the yard got through it on those terms, and it will get through the next one on the same.
