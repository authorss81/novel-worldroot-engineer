# Chapter 653

## The Twenty-Sixth Of The Seventeenth, And Eight Hundred On The Board He Reads From Where He Stands

The pair was not read, and Sennik Vaul said so at the middle table at the seventh hour before anybody had the book out of the rack.

**"Because there is a round figure on the near board this morning and this yard has spent four days learning that a round number is not an event,"** he said. **"If the pair is read out loud at seven then the first thing nine people do afterwards is hold the two figures up against those boards, and the boards are not what anybody is reading. I have watched this yard do that to a figure on a Monday and it took the rest of the day off the figure."**

Harlan Vetch came away from the north end about the eighth hour and walked down the length of the yard to the near board and stood in front of it and did not touch it.

It said eight hundred. Nothing after it, nothing under it, no tail and no *and*, and the far board at the other end of the same wall said eight hundred and forty-seven, and the two of them had been forty-seven apart for four years and were forty-seven apart this morning with nothing whatsoever done about it.

**"I have refused a figure on that wall more than once and every time I have been asked for the reason,"** Harlan Vetch said, **"and the reason has always been the same. A number somebody else has worked out goes on a wall, and then the wall belongs to the number, and then the men who built the wall are working for the number. That is the reason and it will be the reason the next time."**

He put his thumb on the edge of the board where the chalk had been rubbed off by somebody's coat over four years.

**"And here is the thing I have not got a reason for. This one is not somebody else's arithmetic. This one is my own arithmetic and yours and the launder's, counted by hand by a man with a slate since before the wall was finished, and it has arrived at a round figure by doing nothing but being true every morning, and nobody in this holding can be shown to have put it there on purpose."**

He took his thumb off the board.

**"So I have refused a figure on a wall for years and this morning there is one that is not a lie, and it is the only one that has ever been here, and it is on the board I read from where I stand. I would like somebody to write that down."**

He turned his head slightly without taking his eyes off the board.

**"I am not saying it is right. I am saying it is the first one of these that has come out of the yard instead of out of an office, and I do not know what to do with that."**

Then he went back to the north end and refused a figure on the wall again, out of his own hand, in front of nine people, on the same ground as before, and nobody in that yard turned the thing into a number and nobody said it out loud as a number, and one man opened his mouth to and was stopped by a look from Sera Quill, and after that nobody tried.

The clerk had the slate against the wall at half past eight and said the launder before he said anything else, because a Thursday is an even morning and the falling half had stood still overnight.

**One thousand and two hundred and thirty-eight hundredweight.**

The clerk put the chalk against the wall and left it there and spoke to the two halves in the order they were written.

**"The rising half is a hundred and ninety-seven and it is the one that moved overnight, because a Thursday takes it. The falling half is a hundred and eighty-six and has stood still since Wednesday. Three hundred and eighty-three of a window."**

He picked the chalk up again.

**"And eight on the launder this morning, which is the four hundred and sixteenth of it."**

He put the chalk down and stood looking at the near board for a moment with the slate against his hip.

**"And I am not saying the two things this morning, and I will tell this yard why, because on Monday I stood four miles off up a lane and told a woman at a door that I did not know what the two signatures were for. That was the truest sentence I have said in four years in this yard. A man who has said a true sentence in a lane this week does not come back and say the other one in a yard at noon and get to have both of them be about the same thing."**

He put the slate down and picked up his coat.

**"One of those was asked for by a person. The other one gets said out loud because nine people happen to be standing here. I have been on both ends of that and I know which end I would rather be on."**

Then the rotation, because a Thursday takes it and a Thursday has taken it since before the fence at the north end went up.

**"A hundred and forty-three in force,"** the clerk said, **"twenty-nine taken, a hundred and fourteen not. No field was taken this morning and no field was due and nobody in this yard is to be blamed for a field that stood and waited, because it stood and waited for a reason and the reason is written in the book and not in this yard."**

Sera Quill had her slate out already with the two figures on it and the third line under them.

**"Ninety-first,"** she said. **"And seventy-eighth. Thirteen apart. The second one is carried forward and nothing printed in this holding moves it, and I have said that out loud in this yard three times now and I will say it again next time, and the thirteen is the part of this slate anybody can check."**

Kellan Rusk took the three off the rack one under the other and gave them the way they came, which is the form, then the charter, and then the line in Silling's book, and he kept his thumb against the edge of the shelf the whole way through.

**"Three hundred and ten days is what the form is at. The charter is at two hundred and sixteen. The ruled line in Silling's book is at three hundred and four and has nothing on it."**

He shut the rack.

**"That is three mornings this week that the last of those has needed a hand against the shelf to get out of my mouth, and three mornings that I have decided I do not like."**

Sera Quill read the small hand out under the figure rather than the figure itself, because on a Thursday that hand has been hers to read for two days.

**"The four hundred and thirteenth out of four hundred and fifteenth, written under a figure of four hundred and fourteen,"** she said. **"That is what the sheet says and I have read it the way it is written and not the way it sounds."**

She put the slate under her arm.

**"And four bodies of households, and four lines, and two signatures wanted on each of the four, and this yard has not put a column anywhere for what a signature agrees to. That is the difficulty of the whole week and it is not a figure, it is an absence."**

Sera Quill went along the shelves with her slate against her chest and read the standing of the holding off them without stopping, because she is the only person in this yard who checks them on her own account.

**"Thirty on the compost line, paid, and nothing let out. Eleven on the barrow. Seven sessions in the book and a Thursday took none. Fifty-three and fifty-three in the two other books. Six ruled rows for what we do not know, and no seventh. Thirty-nine blanks on the board in the one room, and the rule under them open and empty."**

She wrote nothing down.

**"All of that is the same as it was a week ago and I will check it again next week."**

Nia Vale moved the chair out from under the end of the long table so that two people could sit at it, which she does at the end of every week, and stopped with her hand on the back of it.

**"There is room at this end for two more people than there are papers on it,"** she said. **"The first has been lying here a hundred and thirteen days with about nine inches of clear board either side. The second is still folded back on its crease and has never once been opened. The third carries a name and a date and a hand and a seal, and every one of us has read it and there is nothing in it to complain of. Three papers this holding has carried for a fortnight without entering one, refusing one, or ruling a column under any of them."**

The ladder stands against the tool house wall with nobody on it, and has stood that way through every morning of this week.

The offer on the low board has not been picked up, has not been taken back, and has had no day written on it since it was put there. The ring of bare ground is bare and nobody has walked on it.

The man of about seventy was not fetched and no question was carried out to him at any site of this day. The door nine hundred yards off went past the man of about fifty at about the eleventh hour unread, and nobody in that yard offered him anything when he came back through the gate.

On this Thursday morning the round figure on the near board is still standing there with nothing after it, and nobody in that yard put anything to anybody, and the mason is going to look at it every morning for the rest of his working life.
