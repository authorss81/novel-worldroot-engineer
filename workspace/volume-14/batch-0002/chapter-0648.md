# Chapter 648

## The Twenty-First Of The Seventeenth, And A Clerk Up A Lane With His Own Boots On

Renn Ashby had the book propped open on the middle table with his thumb holding a page down, and he read the pair in the yard, because the yard is the only room in this holding where a figure said out loud does any work at all.

**"One hundred and seventy-six of three hundred and thirty-five,"** he said, **"and the heading over the pair is of a month nine months back, and it has not shifted since the first morning this pair was ever read in this yard, and I would be obliged if somebody would write that down as well."**

Nobody wrote it down, and he shut the book.

The two halves were in chalk on the wall with the rising one on the top line and the falling one underneath it, and the falling one was the one that had moved overnight. The clerk came round from the one room with his coat buttoned and looked at the wall for a while before he said anything.

**"The half that moved is the falling one,"** he said. **"This is a Saturday. A Saturday is an odd morning, and an odd morning takes the falling half now. It has gone over. The run before this one took the rising one first on the morning it closed, and if a man carries that across the boundary he will be wrong on every odd morning for a year and will never once catch himself doing it."**

He put his thumb under the figure that had not moved.

**"The rising one is a hundred and ninety-four and has not moved since Friday. A hundred and ninety-four of a hundred and eighty-four, which comes to three hundred and seventy-eight, and neither of those two halves is the other one and I am not going to add them in front of you for the sake of somebody in a county office."**

Hesta Lyle had the long sheet square in front of her and read the aggregate out with her hand flat on the edge of the paper rather than on the figure printed on it.

**"Four hundred and nine,"** she said, **"and the clause under it says the four hundred and eighth out of four hundred and tenth. Four lines under the whole of it and every one of them wants two signatures at the foot, and not one signature is on any of the four this morning, and there is still no column anywhere in this holding for what a signature would be agreeing to."**

The launder came down the middle road about half past eight with the water low in it, and the clerk said its figure before he had the gate open.

**"One thousand and two hundred and twenty-four hundredweight, and the four hundred and eleventh of it,"** he said. **"Five off yesterday. A Saturday is an odd morning and the launder takes the same five on an odd morning that the falling half takes its one."**

Then he did the thing that had not been done in the four years he had stood in this yard. He copied the third column out of the schedule onto a sheet of his own paper, in ink, in his own hand, and he folded it into his coat, and at the ninth hour he went out through the gate and up the middle road on foot.

He had his own boots on. There were four households on that column and he went to the nearest one, which was a mile and a quarter up a lane that turns once and then does not turn again, and he was gone four hours.

What he said when he came back, at the gate at half past one, in front of whoever happened to be standing there, was this.

**"Nobody signed. I did not ask anybody to sign. There is a difference between those two and this holding has spent a fortnight being careful about it, and I went up that lane to find out which of the two it would be and I have found out, and the answer is that a man standing on a step asking for two signatures is a man asking, and the house I went to asked me what the two signatures were for."**

He put his hand in his coat and left the paper where it was.

**"I told them I did not know. That is the truth and it took me about four seconds to say and about the whole of the walk to be sure of. There is no column in this holding for what the two signatures are for, and there is no column in the office's paper either, and I have now been to a doorstep and come back with that in my hand."**

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden was at the tap with a can in each hand when he said that, and she put one of them down on the step.

**"Then the paper has named a man to do a job and has not told him the job,"** she said. **"Write that down before either of us says anything clever about it."**

Nobody said anything clever about it. The clerk wrote it down badly and did not correct the writing, and that line is the reason the clerk is still standing in this yard at dusk with the third column in his coat.

Soren Rill walked the boards with the barrow and gave the two figures out loud on the way past without stopping at either of them, which is how he has given them for four years.

Seven hundred and ninety-five where he started. Eight hundred and forty-two at the other one. Forty-seven of wall in between, and the two of them have stood that far apart since the wall went up.

Behind the one he started at there is a drawer let into the masonry, and it is shut, and its key went onto the nail in the frame of the one room door at first light and will be on it at dusk.

Kellan Rusk read the three ages at the rack in the order he can hold them, which is the register form first, the charter off the shelf behind it second, and the second ruled line in Silling's own book third, which he did not take down off its nail. The form is at three hundred and five days. The charter is at two hundred and eleven. The third of the three is a line in Silling's own book, and it carries two hundred and ninety-nine, and there is nothing on the line under the figure, and that line is the only one of the three that is ever empty.

He read the last one twice.

**"Two hundred and ninety-nine,"** he said. **"And I will tell this yard something for nothing, because I have got nothing to sell. This is the last morning this holding ever reads that figure off that line. There is another one coming behind it, and a man who has read a line in a book for four years has to decide tonight what he is going to say when the number he has been waiting for arrives, because a number that arrives round is not like the other numbers and everybody in this yard knows that."**

Nobody answered him, and he put the book back on its nail with the ruled side out.

Nia Vale came along the table at about the fourth hour wiping it with her sleeve and stopping at the first sheet, as she does, and put two fingers down on the bare board either side of it to see whether there was room for another one.

**"Nine inches each side, a hundred and eight days on it, and no room for a second,"** she said. **"The second one is still folded on the crease it was folded on and nobody in this holding has opened it. The third has a name, a date, a hand and a seal and there is not a thing in it any of us can call wrong, and it has sat here through all of that without ever being entered or refused or ruled under, because this holding has never yet cut a column for a paper that came from an office."**

Nia Vale did the length of the shelves after the launder had gone, in the order she can reach them, and it took her the whole of a wet afternoon and gave her nothing to do at any of it.

**"Thirty paid on the compost line and not one load ever let out in it,"** she said. **"Eleven journeys on the barrow and no twelfth marked against the man, because eleven is a floor and a floor is somewhere to stand and not a figure to be beaten. Seven sessions in this holding's book and a Saturday takes none of them. Fifty-three in the requests and fifty-three in the section nine notes, and between the two of them they took nothing off that table this morning and nothing out of my coat."**

She stopped at the frame of the board in the one room and put her fingers on the wood beside a blank rather than in it.

**"Thirty-nine blanks, the same as yesterday, and I have never once put a name in one of them and I am not going to begin on a wet Saturday."**

Behind the seed house glass Tova Reed counted fifteen lines in the use log with the back of her hand and stopped where she was. The rotation does not come round on a Saturday, so the count in force stood where Friday had left it, a hundred and forty-one, twenty-nine of them taken off it and a hundred and twelve of them not. No field went out and none was due, and nobody was blamed for a field that waited.

Nobody in that yard said the two things this morning, and nobody gave a reason for not saying them, because the man in this yard who gives reasons for not saying them had gone a mile and a quarter up a lane with a sheet of his own paper in his coat, and a reason nobody gives is a thing that gets said the next time somebody asks.

The tap joint at the back of the tap house was put in on the ninth of this month and it weeps and it holds and the standing pipe behind it is the same pipe it was behind on the day it went in. Harlan Vetch put his thumb on the weep and took it off again and said nothing about it, and a Tuesday has now happened to that joint several times and has proved nothing whatever about it.

Nobody has put a hand on the ladder and no rung of it has gone. The offer on the low board lies where it was put, with no day on it, unpicked, unreturned. Beneath two words on the wall there are four ruled lines and every one of them is bare, and on the shelf in the one room six rows are ruled against what this holding does not know, with nothing cut above them for anybody.

Harlan Vetch came back across the yard at about the eleventh hour, stopped under his own four inches and two forks on the tool house wall long enough to be looked at, and went on. The ring of bare ground was still bare where he passed it, the man of about seventy had nothing fetched from him and nothing put to him at any hour, and the door nine hundred yards off went past the edge of the man of about fifty's eye without stopping him.

At dusk the clerk turned the yard key in the gate and put his hand flat on the wall under the falling half of the window for a moment on his way past, which is not a thing he does.

**"There is nothing written on that line and there is not going to be anything written on it,"** he said, to nobody, **"and I have not the least idea in the world what a man is supposed to say when a figure in a book comes round with an empty page behind it."**
