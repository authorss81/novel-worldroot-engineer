# Chapter 649

## The Twenty-Second Of The Seventeenth, And Three Hundred Standing On A Line With Nothing On It

The Sunday reading did not happen and the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden stood at the middle table and said so before anybody looked for the book.

**"It is not going to be read and I would rather it was my mouth that said so than somebody else's, and the reason is not that the day has gone wrong. A Sunday is the one morning in seven that a man in this yard can put both hands in his pockets and stand in a yard without anybody deciding he is waiting for something. Six mornings out of seven the reading is the first thing that makes this yard a yard. I am not taking the seventh one off it."**

Harlan Vetch, who was at the north end with his back to everybody, made a noise that two people heard and nobody remarked on.

The rest of a Sunday in this holding is a slower thing than the rest of the week, and the clerk did the rotation first, because it is the one job on a Sunday that has to be done at the same hour whatever else is or is not done. The count rose by one, and it rises by one and one only on a morning of this kind, and it has risen by one on every morning of this kind since the holding began keeping it.

**"Twenty-nine taken off it,"** the clerk said. **"A hundred and forty-two in force and a hundred and thirteen of them not."**

He wrote the third figure down before he wrote either of the other two, which he does on the mornings the rotation comes round.

**"No field went out this morning and no field was due, and there is nobody in this yard to blame for one that stood and waited, because waiting is what the rota had it doing and the rota is not a person."**

Sera Quill had her slate out before he had finished. There were two figures on it and a third line under them, and the two had never once agreed with each other since the day she started copying them, and they stood thirteen apart this morning and had stood thirteen apart every morning she has had them.

**"Ninetieth,"** she said. **"And seventy-seventh. Thirteen apart, and they have been thirteen apart since the beginning, and there is no printed rule in this holding that moves the second one. It is carried. I have said that out loud in this yard twice before and I will say it again, and the thirteen is the honest part of this slate, because the two figures never met and I am the only person who writes them down side by side."**

She turned the slate over and put it on the table face down, which she does when she has finished saying a thing she means.

Then she turned it back up, because the thing she had to say next could not be said with it face down.

She went and got Silling's book off its nail and carried it to the middle table and stood it on end in front of everybody, and the second ruled line was open in the middle of the page and there was not one mark on it of any kind, and the figure at the foot of that line was three hundred.

**"That is three hundred,"** she said. **"On Saturday it was two hundred and ninety-nine, and there was a man in this yard last night saying out loud that he had not the least idea what a man was supposed to say about it. So here is what I have to say about it, and I would like it written down in my words and not in somebody else's."**

**"Say it,"** Harlan Vetch said, from the north end.

**"A ruled line is not a pipe,"** Sera Quill said. **"A pipe takes what goes through it and is the same pipe afterwards. A ruled line takes a man. Three hundred on that line is not three hundred because anybody decided it. It is three hundred because a man wrote a number on every third morning for four years and the man did not stop to ask whether the number was round, and now it is round, and it is round on a morning when a field did not go out and did not come back, and there is still nothing written on the line."**

She put her hand flat on the page beside the figure and not on it, which is the way Hesta Lyle does it and which everybody in this yard can now recognise from four miles off.

**"So a round figure in a book does not mean anything has happened,"** she said. **"It means a man has been counting for four years and has arrived somewhere without noticing. I would rather this yard had a mason in it who will not put a figure on a wall than a clerk who thinks a round number is an event, and I have both, and this morning the mason was right."**

Nobody in the yard thanked her for it and nobody asked her to write it down twice.

At about the ninth hour the mason found it. He had been on the north end since before the yard was properly up and he came down the wall with his hands behind him and stopped in front of the table where the book was standing on end, and he read the figure, and then he read it again, and then he said the thing that made the morning.

**"Two hundred and ninety-nine, then three hundred, and not a mark on it either day,"** he said. **"That is the first figure I have ever seen in this holding that had no work behind it at all, and I do not mind it. Do not mistake that for agreement."**

Then the launder, which does have work behind it, and the wall, which does not.

**One thousand and two hundred and thirty-two hundredweight.**

**"Eight on,"** the clerk said. **"The four hundred and twelfth. The rising half is a hundred and ninety-five and the falling half is a hundred and eighty-four, and the falling half has not moved since Friday and this is a Sunday, which takes it. Three hundred and seventy-nine."**

Hesta Lyle had the long sheet square in front of her and her own hand flat on the edge of the paper rather than on the figure printed on it, and she gave the number first and the clause after it, which is her habit and has been since the first morning she stood at that table.

**"Four hundred and ten. And underneath it, in the small hand, the four hundred and ninth out of four hundred and eleventh."**

She moved her hand down the page.

**"Four lines for four bodies of households, and every one of the four is waiting on two signatures at its foot. None of the four has one this morning. None had one yesterday. On the strength of this week I would not promise one tomorrow."**

The near board was at seven hundred and ninety-six and the far one at eight hundred and forty-three, and behind the near one the drawer in the masonry was shut with its key on the nail, and neither of those two figures has ever been added to the window or to each other by anybody in this holding, because forty-seven is a distance and not a figure anybody sums.

Soren Rill came in off the middle road at about eleven with mud to the ankle for the fifth day running and came over to the table where Sera Quill had left her slate face up, and read the two figures off it, and then looked at the third line.

**"Ninetieth and seventy-seventh,"** he said. **"I am not saying the two things this morning and I am going to say why before anybody asks me, because I have walked four miles to say things out loud and I do not want to get to this yard and say one and leave without saying the other."**

He put his hand on the slate and did not move it.

**"Because I have been in three kitchens in three counties in the last nine days telling people what this holding's number is, and every one of them asked me the same second question, which is what happens to us, and I could not answer it, and if I stand in a yard at noon and say the other sentence then I have said a thing in a room that I could not say at three kitchen tables, and the two would not be the same afterwards. Being careful is the whole of what I have got and it is not going to be worth less tonight than it was on Friday."**

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden looked at him for a while and then went back to the tap.

Tova Reed went along the row of shelves at dusk on her way out and touched four things in order without stopping, which is how she checks them.

The compost line is paid at thirty and not one load has ever been let out in it. The barrow stands at eleven journeys, and eleven is a floor rather than a figure to beat. Seven sessions are entered in this holding's book and Sunday takes none of them. Six rows are ruled against what this holding does not know, and the space for a seventh is empty as it has been since the shelf was made.

**"The requests and the section nine notes are both at fifty-three,"** she said, **"and neither of them took anything off that table, and the first of those three papers has a hundred and nine days on it and about nine inches of bare board either side of it."**

A man of about seventy was fetched nothing this Sunday and was asked nothing at any hour of it, and Nia Vale put her own eyes on the ring of bare ground as she crossed the yard and it was bare.

The door nine hundred yards off went past twice and was read neither time. Nobody has gone up the ladder this week or any week. The offer on the low board is still where it was laid, with no day written on it, untaken, and not taken back.

Kellan Rusk read the three ages at the rack before he went home. Three hundred and six days on the register form. Two hundred and twelve on the charter. On the second ruled line in Silling's own book there was three hundred and nothing else, and this time it was Kellan Rusk who read that one out loud, and nobody in the yard said the word round after him.

What the day had not needed was a field, and what the room needed was somebody to open the board and look at it, and Nia Vale did that on her way past, because it is her habit on a Sunday and nobody has ever asked her to keep it.

Thirty-nine blanks, and the second rule underneath them standing open and empty the way it has stood since before the wall, and a rule that comes back empty opens nothing and has never once opened anything. Everybody in this holding was asked for nothing this morning and gave nothing to anybody, and the three sheets on the long table are still unentered and unrefused and uncolumned, and the second of them is folded back on a crease it was folded on before any of this began.
