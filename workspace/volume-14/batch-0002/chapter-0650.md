# Chapter 650

## The Twenty-Third Of The Seventeenth, And A Woman Working Out What Seed Reaches

Renn Ashby read the pair at the seventh hour with the cold still in the yard and four people standing close enough to hear it and one of them not close enough.

**"One hundred and seventy-seven of three hundred and thirty-seven,"** he said. **"The heading has not moved. That is the whole of what I have got this morning and I am not going to dress it up."**

Then he stood where he was, and the clerk waited for him, and he did not go, and in the end it was Renn Ashby himself who said out loud that he was not saying the two things and why.

**"Because I read a thing out of a book five mornings out of seven and a yard has decided that I am the man who does it,"** he said. **"That was not true this morning until half way through the second sentence, and it is not true on the mornings when a person is standing too far off to hear it."**

He shut the book and put his thumb on the shelf.

**"A man who has just been made into an office by a habit he did not choose does not get to use that office to say the hardest thing he knows. If you want the hardest thing from me then this yard has got the wrong man and the right habit, and I would rather be found out on a Tuesday than used on a Saturday."**

Then the wall, and the clerk with his coat still buttoned from the day before.

**One thousand and two hundred and twenty-seven hundredweight.**

**"Five off and the four hundred and thirteenth,"** he said. **"The falling half went up by one overnight and the rising one has not moved since Sunday. A hundred and ninety-five of a hundred and eighty-five, and three hundred and eighty, and the two are two figures and a man who adds them is a man who has decided the sum is more interesting than either of them."**

Behind the seed house glass, Tova Reed had moved her bench again. She did that four mornings ago and nobody asked her to and she said plainly at the time that she was not asking anybody to move anything either, and the bench is now on the seed house side of the glass, where a person sorting at the bench can see the whole length of the trough and the far end of it at the same time.

She was at the bench when the yard began, and she worked there all morning, and about the tenth hour she put both hands flat on the glass and said the thing she had been working at since Friday.

**"I have been counting how people come to me,"** she said. **"Not how many. How. A seed reaches anybody who is already standing where a seed can reach them. That is not a figure about people, it is a figure about a place, and the two are so close together in this work that a person loses the difference inside a fortnight."**

She turned round from the glass and looked at the yard rather than at the trough.

**"A household that comes to a door, that is a seed and it goes in. A household that has nowhere to put a tray because the floor is taken up with a sick person's bed, that is a seed and it does not go in. It is not refused. It is not refused by anybody. It simply has nowhere to sit down, and I have been putting a tray on the step of such a house and going away again and telling myself I had put it somewhere."**

Nia Vale came in from the gate at about half past ten and stood at the far end of the trough and did not go nearer, which is a thing she has done since Wednesday.

**"The pruning is taking the branches outside the zone,"** Tova Reed said. **"And a branch is not a wall. A wall stops a person. A branch takes the shade off a roof and puts a road where a footpath was and takes the last hour of light off a room where somebody sits all day. I am not asking anybody to feel sorry for me about it and nobody in this yard is going to, and I would like it written down that I am not asking."**

**"Then what are you asking for,"** Nia Vale said.

**"Nothing,"** Tova Reed said. **"I am working something out and I have not got to the end of it, and if I say the end of it now in this yard then somebody will hand me a decision I have not made and I will take it, because I am tired and it is easier, and a decision I have been handed is not a decision I will still hold when the frost comes off the road again."**

She went back to the bench and worked for another hour without saying anything at all, and the yard did not press her, because she had said out loud in front of everybody that she was not finished, and that turns out to be enough to stop a yard pressing anybody.

The aggregate on the long sheet is four hundred and eleven, and the small hand under the line reads the four hundred and tenth out of four hundred and twelfth, one step either way. Four bodies of households and four lines under the whole of it, and every one of the four wants two signatures at the foot of it, and there is not one signature on any of the four and no column anywhere in this holding for the thing a signature would be agreeing to.

Soren Rill did this wall the way he does it, from the far end back toward the gate, and gave the two figures from the wrong end of them. Eight hundred and forty-four at the far board. Seven hundred and ninety-seven at the near one. Forty-seven of walking between the two of them, which is a distance and not a figure this holding adds to anything else, and behind the near one, where he finishes, there is a drawer let into the masonry, shut, with one key on a nail in the frame of the one room door.

Nia Vale read the three ages off the rack in the order they were on the shelf, and gave the third one with her hand on the shelf beside it.

**"The form has been going three hundred and seven days, which is the figure the form keeps,"** Nia Vale said. **"The charter is at two hundred and thirteen. The line in Silling's book is at three hundred and one and has nothing at all on it, and that is the one of the three I have to say out loud every third day, because if I go round the rack silently I will come back to it and think there is writing on it."**

Kellan Rusk put the two books on the table together at about the ninth hour and opened both of them on the same spread so that anybody in the yard could see the two figures side by side, which he does when he wants the yard to look.

**"The requests are at fifty-three and the section nine notes are at fifty-three,"** he said, **"and both of those are the same figure and they are two books."**

He shut them.

**"Seven sessions in this holding's book and this Monday took none of them. Eleven journeys on the barrow and a twelfth not marked, because eleven is a floor. Paid at thirty on the compost line, and nothing let out in it, as always."**

Monday is not a morning the rota comes round on, so the count in force is where it was when the yard woke up: a hundred and forty-one, twenty-nine of them taken, a hundred and twelve of them not. It rose by nothing. No field went out and nobody in this yard is to be blamed for one that stood and waited, because standing and waiting is what the rota had it doing.

Nobody in this holding has ruled a column under any of the three papers on that table, and nobody has entered one and nobody has refused one.

The top one is face up and a hundred and ten days old, with about nine inches of bare board either side of it. The next is shut on a crease it was folded on long before any of this began. The third is the one with a name, a date, a hand and a seal on it, and there is not one thing in it that anybody here can put a finger on.

On the board in the one room the count of blanks stands at thirty-nine and it did not move on this Monday, and the second rule underneath them stands open and empty, and nobody put a fortieth name into any of them.

Four inches, and a mark that forks twice, and no third fork has ever appeared under it. Six ruled rows on the shelf for what this holding does not know, with the space above them for a seventh left clear. The man of about seventy has had nothing fetched from him and no question carried out. The ladder is not climbed. The low board still holds its offer, undated, unpicked, unreturned. The ring of bare ground has not been trodden. And the door nine hundred yards off went past the man of about fifty on his way back and he did not stop, and nobody in this yard said anything to him about that either.

What this yard did on this Monday morning was work, and Tova Reed worked at her bench with the whole trough in front of her until the light went off the far end of it, which is a thing a person does when they can see the far end and knows what is at it.
