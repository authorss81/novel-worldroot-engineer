# Chapter 657

## The Thirtieth Of The Seventeenth, And A Network Carrying More Than It Was Built To Carry

The pair was not read on the Monday and Tova Reed said so from the seed house bench before the light had come off the far end of the trough.

**"I have spent this week counting how people come to me and not how many,"** she said. **"If I stand in that yard at seven in the morning and read two figures out loud then the week turns into a thing about numbers, and it is not a thing about numbers. A man reading a pair out of a book on a Monday morning is doing a job. A woman who has been measuring a different thing all week and then reads a pair at seven has been put back on the old bench. So it is not read this morning and I am not asking anybody to agree with me about it."**

Then the rotation, which came round on the Monday the way it has come round on every fourth morning since the holding began counting.

**"A hundred and forty-four of them, and twenty-nine off that, leaving a hundred and fifteen not taken,"** the clerk said.

He put the slate down on the wall ledge.

**"That is the third morning of this kind in a fortnight and it has gone up by one and one only, and it will go up by one on the next one. It does not go up by two because somebody had a busy week, and I have seen people wait for that and I would rather they did not."**

He put the slate down.

**"No field was taken this morning. No field was due. Nobody in this yard is to be blamed for a field that stood and waited, and I have said that on every rotation morning since I began keeping this and I will go on saying it until I stop keeping it, which is a different matter entirely."**

Sera Quill turned her slate over. She had it face down on the table all morning and she brought it up at noon and read both figures off it without looking at the paper, because she has had them on her for nine years.

**"I am not saying the two things this morning either,"** she said, **"and the reason is that there is a figure on this slate that anybody in this yard is entitled to check, and the day I start mixing a permanent sentence in with the two numbers that never agree is the day this slate stops being a thing anybody can lean on. The second of the two is carried forward and nothing printed in this holding moves it. I have said that out loud four times in a yard now and I will say it again, and the thirteen is still the honest part of this slate."**

**"Ninety-second,"** she said, **"and seventy-ninth. Thirteen apart, the way they have been thirteen apart since the first morning I copied them."**

The launder came down the middle road about half past eight for the first time in a week with the road under it firm enough to take a loaded barrow, and the clerk was at the wall.

**One thousand and two hundred and forty-four hundredweight.**

**"The rising half is a hundred and ninety-nine and moved overnight, and the falling half is a hundred and eighty-eight and has not moved since Sunday,"** the clerk said. **"That is a window of three hundred and eighty-seven."**

He turned the slate over and wrote on the back of it.

**"The launder is at one thousand and two hundred and forty-four hundredweight, which is eight on and the four hundred and twentieth. And a yard can watch a launder go by for a whole morning and not see the pair change, which is the one thing I would like anybody to take away from a Monday."**

Kellan Rusk emptied the rack into his own two hands before he said anything, which is what he has done every morning since Sunday, and neither hand went near the shelf while he was doing it.

**"On the form, three hundred and fourteen days. On the charter, two hundred and twenty. In Silling's own book, on the second ruled line, three hundred and eight with nothing written on it."**

**"Four mornings running now that an empty line has been said out loud in this yard,"** he said, **"and it has still got nothing on it, and I am going to go on giving it out until somebody with more standing than I have comes and tells this holding what belongs on that line."**

He put the book back on its nail.

Hesta Lyle had her own sheet out on the middle table with the ruled line down the middle of it, and she had written on the left of the line and not on the right, and she read it out in her own hand without looking up.

**"Three places have been reached,"** she said. **"Four houses, four lines, two signatures wanted on each. On the Thursday before this one the man of about fifty was let in at one of them and asked what this holding stands at, and told the answer, and no signature went on the line. On the Friday the second place was not reached because the second place and the fourth place cannot both be reached on one day with what there is."**

She put her finger on the right of the ruled line, which is blank.

**"That is the cost. It is on the right of the line and it is not going on any of the four lines and it is not going in the requests or the section nine notes because those are at fifty-three and fifty-three and they are not going to be moved for this. It is not shared out. It is not halved and it is not spread over the week. It is one place not reached, and the person who was going to be asked there has not been asked, and that is a true sentence and it stays a true sentence however many mornings I stand in this yard and read it."**

Nobody in that yard said the word temporary.

**"I want it written down that nobody in this holding has said that word, and I want it written down that nobody has said the network can carry it, either,"** she said. **"Three houses are three houses. This yard was built to answer for one and it is answering for three, and a road that is soft for six days a week is the reason it cannot answer for four, and neither of those two sentences is anybody's fault and neither of them is going away."**

The near board is eight hundred and four. The far one is eight hundred and fifty-one. Forty-seven of wall separates them and neither of the two has ever been added to the window or to the other. Behind the near one the drawer in the masonry stands shut, and on the nail in the one room door there is a key for a man who asked for six weeks to be noticed with him and has not been noticed with anybody.

Thirty on the compost line, paid, and not one load let out in it in four years. Eleven journeys marked on the barrow and a twelfth refused, because eleven is where it is put and not where it is aiming. Fifteen ruled lines in the use log and the space above them empty since the log was started. Seven sessions in this holding's book; this Monday entered none. And on the shelf in the one room there are six ruled rows against what this holding does not know, with no seventh cut for the schedule, or the column, or the paper, or the other thing.

Three papers, one table, a fortnight, and not one of them moved. The top one is a hundred and seventeen days old and has about nine inches of clear board either side of it. The middle one is shut on its old fold. The bottom one carries a name, a date, a hand and a seal, and the whole of this holding could not put a finger on anything wrong with it. Entered, none of them. Refused, none of them. Ruled under, none of them, because this holding has never cut a column for a paper that came from an office and there is not going to be one.

The board in the one room carries its thirty-nine blanks, and underneath them the second rule is open and empty and opens nothing, and nobody put a fortieth name into any of them this morning.

The man of about fifty looked at the two boards on his way in and said nothing about them, and behind him on a nail there is a key and six weeks that nobody has noticed with him.

The ladder is against the tool house wall with nobody on it. The offer on the low board has no day written on it, and has not been picked up, and has not been taken back. The ring of bare ground has not been walked on.

Nothing was fetched from the man of about seventy and nothing was put to him anywhere on this day. And the door nine hundred yards off went past the man of about fifty's eye at the eleventh hour, unread, and was not offered to him or to anybody else.

The clerk gave the long sheet this morning standing at the end of it rather than sitting down, because a Monday is a day the yard is back at work and does not want a ceremony.

**"Four hundred and eighteen on the sheet,"** he said, **"and the clause in the small hand under it reads the four hundred and seventeenth out of four hundred and nineteenth. That has been the shape of that clause every morning this holding has kept it."**

He put two fingers on the wood below the paper.

**"Underneath, four bodies of households and four lines, and every one of the four is short the two signatures it wants at the foot. This holding has not cut a column for what a signature would agree to, and a column for that is the office's paper and not ours."**

Dusk came and the clerk turned the yard key with the one other key in this holding on its nail behind him, and the third rotation of the fortnight had come round and gone by that morning with no field taken and nobody blamed for one that waited, and the yard that was built to answer for one household is answering for three, and the whole of the week added up to a road and a ford and a pencil and one place that is still not reached, and nobody in the yard had been asked to pick up any of it.
