# Chapter 664

## The Seventh Of The Eighteenth, And What Comes Back Up The Road After A Refusal

Renn Ashby read the pair at the seventh hour on the Monday and read it into a yard that was quieter than a Monday usually is, and he did not fill the quiet with anything.

**"One hundred and eighty-four of three hundred and fifty-one,"** he said. **"Of this month. The heading has not moved and I am not going to talk about the heading again this week."**

A man came up the middle road at about the ninth hour on foot, from the third place, and he had walked it. He would not sit down and he would not take anything out of his coat except a folded paper he did not open.

**"The head sluice is not going to be opened at the fifth hour again this season,"** he said.

Nobody in that yard said anything for about three seconds.

**"Say the rest of it,"** Soren Rill said.

Soren Rill did not raise his voice, and he did not move away from the gate either, and the man off the sluice looked at him for a moment before he answered.

**"That is the rest of it. The head sluice. Fifth hour. Not again this season."**

Harlan Vetch put the hod down.

**"It is not broken,"** the man said, before anybody had asked. **"It is not been broken and it is not been damaged and nobody has had a hand on it. It opens and it shuts and it has opened every morning since it was built, including this morning, at the second hour, because it opens at the second hour as well as at the fifth and nobody asked me to stop doing that either."**

Somebody at the trough asked why, and nobody stopped him, and nobody backed him up either.

**"I am not going to tell you why,"** the man said. **"You did not ask me a question that I owe you the answer to. You asked me whether it is broken and I told you it is not, and that is the whole of what I came with."**

He put the folded paper back in his coat.

**"Here is what it costs and I am telling you the cost and not the reason. Your water comes round the long way now. It comes round by the north ditch and along the top and in behind the far tap, and a long way takes its own time whatever the weather is doing. On a hard week your far tap is dry by the third hour. On a soft week it is all right. That is the cost and it is not a threat and it does not go away at the end of the season."**

Marek was standing at the north-side room door with the chalk in his hand and he put it down on the floor and stood there.

**"The third hour,"** he said.

The man off the sluice nodded at him without softening anything in his face.

**"The third hour, on a hard week. On a soft week you would not notice it at all, and a soft week is not a plan."**

Marek came out of the doorway and stood on the step in front of it, with the chalk still in his hand.

**"That room has one tap in it,"** he said, **"and it is at the end of the trough, and in a hard week the trough has been empty by the second hour for three of the last four weeks. I have a cistern going in on that wall this week and a cistern on a dry tap is a box."**

The man off the sluice looked at the chalk in Marek's hand and then at the doorway behind him and did not answer the cistern at all.

**"Then you know what I have come to tell you,"** he said, and he put his cap on and went down the middle road the way he had come, on foot, and this holding got nothing else out of him.

Nobody apologised to anybody about Sunday. Nobody in that yard thanked anybody for standing still on Monday. The man of about fifty came back from the long walk along the hedge at about the tenth hour, having heard the whole of it from three people, and nobody said a word to him about Sunday and nobody asked him whether he agreed.

Then the launder, which came down about half past eight while all of that was happening two hundred yards away.

**One thousand and two hundred and forty-eight hundredweight.**

**"Five off, and the four hundred and twenty-seventh of the run,"** the clerk said. **"A Monday is an odd morning, so the half of that window which fell in the night is a hundred and ninety-two and the half that stood where it stood on Saturday is two hundred and two. The two of them come to three hundred and ninety-four and I have not added them in front of anybody today."**

The window was three hundred and ninety-four that Monday morning, the rising half standing at two hundred and two where it stood on Saturday, and the falling half up at a hundred and ninety-two overnight, and nobody in that yard added them together.

A Monday is not a morning the rotation comes round on. The number in force is a hundred and forty-four, with twenty-nine gone out at their own hour and a hundred and fifteen still standing at this hour. Nothing went out of this holding today that was due out of it and there is nobody here who will be blamed for a field that waited.

Hesta Lyle took the long sheet up off the table after the man off the sluice had gone and stood it against her chest the way a person holds a thing she has finished with for the day.

**"Four hundred and twenty-five,"** she said. **"That is the only figure on the whole of that sheet and it is the whole of what this holding answers for this week, and it has gone up by one a morning since before I started standing here."**

She put two fingers on the small hand underneath it and did not read that part of the page at all for a moment.

**"Underneath there are two more figures, one on either side of that number, and they have sat underneath it for four years and neither of them has ever been the number itself. The lower one is the four hundred and twenty-fourth. The upper one is the four hundred and twenty-sixth. And the hand that writes them is not my hand and did not arrive with me."**

She laid the sheet back down flat and squared the top edge against the table.

**"Whoever is keeping the next one of these ought to be told that I am the voice and not the author."**

Sera Quill had the slate out and turned it face up, and everybody in that yard looked at her to see whether she was going to say the two sentences, and she did not say them and she did not give a reason either.

**"I gave one on Monday of last week and this yard has it and I am not going to improve on it,"** she said. **"A person who finds a fresh reason every morning of the week is a person who is enjoying it, and I have watched four people in this yard enjoy themselves in a small way and I have not enjoyed any of it."**

She turned the slate over and left it face down on the table.

**"I am not saying them,"** she said. **"That is the whole of what I have to say about it and I would like that written down as a fact about a Monday and not as a refusal to work."**

Kellan Rusk read the three ages off the rack into the north-side room, where the cistern wall was going to be, and read them in the order Harlan Vetch can hold them, which is backwards.

**"The line in Silling's book is at three hundred and fifteen and it has nothing on it. The charter is at two hundred and twenty-seven. The register form is at three hundred and twenty-one days. That is the third of them to be the one with nothing written behind it in the space of four days and I have started to notice that."**

He put the book on its nail and then took it off the nail and looked at the line again, and put it back.

**"And that is the second time this week I have done that, and I have not told anybody, and I am telling the room now instead."**

Harlan Vetch carried the three figures up to the wall on the back of his hand while he was doing his own work and gave them out loud without stopping, in the order he could hold them without looking at anything.

**"Three hundred and fifteen on the line and nothing on it. Two hundred and twenty-seven on the charter. Three hundred and twenty-one days on the form."**

He wiped the dust off his hand on his coat.

**"That is the order they come off my arm and not the order they sit on the shelf, and it is the only order I can do at this height without putting the hod down."**

The clerk read the two boards off the slate instead of off the wall, because the wall was behind him and he had the slate in his hand and this is a thing he has done perhaps nine mornings in four years.

This end of the wall is at eight hundred and eleven. The far one, behind the tool house, is at eight hundred and fifty-eight. Forty-seven of wall between them and forty-seven of nothing else, and neither figure has ever gone on to the window or onto each other in this holding. Behind the near board the drawer in the stone is shut. Its key is on the single nail inside the one room doorway, and it has been on that nail at every hour of this day and will be at dusk.

The man of about fifty was asked about the head sluice by three people and answered none of them, and instead said, at the gate, with his hand on the gate board:

**"That line is paid at thirty-one and it has not been let out in a load in four years,"** he said, **"and I have been in this yard four years and that is the only figure I have ever been asked to be an opinion about by nobody. It is not going to change because a man came up a road this morning and told us where the water will be."**

He let go of the gate board and went back to his own work.

Nia Vale came past the gate on her way to the trough and put two fingers in the chalk of that line and read it, and did not write anything next to it.

**"Thirty-one,"** she said, **"and still thirty-one, and nothing has ever been let out of it. And nobody in this yard decided that and nobody in this yard knew it before the board said so, and this morning the board said the same thing it said on Thursday, which is what a board is for."**

Marek went at the standing of the holding for himself in the north-side room, because a cistern cannot be built by a man who does not know how many days of work this holding has ever entered into a book, and he said so before he said any of it.

**"I want the sessions figure and I want it because I want a mason and a day with a mason, and a day with a mason is a session, and this holding has seven sessions entered in its book in four years and it entered none this morning and it entered none on Sunday."**

He came out into the yard with the book still under his arm.

**"Seven sessions,"** he said. **"The barrow stands at eleven journeys and no twelfth. The use log is at fifteen lines with nothing above the fifteenth. The requests and the section nine notes are both at fifty-three and neither of them took the man off the sluice, because neither of those books has a column for a man off a sluice."**

He put the book on the middle table and squared it to the edge.

**"And thirty-nine blanks on the board in the one room, and the second rule under them open at the thirty-ninth time, and six ruled rows on the shelf against what we do not know, with nothing cut above them. Nobody has fetched the man of about seventy this month and his standing in this holding is twenty-nine fetchings, and nobody has ever put the number to him."**

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden carried her cans out to the far tap at about the eleventh hour, which means she crossed the yard, and she went around the ring of bare ground rather than over it, the way she has gone around it every day for four years without ever saying why.

**"Thirty-one at the gate,"** she said on the way back. **"Eleven journeys on the barrow. Fifteen lines in the log. Seven sessions, and today took none. Fifty-three and fifty-three in the two other books. And the first of those three papers on the long table is a hundred and twenty-four days old, with nine inches of clear board either side of it, and the second is shut on its fold, and the third is the one with the seal, and I have looked at that third one more times than I have looked at my own."**

She set the cans on the step.

**"None of them entered, none refused, no column cut under one. And the ladder has nobody on it and the offer on the low board has nothing written on it that would say when. The joint at the back weeps and holds and the standing pipe behind it is the same pipe, and a Monday is not a Tuesday and has never once claimed to be."**

The door nine hundred yards off went past at about the eleventh hour and was not read, and nobody in that yard put it in front of anybody, and nobody offered it to the man who had walked up the middle road and back down it.

Marek stood at the north-side room door with the chalk in his hand for a while after the yard had gone quiet, and he understood that there was nothing he could do about the third hour that had not already been done by nine people refusing to put a name at the top of a piece of paper on Sunday, and he understood that he had been one of the nine and that his was the standing that had put a pipe in the ground at the north end of this yard on Tuesday.
