# Chapter 661

## The Fourth Of The Eighteenth, And A Figure That Comes Out Round In The Middle Of A Run

The pair was not read on the Friday and the man of the north row with the cough was the one who stood in front of the middle table and said so, before the book was out of the rack.

**"It is off this morning and I am the one saying so, and I will say why this once and then not again,"** he said. **"A Friday is the morning I walk up to the office for my own paper and I am on that road at the sixth hour with the gate still shut behind me. There are four counties between this yard and that door and one of them is nine miles of nothing that stops being nothing the moment it rains."**

He put his hand flat on the table to lean on and then took it off again, as if he had caught himself doing it.

**"That is all. It is not that the morning has gone wrong. It is that a man of seventy-six goes up for his paper while there is still light and comes back when there is none, and I have done that every Friday since before this yard had a trough."**

The cart came up the fen road at about the ninth hour with two men on it who were not from this holding, and neither of them said anything, and they went as far as the wall, put it down where Soren Rill pointed, and went back down the road.

> A return, on the interval this holding has always used and has never written down anywhere anybody can go and read. This yard keeps no ordinal for one and no weight for one, and it keeps neither of those things the way it keeps everything else here, which is by not carrying them. The board in the one room stands at its thirty-nine blanks, and that figure is not altered by a cart at a wall. Underneath those blanks the second rule is open and empty and opens nothing, and the standing for how many times it has stood that way is the thirty-ninth time.

Then the rotation, which came round on a Friday the way it has come round on every morning of this kind since the holding began counting.

The clerk took the slate off the wall ledge and held it the way a man holds a thing he is about to read out of rather than the way a man holds a thing he has already read.

**"A hundred and forty-five in force,"** he said, and put the last of the three on the slate before the first of the other two, because the last of the three is the one he wants a yard to be holding when it stops listening.

Harlan Vetch put his hod down against the wall to have both hands, which took him longer than the number took.

**"Twenty-nine of those gone out at their own hour. A hundred and sixteen of them not gone out yet, and that is the half that a person is tempted to read as a debt and it is not a debt, it is a Tuesday's worth of mornings with nobody's name at the end of them yet."**

He put the slate down on the wall ledge.

**"Six mornings of this kind in this season and every one of them has added a single one and not a single one more than that, and the next one will do exactly the same, and in four years it has never gone up by two because somebody had a busy fortnight."**

He squared the slate against his thigh.

**"No field went out of this holding this morning and none was due out of it, and nobody in this yard is to be blamed for a field that stood and waited, because waiting is what the rota has that field doing."**

Sera Quill had the slate out before he had finished and turned it face up on the table.

**"Ninety-third,"** she said. **"And eightieth. Thirteen apart, and they have been thirteen apart on every morning I have had them, and I would like this yard to keep treating thirteen as the honest part of this slate and not as something that is going to be corrected one day."**

She put her finger on the third line under the two figures.

**"The second of those two is carried forward. It is carried, and nothing printed in this holding moves it, and I have said that out loud in a yard five times now and I will say it a sixth. The two figures never met. I am the only person who writes them down side by side."**

The launder came down about half past eight and the clerk was at the wall before it had finished arriving, and this morning it came to a figure with no tail on it.

**One thousand and two hundred and fifty hundredweight.**

He put the chalk down and did not write anything for a moment, which nobody in this yard had seen him do.

**"Eight on, and the four hundred and twenty-fourth of the run,"** he said. **"And that figure has come out round in its last two figures on a morning that already has a cart at the wall and the rotation coming round and a man from the north row standing at a table saying he is too old for a road."**

Kellan Rusk came away from the rack with his hand still on the shelf and looked at the figure on the wall, and then took his hand off the shelf.

**"It is a number in a run,"** he said. **"Two numbers both went up by one on the same morning and this one came out with nothing after it. That is all it is. I would like it written down in this yard that no one has said the word season and no one has said the word sign and no one has said the word change, because I have watched forty people say all three of those about a figure with a tail on it and every one of them was wrong."**

He shut the rack and then went along to the board in the one room door frame and looked at it without touching it.

**"Thirty-nine blanks on that board, and the second rule underneath them open and waiting at the thirty-ninth time, and there is no morning of the four years on which that rule has opened one thing. Six ruled rows on the shelf beside it for what this holding does not know, and no seventh cut for a cart at a wall or for a figure with no tail on it."**

He came back and put his hand on the shelf again, and then took it off again.

**"I have been a bookkeeper in this holding for four years and I am good at numbers the way Harlan Vetch is good at a wall, and neither of those is the same as being good at what a number means. A round figure in a book means a man has been counting without noticing. That is all it has ever meant."**

The window on that Friday was three hundred and ninety-one, and the rising half had gone up in the night to two hundred and one because a Friday is an even morning and an even morning takes the rising half, and the falling half stood where Thursday left it at a hundred and ninety. The two were two figures and they were not added together in front of anybody.

Hesta Lyle sat down at the long sheet rather than standing at it, which she does on the mornings when she has something she has already said once and is going to say again.

**"I have learned to be able to tell anybody in this yard what it will do before it does it,"** she said. **"The count comes up by one. The launder comes down at half past eight. The second place on my sheet is the one that cannot be reached. There is a road, and there is a ford, and there are four places and five working days and three of the four get a man to the door this week."**

She put her hand flat on the wood beside the sheet and not on the sheet.

**"So I am not going to say the hardest sentence I know out loud in a yard that has become that predictable. It is not a better sentence. It is simply that a yard where everybody knows what Tuesday will do before Tuesday has nothing left to say at noon on a Friday, and I have noticed the difference in myself and I would rather be the person who noticed it than the person who did not."**

She drew the sheet toward her and squared the top edge with her thumbnail, and the yard waited while she did it, which it would not have done on Monday.

**"Four hundred and twenty-two on the sheet,"** she said, going back to it the way she always goes back to it, **"and the small hand under it reads the four hundred and twenty-first out of four hundred and twenty-third. Four bodies of households and four lines under the whole of them, two signatures asked for at the foot of each, and not one signature on any of the four this morning, on a day with a cart at the wall and a man in the yard."**

Sera Quill read the three ages off her own slate rather than off the rack, because she had copied them onto it on Wednesday and had not been near the shelf since.

**"Register form, three hundred and eighteen days. Charter, two hundred and twenty-four. The second ruled line in Silling's book, three hundred and twelve, and nothing written on it, and there is no seventh copy of that number anywhere in this holding because there is only one man who writes that line."**

She put the slate in her coat.

The clerk came back from the wall with the slate turned over so that the blank back of it was what showed, and stood against the near board and read the two boards off the wall behind it.

Eight hundred and five... no, near eight hundred and eight and far eight hundred and fifty-five. He had them in the right order, near first, and he gave them without turning round.

**"Eight hundred and eight at this end, eight hundred and fifty-five at the one by the tool house, forty-seven of wall and forty-seven of nothing else."**

He turned the slate back over.

**"Behind this end there is a drawer let into the stone. It is shut. Its key is inside the one room door frame on the only nail that has anything on it, and it has been on that nail since before I came, and if a man of about fifty asks me this afternoon whether anybody has ever opened that drawer the answer is no and it will still be no this afternoon."**

Marek had the tape out of his coat, because he meant to measure the room at the north side after he had eaten, and he measured the three papers on the long table instead and told the yard what they came to.

**"The first of them is a hundred and twenty-one days old and it has the same width of bare board either side of it that it had on Monday,"** he said. **"I have put a tape on that gap four times this week and it is nine inches every single time. The second is shut on a fold. The third is signed and dated and sealed and named, and I can read the seal from where I am standing, which is the whole difficulty with that one."**

He put the tape back in his coat and left the papers exactly where he found them.

**"None of the three entered. None refused. This holding has never cut a column under anything that came out of an office, and I have watched a man refuse to sign a thing for nine days and watched a woman in three county offices write down that a thing cannot be reached, and neither of those is a column."**

Harlan Vetch went out to the gate at about the eighth hour and looked at the compost line on the gate board on his way past and did not look at it at all, which took him slightly longer than looking at it would have.

**"Thirty-one since Thursday,"** he said, when somebody asked him whether he had seen the new figure. **"I have seen it. I am not going to have a conversation about it, because a man who has a conversation about a figure at a gate is a man who has started expecting it to be a different figure tomorrow."**

He carried the hod off the wall and went back up to the north end.

The man of the north row read the use log over his own shoulder at the one room door, which he has done at that door for nine years without ever once being asked to.

**"Fifteen lines and no sixteenth, and the sixteenth was never ruled. That is not a thing somebody decided. That is a thing a man made a log with and then did not need."**

He came down the step.

**"Eleven journeys on the barrow and no twelfth. Seven sessions in this holding's book and a Friday takes none. The two other books are each at fifty-three. Six ruled rows on that shelf for what we do not know and no seventh, and there is no seventh for the cart at the wall or for the man who brought it. And the line at the gate is at thirty-one this morning and nothing has ever been let out of it."**

He looked along the yard toward the back of the tap house and stopped for a moment.

**"And I can hear that joint from that bench,"** he said. **"Nine years and I have never said so to anybody in this yard and I am not saying it this morning either, but it is there, and a man who is seventy-six is going to tell you the things he can hear whether or not anybody asked him."**

At about the eleventh hour the door nine hundred yards off went by a man walking the long way back from the hedge and nobody read it and nobody offered it to him. A man of about seventy was standing at the trough at the ninth hour with a can in each hand and nothing was fetched from him and nothing was put to him at any hour of this day.

The ladder stands against the tool house wall and no rung of it has gone. The offer on the low board has nothing written on it that would say when, and no hand has gone near it, and it has not been drawn down. The ring of bare ground inside the eleven acres has not been walked on and has not been measured and has not been priced.

Two figures came round this holding on the same Friday morning, one of them a count of people that goes up by one and one of them a weight that came out with nothing after it, and neither of them was an occasion, and neither of them was anybody's fault.
