# Chapter 659

## The Second Of The Eighteenth, And A Round Figure Sitting On One Side Of A Pair

The pair was not read on the Wednesday and Marek was the one who said so, at the gate end of the yard, with the straightedge still under his arm.

**"It is not read this morning because I have a chalk line and a straightedge and one clean hour to lay it in that room at the north side, and the hour that line wants is seven,"** he said. **"There is one hour a day when nobody in this holding interrupts me and it is not because of any standing I have. It is because at seven the others are at a table. I would rather have that hour with nobody behind me than have a number in the air at seven, and either of those two is a better use of a Wednesday morning."**

He went off up the yard with the straightedge against his leg.

Nia Vale did not move from the long table, and she had her own reason ready, and she gave it before anybody had finished asking her.

**"There are two sentences this holding knows by heart and I am not going to say either of them out loud today,"** she said. **"The first of those three papers has gone past a hundred and nineteen days and every one of us has had that figure in our mouths since Tuesday, and today is the morning it is a fresh number rather than yesterday's number."**

She turned the cloth over on her shoulder.

**"If a sentence about the whole of a thing goes into the same breath as that one, then the paper on this table stops being a piece of paper that nobody has answered and turns into an occasion. It has happened twice in four years and it took a week to get back to a sheet of paper both times."**

The launder came down the middle road about half past eight with the road under it firm for the first time in six days, and the clerk was at the wall.

**One thousand and two hundred and forty-seven hundredweight.**

**"Eight on, and that is the four hundred and twenty-second of the run,"** he said. **"A Wednesday is an even morning and an even morning takes eight on the launder and takes the rising half of the window up by one."**

He put the chalk against the wall and left it against the upper of the two lines while he said the next thing.

**"The rising half is two hundred."**

He took the chalk off the wall and waited, which he does not usually do.

**"I will say it once more slowly, because this holding has not had that figure on that wall before in the four years I have stood here. It is two hundred. It is not a hundred and something, and it is not two hundred and something. The way a split half of this window is written changes at two hundred and not at a hundred, so the round one loses its and and there is no tail on it at all. Three hundred and eighty-nine of a window, of which that round figure is one half and a hundred and eighty-nine is the other."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the north end about the ninth hour and stood in front of the wall and put his thumb under the upper figure without touching it.

**"Two hundred,"** Harlan Vetch said. **"And there is nothing whatever behind it. That is what four years of watching two boards teaches a man, and this morning it has taught me something else besides."**

He kept his thumb where it was and shifted it a quarter inch along the chalk without lifting it off the wall.

**"One half went up by one and the other half went up by one and at some point along there the sum comes out round. Nobody chose it. Nobody stood at this wall and thought about a round figure. Two numbers doing the only thing two numbers do arrived at a figure with no tail on it, and that is the whole of the event."**

Somebody at the middle table wanted to know what he would like it to mean, and said so.

Harlan Vetch took his hand off the wall and looked at the print it had left on the chalk dust for a moment before he answered.

**"I would like it to mean nothing, and it does, and I would like the yard to leave it alone."**

A Wednesday is not a morning the rotation comes round on. The clerk read the rota book off the shelf the way other people read a sky, and gave the number without being asked for it, and gave it in one breath and did not put it down.

A hundred and forty-four in force, of which twenty-nine are gone out at their own hours and a hundred and fifteen have not gone out yet. No field left the holding today and none was due out of it, and there is nobody in this yard to hold responsible for a field that stood where the book had it standing, because a book is not a person and never claimed to be.

Hesta Lyle had the long sheet turned a quarter round on the table because the wind had got hold of the corner of it, and she read it upside down without turning it back, which saves the paper and takes her twice as long.

**"It is four hundred and twenty,"** she said, **"and the small hand under it is the four hundred and nineteenth out of four hundred and twenty-first. I read that hand upside down about twice a year and I have never once read it wrong, and that is not a talent. It is nine years of looking at a shape."**

She turned the sheet the right way round and left her palm flat on the wood beside it.

**"Four bodies of households and four lines underneath the whole of them, two signatures asked for at the foot of every one, and none of the four has one this morning. And the office's day is the twenty-ninth and there are three places that can be reached and one that cannot, and that has not changed since Friday and it is not going to change before Thursday."**

Kellan Rusk gave the three ages off the rack in the order he can say them without stopping, which is not the order they sit in, and he named the order before he started so that nobody had to guess.

**"Charter first. Two hundred and twenty-two. Then the register form at three hundred and sixteen days. Then the line in Silling's book at three hundred and ten, and there is nothing written on that line and I have stopped putting my hand on the shelf to say so."**

He put the book back on its nail and took his hand away from the nail as well.

**"I want that noticed. Four years of a hand on a shelf and I have put it down twice this week, and the second time was because a man at the north end told me on Sunday that a figure being a habit instead of a report starts at the hand."**

Nia Vale came out from under the eaves and read the two boards from where she was standing, which is a different way of reading them and takes in both at once.

Eight hundred and six at the near end and eight hundred and fifty-three at the one behind the tool house, and forty-seven of wall between them, which is a walk and not an addition. Behind the near one the drawer in the masonry is shut and has been shut for four years, and the key to it is on a nail inside the door frame of the one room and it was on that nail before the yard was up.

The clerk came to the long table with a sheet of his own paper in his hand and put it down on the bare board beside the three, face down, and kept his palm flat on the back of it.

**"That is my own paper and it is not a fourth paper on this table,"** he said. **"There are four households on the office's column and I have written the address of each one at the head of a sheet of mine, and all four of those sheets are in my coat and not in either book, because neither of the two books has a column for a sheet a man wrote himself in his own hand at his own table."**

He left his hand on it while he said the rest.

**"So you have four pieces of paper on that table now,"** Nia Vale said, with her chin up.

The clerk turned his own sheet over on the board so that the writing on it faced the sky, and left it there, and that was his whole answer to her for about four seconds.

**"You have three pieces of paper on that table now,"** he said. **"About nine inches of clear board either side of the first one, which came up the fen road at the ninth hour of a day two of you were not here for. The second is folded shut on an old crease. The last one is signed and dated and sealed and named. Entered, none of them. Refused, none of them. Ruled under, none of them."**

He put his hand in his coat and left the sheet where it was.

Kellan Rusk went out to the gate with a stick of chalk and put his thumb in the compost line where it is kept, and found the chalk there and took his thumb out again.

**"Paid at thirty, and nothing has ever been let out of it,"** he said. **"That is four years and the first week of this one and it will be four years and the last week of this one. If a man in this yard ever lets a load out of that line then somebody has to write down why on the same line, and I have never once had to write that."**

Soren Rill came past the yard end with the barrow over his shoulder and somebody called out at him about the twelfth mark on it.

**"No,"** he said, without stopping. **"Eleven journeys is on it, and eleven is where it is put, not where it is aiming. If I put a twelfth on there today for the sake of a morning that looks busy then in six weeks somebody will be standing in this yard telling me I should have made thirteen, and there is no weather in this county that would have made thirteen a right thing to do."**

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden was at the tap with a can going in and a can coming out, and she gave the rest of the standing of this holding in the order she can reach it going out of the yard rather than coming in, which reverses it.

**"Fifteen lines written in the use log behind that glass and the space for a sixteenth as blank as a Monday. Seven sessions in this holding's book and Wednesday has never once taken one. The requests and the section nine notes are both at fifty-three and between them they have taken nothing out of anybody's coat today. Thirty-nine blanks on the board in the one room, and the rule under them standing open and empty at the thirty-ninth time, and it has opened nothing this morning or any morning."**

She set the second can down on the step.

**"Six ruled rows on the shelf against what we do not know and no seventh cut for anything anybody has said out loud this week. And a man of about seventy stands at twenty-nine fetchings and nobody fetched him and nobody asked him a thing, and he is the only figure in this holding I have never once seen anybody else read."**

Harlan Vetch went past the ladder, past the low board and past the ring of bare ground on his way in and counted none of them, and he came back past the same three on his way out and counted none of them then either. At the back of the tap house he put his thumb on the weep at the joint and left it there long enough for two people at the trough to see him doing it, and then he looked at the standing pipe behind the joint, which is the same pipe it was standing behind the day the joint went in.

The man of about fifty went out at the ninth hour to the long walk along the hedge and came back at the eleventh. There is a door nine hundred yards off with a figure cut into the seventh column of its frame, and it went by his eye while he walked, and he did not stop for it and nobody in this yard put anything about it in front of him on the way past.

A round figure came and went on the wall of this yard this morning without one person in it deciding anything, and the two halves of that window were two figures all the same afternoon, and nothing about either of them was a season, a change or a sign.
