# Chapter 663

## The Sixth Of The Eighteenth, And Two Places That Will Not Put Themselves In Front

The pair was not read on the Sunday and the man of about fifty was the one who put it out of his own mouth before anybody had the book open, which is the first time in four years he has said a whole morning's business out loud before anybody else in this yard.

**"It is not read this morning and I am the one who is saying so,"** he said. **"A Sunday is the morning I go to my sister's at the fourth house down the track and I have gone every Sunday for as long as there has been a trough here to read at. I am not going to stop doing that in my sixty-first year because there are two figures to be said at seven."**

He put his coat on over his arm instead of on his back, which is what a man does when he is saying he is leaving.

Nobody asked him to stay and nobody gave him a reason.

Then the launder, which came down about half past eight on a Sunday with the middle road firmer than it has been all week, and the clerk was at the wall.

**One thousand and two hundred and fifty-three hundredweight.**

**"Eight on, and the four hundred and twenty-sixth of the run,"** he said. **"A Sunday is an even morning, so the rising half of the window took one overnight and the falling half stood. Rising is two hundred and two. Falling is a hundred and ninety-one. Three hundred and ninety-three of a window, and I am not adding those two in front of you because there has been enough sum in this yard since eight o'clock to last a fortnight."**

The window was three hundred and ninety-three on that Sunday morning. The rising half had gone up in the night to two hundred and two and the falling half stood where Saturday left it at a hundred and ninety-one, and they were two figures and stayed two figures.

A Sunday is not a morning the rotation comes round on, and the count in force did not move off the number it has been sitting on since Friday, a hundred and forty-four of them, twenty-nine gone out and a hundred and fifteen not gone out. No field left this holding and none was due out of it and nobody in this yard will be blamed for a field that stood and waited.

Then Hesta Lyle put a sheet of her own paper on the middle table that was not the long sheet, and it was folded in three, and she did not open it until there were nine people round the table.

**"This is not the office's sheet and it is not any of the three on the long table,"** she said. **"It is a piece of my own paper and it has four headings on it and under each heading I have written what it would cost to put a pipe on that place off the head of this column."**

She opened it at the first fold.

**"One pipe and the labour to lay it. One pipe and the labour to lay it. One pipe and the labour to lay it. And one pipe and the labour to lay it. That is the whole of what the column's money is for, this season, and I have not got it from an office. I have got it out of two men who both told me the same figure without either of them being asked to agree with the other."**

She turned the sheet a quarter turn so that the yard could see it.

**"So it is not a vote, because a vote would give us one place and a majority and the other three would go and be somebody else's problem by Thursday. It is one pipe. One of the four. And the place that is first pays the whole of it and gets a pipe, and the three that are not first have spent nothing and have nothing, and they cannot argue with anybody afterwards because the money is gone and it went on a pipe that somebody else is drinking from."**

She put her finger on the sheet and stayed there.

**"That is the thing that cannot be dodged. Somebody's name is going at the top of that page this month and the only question this morning is whether it gets there because a place volunteered or because a place ran out of ways to say no."**

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden put her two cans down on the step and came over to the table, which she does not do.

**"Then say the other thing out loud,"** she said. **"Say that the three of them are letting four houses go without water because they will not put a name on a page first."**

Hesta Lyle looked at whoever said that for long enough that the yard stopped.

**"No,"** she said. **"That is not what that is. Four houses with no water is a thing that happens on a road, on a day, with a ford in it, and I put the road and the ford in the right-hand column of this sheet three mornings ago. A person who will not go first is not the same as a person who does not care, and the second one is not on this column and is not going to be."**

Soren Rill had been at the far end of the table through all of it with his hands flat on the wood, and he took them off and put them in his pockets.

**"Then this holding will not be first either,"** he said. **"And I am going to say the reason before anybody puts a shape on it, because this yard has a way of turning a reason into a temper by lunchtime."**

He came round to the front of the table.

**"We are the place on this column with the launder and the standing and the only standing anybody up this road can check. If we put our name at the top of that page, then the other three have a pipe with nobody paying for it and a reason to stop trusting the column altogether. A network that spends itself on the first answer has no second answer left to give anybody, and you cannot spend the same pound on the second place by having spent it on the first."**

Soren Rill took his hands out of his pockets and laid them on the table on either side of Hesta Lyle's own sheet.

**"That is arithmetic and that is money,"** he said. **"I have not said anything else and I am not going to. And I am not going to stand in this yard and call three people I have never met cowards, because they have all got the same sum in front of them that I have got in front of me, and the sum does not care whose turn it is."**

Sera Quill said something short from the wall, and the yard heard all of it.

**"Say what it is,"** she said, **"in a sentence somebody in another county can put a finger on."**

He turned round to the wall to answer her, which cost him the table.

**"It is that this holding will not put its name first, because being first is the only way of being wrong that cannot be undone afterwards."**

Harlan Vetch went back up to the north end with that, and he has built a wall for four years on the strength of nothing worse.

A word came up from the third place in the middle of the afternoon, brought by a boy on a bicycle with nothing written on it. The third place had asked whether this holding was going first.

**"Tell them no,"** Soren Rill said, **"and tell them that no is not a yes later, because I am not saving it up to spend it when the other three have said no. If the three of them go first I will hold this yard where it is and I will not hold it against them afterwards. Tell them that as well."**

Hesta Lyle turned her own sheet face down and took up the long sheet instead, which is the only paper on that table anybody in this yard answers to, and she did not put her own sum away.

**"Four hundred and twenty-four at the head of it,"** she said. **"And under the line, in the small hand, the four hundred and twenty-third out of four hundred and twenty-fifth. One step under the number and one step over it, every morning of the four years I have stood at this table, and it will be that shape when I am not."**

Nobody in this yard was asked to choose anything this morning. There was no vote and no show of hands and no paper with anybody's name on it, and the refusal that was made was a refusal to go first and not a refusal of anything else, and it closed nothing, and it did not stop a pipe being laid somewhere before this month is out.

Marek read the three ages off the rack into the north-side room, where he had the chalk line and a tape and a straightedge laid out on the floor and was measuring the length of a wall that is going to have a cistern against it.

**"The charter is at two hundred and twenty-six days and I am going to write that on the line with the cistern on it, because a cistern is a thing this holding will have to answer for and the charter is the piece of paper it answers under,"** he said. **"The register form is at three hundred and twenty. The line in Silling's book is at three hundred and fourteen with nothing on it, and I am not writing anything on that one and I am not going to."**

He put the chalk down on the floorboards rather than on the wall.

**"And I am not saying either of the two sentences this yard is waiting for me to say this morning,"** he said. **"I refused a man in front of nine people twice before the light went and he has been right both times and one of the two refusals was mine. A man who has spent a morning being right in public does not get to also be the man who says the permanent thing, because then the refusal sounds like the preamble to it and it stops being a refusal and starts being a case."**

Harlan Vetch came back from the north end at about the third hour and said what he says on a Sunday, which is very little, and then he said one more thing.

**"There is an offer on the low board and it has had nothing written on it that would say when, from the day it went there,"** he said, **"and in four years I have not taken it down and I have not put it up, and I have not once asked anybody what it means, and I would like it left like that."**

He picked the hod up.

**"And the ladder has nobody on it. And the ring of bare ground inside the eleven acres has nobody's boot on it, nobody's tape on it and nobody's price on it. I have been past both of them twice today and neither of them asked me anything."**

He set the hod down outside the one room door and stood for a moment listening to the joint at the back of the tap house, which was weeping as quietly as it has wept every day since the day it went in, and he did not go and look at it, because it is a Sunday and because Harlan Vetch has never once gone and looked at it on a Sunday.

Nia Vale came out to the gate with a cloth and wiped the gate board before she let anybody write anything else on it anywhere, and found the line where she had left it.

**"Thirty-one,"** she said. **"Still thirty-one, and it will be thirty-one tomorrow because a thing that goes over once does not go over again the next day. And nothing has ever been let out of that line."**

The man of about fifty read the two boards on his way out through the gate at about the fourth hour, at the point where the wall narrows and both of them can be seen from one place.

This end of the wall says eight hundred and ten. Down at the tool house end it says eight hundred and fifty-seven, and between the two of them there is forty-seven of wall and no other difference between them whatever. The drawer behind this end is shut, as it has been since the wall went up, and the only key to it in this county is hanging inside the one room doorway where it hangs every hour of every day of the four years anybody here can remember.

Harlan Vetch counted the bare board along the top of the long table with the side of his hand at about the fifth hour and did not turn the second of the three papers over while he was doing it.

**"Nine inches at that end and nine at this one, and the first of them is a hundred and twenty-three days old,"** he said. **"The second is shut on its fold and I have not opened it and I am not going to. The third has a name and a hand and a seal and there is not one thing in it I could object to. Not one of the three entered, none refused, no column cut under any of them, and I am the man who builds four feet from that table and has never once been asked to read what is on it."**

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden went the length of the shelves at dusk going out of the yard rather than coming in, which is the reverse of the way she has done it before, and read off the end of her arm as she went.

**"Eleven journeys on the barrow, and a twelfth not marked. Fifteen lines in the use log and the sixteenth not written. Seven sessions in this holding's book, and a Sunday takes none. Fifty-three in the requests and fifty-three in the section nine notes, and this morning neither of them took Hesta Lyle's own paper."**

She stopped at the last shelf.

**"Thirty-nine blanks on the board in the one room, with the rule under them open and empty at the thirty-ninth time. Six ruled rows for what we do not know, and nothing cut above the sixth. A man of about seventy at twenty-nine fetchings, not fetched today, not asked anything. And the door nine hundred yards off went past at the eleventh hour and was not read and was not offered to anybody."**

Sunday went by in this yard with two people refusing to go first for reasons that were money and road and arithmetic, and the page that one of those reasons belongs on is still blank, and nobody in the yard was asked to put their name on it.
