# Chapter 658

## The First Of The Eighteenth, And A Yard That Turns Its Page Without Anybody Deciding Anything

Renn Ashby read the pair at the seventh hour with the book propped open on the middle table and his thumb where it always is, and the heading over it is of a month nine months back and did not move, because a month turning is not the same thing as that heading moving and this yard has never once pretended it was.

**"One hundred and eighty-one of three hundred and forty-five,"** he said. **"And I am going to say one thing about the heading, because it is the first and because a yard has never let me say this on any other morning of the year."**

He closed the book and left his thumb in it.

**"For four years I have had to say that whole line out, the words and the rest of it. From today I can say of this month and stop there, and the heading will go on being of a month nine months back, and those two things are not going to disagree with one another this morning or any morning after it. A heading does not know what day it is. That is what makes it worth having."**

The clerk had the day book open at the middle table with his coat still buttoned and a fresh ruled column ruled off down the right-hand side of the page, and he was copying the standing forward out of the last one by hand before the yard was properly up.

**"There is one morning a month where the work in this holding was chosen by this holding and it is this one,"** he said, **"and the work is copying. Twenty-one things go into the fresh column, one under the other, in the order a person can reach them, and I do them by hand because a copy a man can make without stopping has stopped telling anybody anything."**

He read the column down as he filled it, because that is how it has been done every month and because a yard can check a copy.

He came to the end of that and squared the sheet to the edge of the book before he carried on, which takes him about four seconds and which nobody in this yard has ever asked him to do.

**"Thirty paid on the compost line at the gate, and not one load has ever been let out in it. Eleven journeys marked against the barrow and a twelfth refused, because eleven is a floor and not a figure anybody here is trying to beat. Fifteen ruled lines in the use log, and the space above the fifteenth as bare as it was the day the log was started."**

He dipped the pen.

**"Seven sessions stand entered in this holding's book and a Tuesday takes none of them. The requests and the section nine notes are each sitting at fifty-three, and they are two separate books, and between the two of them they have taken nothing off that table and nothing out of anybody's coat."**

He ruled the line under that and came to the last item in the column.

**"Thirty-nine blanks on the board in the one room, and the second rule under them standing open and empty at the thirty-ninth time, and a rule that comes back empty opens nothing. And a man of about seventy stands at twenty-nine fetchings, and nothing was fetched from him this morning and nothing was put to him at any hour of it, and nobody in this yard has ever asked him which number."**

Somebody at the tap end asked him why he was not saying the two things this morning, and he put the pen down flat before he answered, which is not a thing he does for anybody in this yard.

**"Because I am copying,"** he said. **"I copy alone from the sixth hour, and a man with his hand on a fresh column for two hours has not got the hard sentence anywhere in him, and I am not going to stand up at ten o'clock and pretend otherwise. That is the reason. It is not a worse reason than the ones the rest of you have given this week and it is not a better one, and I would like it written down exactly as I have said it and not improved on."**

The launder came down the middle road about half past eight with a cold morning lying on the water, and the clerk was at the wall with the slate before it had finished arriving.

**One thousand and two hundred and thirty-nine hundredweight.**

**"Five off, and it is the four hundred and twenty-first of the run,"** he said, and put the chalk against the wall and left it there. **"A Tuesday is an odd morning. An odd morning takes five off that and takes the falling half of the window up by one, and the falling half is a hundred and eighty-nine and it moved in the night. The rising half is a hundred and ninety-nine and has stood where Monday left it. Three hundred and eighty-eight of a window."**

He wiped the chalk down his sleeve and looked at the two lines as if they were two lines and not two halves of one.

**"And I will say this once, in a yard, and then I will not say it again for a month. A fall is on an odd morning in this holding now. It was the other way round for four years and it turned over when the run before this one closed, and a man who carries the old order in here with him will have the two halves the wrong way round from the first odd morning onwards and will not find out for a month."**

The turn itself took the length of a cup of water and nobody in the yard was asked about it.

**"This is the first,"** the clerk said, and wrote the ordinal at the head of the fresh column. **"And that is the whole of what it does. The launder does not begin again. The window does not find a new place to start itself from. The run keeps the number it has. Neither board moves a step toward the other one. The three ages go on counting themselves and none of them counts this morning. The letter does not jump because a page of a calendar turned over. And the count in force does not go up, because today is not a morning the rotation comes round on."**

The number in force has not moved since Monday and it will not move today. It stands at a hundred and forty-four. Of that number twenty-nine have gone out at their own hour and a hundred and fifteen are standing at this hour and will go when they go. No field went out, none was due, and nobody in this yard is to be blamed for one that stood and waited, because the rota has that field waiting and the rota is not anybody.

Hesta Lyle had the long sheet squared in front of her and gave the small hand before she gave the figure above it, which is her habit and has been for nine years and which this yard can now recognise from the far end of the yard.

**"The figure on the long sheet this morning is four hundred and nineteen,"** she said, **"and the small hand underneath it reads the four hundred and eighteenth out of four hundred and twentieth. A step below the number and a step above it, and that is the only shape that hand has ever had and it will have it this evening as well."**

She put two fingers on the wood below the paper rather than on the figure on it.

**"Four bodies of households and four lines under the whole of them, and every one of the four is still short the two signatures it wants at the foot, and not one signature is on any of the four this morning, on the first of a month or on any other morning of this month. That is the one item on this table that does not get copied forward, and I would like it copied forward."**

Kellan Rusk came to the rack with the copy sheet squared beside him and took the three off the shelf in the order he can hold them, which is the register form first and the charter second and the line in Silling's own book last, and he gave that last one with his thumb against the edge of the rack.

**"The form is at three hundred and fifteen days,"** he said. **"The charter is at two hundred and twenty-one. The second ruled line in Silling's book is at three hundred and nine, and there is nothing written on it, and it is the only one of the three that has ever been empty and it will be the only one of the three that is empty this evening."**

He put the book back on its nail.

Soren Rill came in off the middle road with the barrow over his shoulder and gave the two boards on his way past without stopping at either of them, which is the way he has given them for four years.

Eight hundred and fifty-two at the far end, where the tool house is. Eight hundred and five at the near one. Forty-seven of walk between them, which is a distance and not a figure this holding adds to anything.

Behind the near board the masonry has a drawer let into it, and that drawer has not been opened once since the wall went up, and the only key to it in this county hangs inside the frame of the one room door on a nail that has one hole in it worn deep. It was on that nail when the yard opened this morning and it will be on it when the yard shuts, and there is no second key and there never has been.

Nia Vale came the length of the long table with a cloth over her shoulder and stopped at the first sheet and wiped around it rather than across it.

**"A hundred and eighteen days on the one lying open,"** she said, **"and about nine inches of bare board either side of it, and those are the same two nines they were on Saturday. The second of them has been folded on some old crease and has not been opened by anybody in this holding in the whole time it has been lying there. The last one is signed and dated and sealed and named, and anybody in four counties could read it at a glance and find nothing in it at all to complain about."**

She shifted the cloth to her other shoulder.

**"Entered, none of them. Refused, none of them. Ruled under, none of them. And there is no column in this holding for a paper that came out of an office, and a first of a month is not going to cut one."**

She turned round from the table and looked along the shelf behind her before she went.

**"Six ruled rows on that shelf against what this holding does not know, and the space above the sixth as clean as the day the shelf was made. There has never been a seventh cut for the schedule, and there is not going to be one cut for the office's day, or for the reach, or for a man with a form."**

At dusk the clerk counted three objects on his way out and did not count them out loud. The ladder stands against the tool house wall with nobody on it and nobody has gone up a rung. On the low board there is an offer with nothing written on it that would tell anybody when, and no hand has gone near it, and it has not been drawn down either. The ring of bare ground inside the eleven acres is bare, and being the first of a month did not put a foot on it any more than any other morning has.

Harlan Vetch put his thumb on the weep at the back of the tap house about the second hour and took it off again and said what he says about it.

**"It weeps and it holds,"** he said, **"and a Tuesday has now happened to that joint and proved nothing whatever about it. The standing pipe behind it is the same pipe it was standing behind on the day the joint went in, and that is the whole of what I know about it."**

He put his thumb back on the weep and left it there while he said the next part.

**"And a joint that weeps is a joint that is doing the work it was put in to do. The day it stops doing that work I want to be the first one told and not the second one, and I will not have anybody in this yard settling for me."**

Nobody in that yard said it had settled.

The man of about seventy stood in the yard at the ninth hour with his hands behind him and nobody put anything to him at any place he was seen at today.

Nine hundred yards off there is a door with a figure scratched into the seventh column of it. It went by at about the eleventh hour with the man of about fifty walking the long way back along the hedge, and nobody read it and nobody offered it to him or to anybody else, and that is what a figure that is walked past is.

By the time the light went this yard had washed out a trough, copied a column by hand and put a saucer under a weep, and the month had turned over in the night without once moving a figure this holding stands on.
