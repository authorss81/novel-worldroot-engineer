# Chapter 666

## The Ninth Of The Eighteenth, And A Room At The North Side That Has Begun Asking For Water

Renn Ashby read the pair at the seventh hour on the Wednesday with the book propped open on the middle table and his thumb where it has always been.

**"One hundred and eighty-five of three hundred and fifty-three,"** he said. **"Of this month, and I am saying of this month and not the frame, because the frame behind those figures is nine months back and it will be nine months back next week, and a Wednesday morning is not the morning I take a reader out to look at a date. That is the whole of what I have."**

He shut the book and put his weight on the back of his heel to get up, which he does at seven every morning of the four years and which nobody in this yard has ever once said anything about.

The cistern in the north-side room had taken its last course of brick before the light came. Harlan Vetch put the hod down in the doorway of that room, looked at the new work from the side, and then went down underneath it on one knee, which is what he does to a wall.

**"That is a cistern and not a repair,"** he said. **"A repair is a thing you do to something that was there. This is a thing I have made against a wall that was dry and was never wet, and I want it said in this room once, because the wall at the end of this yard where I have been four years is a different wall and a different job and I am not having the two confused just because they are both mine."**

Marek had the tape and the chalk out on the floorboards of that room and was not looking at the brickwork, because the brickwork was done and the arithmetic was not.

**"The room wants water at the second hour,"** he said. **"The trough at the end of it has been empty by the second hour on three weeks out of four and the hard weeks are now the ordinary weeks, so a cistern on this wall takes what the trough cannot hold and holds it until the fourth hour. That is the whole of what I am asking for and it costs a man and a day."**

Hesta Lyle came in with the long sheet against her chest and stopped in the doorway, which is not a thing she does.

**"Then say the second half of it,"** she said. **"Because this yard has been listening to the cost of a pipe since Sunday and I have heard one pipe's worth of labour."**

Marek put the chalk down on the boards and pointed at the wall with it.

**"A labour is a man and a day. This is a pipe off the head of the column, and the head is what four places on that sheet are all arguing over at the same time, and I have not written a labour for the pipe because I do not know what a pipe off that head costs this month."**

Hesta Lyle came the rest of the way into the room and did not unroll the sheet.

**"Then I am going to say the sum in this room and not at the table,"** she said. **"Four places on that column. One of them costs a pipe off that head. The second one cannot be reached at all this month and the second one has been the second one since Friday. Three of the four could take a pipe. None of the four could take two, and this holding has just asked for the second."**

**"Then do not put it on the sheet,"** Marek said.

**"I am not going to put it on the sheet,"** she said. **"That sheet has four places on it and it came to me as the column's sheet. The moment I write this holding's own room at the head of it, somebody in three counties reads a fifth place, and a fifth place on a column is a thing somebody has to answer for, and this yard has not asked to be anybody's answer for anything. I will write it on my own paper at home tonight and it will stay in my own house, and that is the whole of what I am going to do about it."**

The woman of about thirty-eight of Marden said it from the door with her two cans in her hands, which is how she holds them when she is about to put something down.

**"It goes up whether it is on a sheet or not,"** she said. **"Four years I have carried water past this gate every morning and nothing has ever gone up that column out of this yard except words and a form and a man in a good coat. This morning it is a room and a pipe. You cannot unsay it by writing it on your own paper at home."**

Nobody in that room wrote it on a sheet.

Hesta Lyle squared the long sheet on the middle table on her way out of the room and gave it its two sentences, because the figure at the head of that sheet has gone up by one every morning since before she started standing there.

**"Four hundred and twenty-seven is the figure at the head of it,"** she said, **"and under the line, in the small hand, it is the four hundred and twenty-sixth out of four hundred and twenty-eighth. A step below the number and a step above it, every morning of the nine years I have stood at that table, and it will be that shape again this evening."**

She put two fingers on the wood below the paper and not on the paper.

**"Four bodies of households and four lines under the whole of them, two signatures wanted at the foot of each, and not one signature is on any of the four this morning. That is the one line on this table that gets left where it lies instead of being written forward, and I would like it left there."**

Tova Reed came in with the first stack of trays under one arm and set them down along the south wall in two rows on the floor, and went back for the next stack, and nobody in that room offered to carry one for her and she did not look up to see whether anybody had.

**"The bracket is in the way,"** she said. **"It comes out of the wall a foot above the floor and the cistern is a foot and a half at the top of that course, so the shelf cannot go back where it was. And I am not having seed stood against a wall with water standing against the other side of it."**

Marek said he could carry the rest of them across.

**"You can carry the rest of them across,"** she said, **"after the pipe is decided. If that pipe comes off that head in the spring there will be a lead running down this wall and a stopcock on this side of the room, and I am not moving a bench twice in one season for a wall I have not seen the pipe in yet."**

She set the last two trays at the end of the row and squared them with the side of her hand.

**"The floor is colder than the shelf. That is better for the seed and worse for my knees and I have decided to take the knees."**

One row of bench along the north wall was left bare, and she did not say what was going into it, and nobody in that room asked her.

The water in that room came down the middle road at about half past eight, and the woman of about thirty-eight of Marden read it off the mark on the trough side, because she was at the far end with her cans and the wall with the figures on it was behind her.

**"One thousand and two hundred and fifty-one hundredweight,"** she said. **"That is five off, and it is the four hundred and twenty-ninth of the run."**

**"Say the halves the way they went,"** the clerk called across the yard. **"That is a Wednesday."**

**"The half that fell in the night is a hundred and ninety-three,"** she said. **"The other one stood where it stood yesterday and it is two hundred and three, and the whole of the window is three hundred and ninety-six. I have said the moved one first because I am tired of this yard deciding which half of a thing is the important one."**

Harlan Vetch read the two boards on his way down to the tap house and said them without stopping, which is his habit and has been since the wall went up.

**"This end of the wall is at eight hundred and thirteen and the end behind the tool house is at eight hundred and sixty, and there are forty-seven of wall between them and forty-seven of nothing else. In four years I have watched two men in this yard try to make those two numbers mean something about each other and they are the two ends of a wall."**

Let into the stone behind the first of those two figures there is a drawer, and it is shut, and its key is on a single nail inside the doorway of the one room, and it has been on that nail at every hour of this day and will be on it at dusk.

**"And the joint at the back of the tap house weeps where it has always wept and holds where it has always held, and the standing pipe behind it is the same pipe it was on the day it went in,"** he said. **"I have chalked it four times this year and I am not going to chalk it a fifth, and if anybody in this yard ever says that joint has settled after a week then I will take the chalk off that wall myself and I would rather say that at noon than be asked about it at dusk."**

Kellan Rusk came to the rack from the bottom up, which is not the order he takes it in and which he has never explained to anybody.

**"The charter is at two hundred and twenty-nine days. The register form is at three hundred and twenty-three days. And the second ruled line in Silling's own book is at three hundred and seventeen with an empty space behind it that has been empty since before I came to this yard and that I have stopped inventing an answer for."**

He put all three back the way he had taken them out.

**"That is the bottom-up order and I have given nine days of figures top-down. A man can read a shelf either way and the shelf does not care, and I would like it noticed that I have noticed it."**

The clerk had the copy sheet squared in front of him with a wet pencil and read the column out in the order he wrote it, which is the only order he has ever read it in.

**"Eleven journeys on the barrow and no twelfth. The use log is at fifteen lines and the space above the fifteenth is as bare as the day it was started. This holding's book is at seven sessions and a Wednesday puts none in. The board in the one room carries thirty-nine blanks, and the rule underneath them has come back open and empty for the thirty-ninth time. An empty rule opens nothing. On the shelf are six ruled rows against what we do not know, nothing cut above the sixth, and no seventh for a cistern, a pipe or a man with a form. The two other books are each at fifty-three and neither took a cistern off a table."**

He ruled the line under the last item and then stopped, because the gate board had a figure on it that had not been on it on Monday, and he wrote it into the column like anything else.

**"Paid at thirty-one,"** he said, **"and not one load ever let out of it, in thirty-one or in thirty. That is the second morning I have copied that figure off that board and I am copying it the way I copy everything else. I am not going to say a word about it, and I would like the yard to notice that a line which changed on Thursday got copied on Thursday and has been copied again this morning without one person in this yard deciding anything about it."**

He wrote two more items under the last one before he put the pencil down.

**"And there is a man of about seventy in this yard whose figure here is twenty-nine fetchings. He was not fetched this week and he was not put to by one person or the other, and that is the arrangement and not an oversight. It is the last item on my sheet and the only one I have never had to check twice."**

Sera Quill turned the slate face up at the end of the long table.

**"Ninety-fourth,"** she said, **"and eighty-first. Thirteen apart, and that gap has not closed on one morning out of four years."**

She put her thumb on the third line under the two figures and left it there.

**"Underneath those two there is a third line, and the third line says that the second of the two is carried forward and that no printed rule in this holding moves it, and that sentence is worth more than the two figures above it and it does not need me to say it out loud a sixth time in a yard. Thirteen is the honest part of this slate and it has been the honest part since the day I had it."**

Nia Vale came along the long table with her sleeve and wiped around the first of the three papers rather than across it.

**"That one is a hundred and twenty-six days old and there are about nine inches of bare board either side of it, and both of those nines are where they were on Monday. The second is shut on its fold and has not been opened by anybody. The third has a name and a hand and a seal on it and there is not one thing in it that anybody in this yard could call wrong. None of the three entered, none refused, no column cut under one."**

She straightened up and looked over at the tool house wall without going near it.

**"And the third column on that sheet of terms is ruled and empty under a heading nobody has improved in four years, and I checked it this morning because I was wiping under it. Two words higher up that same wall have four ruled lines under them, and not one of the four has ever had anything written on it. I am not going to ask anybody what belongs in them and I would rather they stayed the way they are."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the north end at about the eleventh hour and put the hod outside the door of the one room before he said anything.

**"There are two sentences this yard has on it and I am not saying either of them this morning,"** he said. **"I have been asked eleven times since the sixth hour whether that cistern is finished. Not by you. By everybody, by people who wanted an answer and by people who wanted to be able to say afterwards that they had asked. A man who has been asked eleven times whether a thing is done does not walk back into a yard at noon and say the most permanent sentence in this holding. From that hour on, this yard would treat every man in it who has anything half made as though he had just said a thing about forever."**

He turned round and looked back at the wall he had been on.

**"The cistern is not finished. There is a joint to come and I have not done it and it will not be done this evening. That is the state of it."**

The number in force is where Tuesday left it and it stays there today, because the rotation does not come round on a Wednesday. A hundred and forty-six of them, with twenty-nine gone already and a hundred and seventeen still on the books at this hour, waiting for a turn that has not arrived yet. Not one field went out of this holding today and not one field was due out of it, and there is nobody in this yard who will be blamed for a field that stood still.

Soren Rill walked the width of the yard near the end of the afternoon to see whether the gate had been shut and came back along the inside of the eleven acres without going near the ring.

**"The ladder has gone up no rung and there is no figure in this holding standing against either of the two people it belongs to. The offer on the low board has nothing written on it that would say when and nobody's hand on it, and it has been that shape since before there was a table to put it against. The ring of bare ground inside those acres took no foot today and no tape and no price, and I walked the inside edge and did not put a heel over it once."**

He turned the gate to see whether the latch had dropped and came back to where he had been standing.

**"And there is a door nine hundred yards off this yard that went past at the eleventh hour. Nobody read it. Nobody carried it to anybody. A figure a man walks past is not a figure the man has read, and nobody here thinks otherwise. And the woman from the second place is standing in a room at that place this afternoon with nine people in it, and this yard does not know how that room goes and is not going to be told by Friday."**

At dusk the cistern stood against the north wall of the north-side room with a bare row of bench along the bottom of it and two rows of trays on the floor at the other side of the room, and the trough at the end of that room stood at a height it had not stood at since the wall went up, and the pipe that fills the cistern comes off a head that three other places on a sheet in another county are arguing about as well, and nobody in that yard was asked a single question about any part of it.
