# Chapter 665

## The Eighth Of The Eighteenth, And A Man With A Form And No Standing In This Yard

The pair was not read on the Tuesday and the clerk said so himself, at the rack, with the yard half full of a stranger.

**"It is not read this morning and I am the one saying so,"** he said. **"There is a man in this yard with a case and a form and he has wanted two numbers out of me since before the yard was properly up, and the last thing this holding needs at seven in the morning is a third person asking me for figures. One pair a week is what I can stand and I have stood one for four years."**

The stranger was about forty-five and he had a leather case and a good coat and he had put the case down on the long table in the right way, square to the edge, before he said anything at all. He was not from this holding and nobody had sent for him.

**"I am not asking this holding for anything today,"** Ansell Dree said. **"There is no form in my case for this and I have not come with one to be filled in. What I have come with is a question, and the question is whether this holding's standing on the regional column can be entered against a guarantee. That is all of it. It can be yes or it can be no and there is no third shape on the form."**

**"No,"** Kellan Rusk said.

Ansell Dree took his pen off the form.

**"You have not asked what the answer costs."**

Ansell Dree put his pen down on the form, which a man with a case does not usually do.

**"It does not cost anything in this yard,"** Kellan Rusk said, and put his thumb on the shelf and then took it off again. **"You have brought me a piece of paper with a box on it, and the box has a line in it for a number, and this holding's number is not yours to enter anywhere. If you want the column number I will give you the column number, because a column number is a fact about a column and not about us. It is the fourth on the second sheet. Write that down if you want it."**

Ansell Dree wrote the column number down on the corner of his own form and did not put anything in the box.

**"And if I came back with a figure against it,"** he said.

Harlan Vetch came through the yard with the hod on his shoulder and did not stop, and Ansell Dree waited for him to get past before he went on.

**"Then I would fill that box in with a figure, and it would be my figure and not yours,"** Kellan Rusk said, **"and you would take it out of this yard to a man who does not live in this yard, and he would decide what it was worth. That is not a sale and it is not a pledge and it is not a thing this holding does. I have said no to a person with a form in this yard twice this week and I am not going to be difficult about it a third time."**

Hesta Lyle had the long sheet squared in front of her the whole time the man was talking, and she did not look up until he had been answered, and then she gave the sheet its own two sentences before she gave him hers.

**"Four hundred and twenty-six is the figure at the head of it,"** she said, **"and there are two figures under that figure, written smaller, one either side of it. The lower of the two is the four hundred and twenty-fifth and the higher is the four hundred and twenty-seventh, and that hand has been making that shape for four years and I did not teach it and I cannot improve it."**

She put two fingers on the wood beside the paper and not on the paper.

**"It is not for sale and it is not dear,"** she said, **"and those are two different sentences. I have only got the first one. The first one is not an insult."**

Then the rotation, which came round on the Tuesday the way it comes round on every morning of this kind, and Kellan Rusk gave it because he keeps the second book and does not need the first one explained to him.

**"A hundred and forty-six in force,"** he said. **"Twenty-nine gone out at their own hours. A hundred and seventeen not gone out, and that half is above a hundred and has been above a hundred all season."**

He shut the book.

**"Seven mornings of this kind and it has gone up by one each time and never by two, and the next one will do the same. Nothing went out of this holding today that was due to go out of it, and there is nobody here to blame for a field that stood where the book had it standing."**

He looked at the stranger's case while he said the next part.

**"I am saying that to a man who has come to put a price on a standing, because I would like him to hear that this holding does not run on anybody being hard."**

Sera Quill turned the slate face up on the end of the long table and read both figures off it without looking down.

**"Ninety-fourth,"** she said, **"and eighty-first. Thirteen apart, and that gap has never once closed on any morning I have had this slate in my hands."**

She put her finger on the third line under the two figures and left it there, and a stranger in a good coat read the finger and not the numbers.

**"That second one is carried forward. It comes up the slate every morning and no printed rule in this holding ever shifts it, and I have said that out loud in a yard six times and I am not going to stop saying it."**

Sera Quill lifted her hand off the slate and held it up flat, which is not a thing she does.

**"And I am not going to say either of the two sentences this yard has on it this morning,"** she said, **"because this is a morning when nine people in here are being asked to say numbers to a stranger, and the moment a yard gets used as a place where figures are given out on request, anything said in it gets counted as part of the answer. I have seen a room work that way in a county office and it does not stop with the figures."**

Ansell Dree looked at the slate, then at her, then wrote something on his own paper that was not a figure.

**"For whatever it is worth,"** he said, **"I was not going to write that one down either."**

**"Then we agree about one morning,"** Sera Quill said, and turned the slate face down.

The man of the north row with the cough was at the end of the long table and had heard the whole of it.

**"I will say the thing I have come to say and then I am going back to my end of the yard,"** he said. **"I am not going to use this yard's habit against it. Every morning of the four years this holding has said something out loud that it did not have to say, and I have been the man at the end of it listening, and it has been a thing that was given out. The day I stand in a yard and get a hard sentence said out loud on my account, then everything this holding says out loud is about me, and that is the day I stop coming to the gate at seven."**

He went back to the north row and sat down.

The launder was going down the middle road at half past eight while a stranger was still in the yard, and the clerk was at the wall with the slate, and Ansell Dree came and stood beside him to watch it arrive, which nobody in this holding had ever done.

**One thousand and two hundred and fifty-six hundredweight.**

**"Eight on, and the four hundred and twenty-eighth of the run,"** the clerk said. **"A Tuesday is an even morning, so the rising half of that window took one overnight and the other one stood. The rising half is two hundred and three. The other half is a hundred and ninety-two. The two of them make three hundred and ninety-five."**

The stranger got his pen out and wrote it down.

**"Not on that form,"** the clerk said. **"That is three counties of people who would then believe this holding stands behind a figure."**

**"It is my own paper and my own hand and it is going into a case,"** Ansell Dree said, **"and I will show it to nobody who comes to this gate with a question about water."**

**"Then write it,"** the clerk said.

The window was three hundred and ninety-five that Tuesday, the rising half up at two hundred and three in the night, the other half standing at a hundred and ninety-two, and the two were not added together in front of anybody.

Ansell Dree asked which of the two boards was the near one and which was the far one, and the clerk told him, and he copied both onto his own form in his own hand.

Eight hundred and twelve at the end he started from. Eight hundred and fifty-nine at the other one. Let into the stone behind the first of those two there is a drawer, and it is shut, and its key is on a nail inside the doorway of the one room. He wrote down the difference between the two figures, which is forty-seven, and then he wrote the word distance beside it.

**"That is the first thing anybody has written on a piece of paper in this yard in four years that is not a figure of ours,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"You have written a distance and called it a distance. If you had written forty-seven into a box with a line in it for a standing I would have had to tear the form."**

Ansell Dree asked about the three papers on the long table, and whether any of them could be entered against anything, and while he was asking he looked at the first of them for a while.

Nia Vale came along the table with her sleeve and wiped around that first sheet rather than across it, which she does and does not explain to anybody.

**"That one has been lying there a hundred and twenty-five days,"** she said, **"and there are about nine inches of bare board either side of it, and those two nines have not shifted since Saturday. The second is shut on its fold. The third is signed and dated and sealed and named. None of the three entered, none refused, no column cut under one."**

She put the cloth over her shoulder.

**"And if you write any of that down it will be the first time anybody outside this holding has held it in a hand, and I would rather it were a figure on a wall than a line in a case."**

**"No,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"None of them. This holding has never cut a column under a paper that came out of an office, and there is no column here for what a signature agrees to either, and if you write a name on that form and put it in the box you will have made a column where there is not one."**

**"Then I will write none,"** Ansell Dree said, and wrote the word in the margin of his own form, and underlined it, and showed it across the table to Kellan Rusk before he shut the case.

Kellan Rusk read the slate he had written four minutes earlier off the middle table where it lay face up. off the shelf and wrote them on a slate in chalk because the yard was too busy for anybody to stand at the rack, and he set the slate on the middle table face up.

**"The register form stands at three hundred and twenty-two days. The charter is at two hundred and twenty-eight. The line in Silling's own book has three hundred and sixteen at the foot of it and an empty space above."**

He left the slate lying there and went back to the rack.

The slate stayed on the middle table with three figures on it and nobody in the yard looking at them, which has not happened in this holding before.

**"That is a figure of mine sitting on a table for an hour with nobody asking to hear it again,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"It is the first time in four years and I would like it noticed."**

Marek went out through the gate about the ninth hour and put his thumb into the chalk of the compost line at the gate post on his way past, and did not say anything about it, and did not write anything next to it, and came back in through the same gate about ten minutes later.

**"Thirty-one,"** Nia Vale said, from the trough, when somebody asked her what he had stopped for on his way out.

She did not put her cloth down.

**"Paid at thirty-one and nothing has ever been let out of it,"** she said, and then, **"He did not say anything about it. He put a thumb in it and he took his thumb out. That is not a conversation and I am not going to make it into one. If he wants to talk to me about that line he can come to the bench like anybody else."**

Ansell Dree had five items off the top of his own head that he wanted to write onto the top of his form, and he got as far as the fourth before Kellan Rusk put his hand flat on the paper.

**"No,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"Not those. Eleven journeys on the barrow, fifteen lines in the use log, seven sessions in this holding's book, fifty-three and fifty-three in the two other books, thirty-nine blanks on the board in the one room with the rule under them open at the thirty-ninth time, six ruled rows against what we do not know with nothing cut above them, and a man of about seventy at twenty-nine fetchings who has not been fetched and has not been asked anything."**

**"That is nine, not four,"** Ansell Dree said, and looked at his own form to make sure he had counted right.

Kellan Rusk put his other hand flat over the four lines the stranger had already written.

**"Then it is nine and the four you had are inside the nine, and not one of the nine goes onto a form of yours,"** he said, and pushed the form back across the table, **"because the moment those are on a piece of paper in a case they stop being the standing of a holding and they become assets, and I am not the man who converts one into the other before lunch."**

Ansell Dree asked, on his way out through the gate, what the third column of the sheet of terms on the tool house wall was for.

**"Ruled,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"Empty. There is a heading over it and nobody in this holding has improved on that heading in four years and I am not going to start today because a man with a case asked me what it was for."**

He nodded at the wall without touching it.

**"And under two words on the same wall there are four ruled lines, and every one of them is bare. That is somebody else's condition and it is not mine, and nobody in this yard has been asked what goes in them and nobody is going to be asked today."**

He shut the rack.

Ansell Dree asked whether the weeping joint at the back of the tap house was a fault in this holding's standing.

**"A standing pipe is not a column,"** Kellan Rusk said. **"It weeps and it holds and the pipe behind it is the same pipe. If you write that down as a fault in a standing then somebody will come and mend it and cost this holding money for a thing that is not broken, and that is a mistake a man makes once."**

Harlan Vetch came down off the north end at about the eleventh hour and looked at the stranger's case and said nothing about it, and went out to the far end of the yard with the hod.

The ladder is against the tool house wall and no rung of it has gone, and no figure in this holding stands against either of the two people it belongs to, and nothing this morning put one there.

Down at the low board there is still an offer, undated, untaken, undrawn, and it has been that since before this yard was a yard. Inside the eleven acres the ring of bare ground has not been walked on, has not been measured, and has not been priced, and a man with a form in a case walked within ten feet of it on his way out.

Ansell Dree went out through the gate at about the eleventh hour and walked the long way along the hedge, and nine hundred yards off there is a door with a figure cut into the seventh column of its frame, and it went past his eye while he walked, and he did not stop for it, and nobody in this yard offered it to him or mentioned it after he had gone.

On this morning a man carried a piece of paper and a good coat into this yard and asked a question at the long table, and the bookkeeper gave him his answer in one line with his hand resting on a shelf. The reason behind that answer was worked out in somebody else's kitchen, nine miles off and four hundred yards up the lane, and this yard never saw the working out of it.
