# Chapter Summaries

## Volume 01 — The White Furrow

**1. Iron in the Water** (day 1, well head, ~3,400 words)
The pump test comes in seven short (41/48, static lift, collar shut) and the machine is sound. The outer rows beyond the yard have gone over sideways, lying along the row, white at the top, uncut. Marek opens the well head with Ilsa Kern, gets an arm to the elbow, and finds the wound held not by a seal but by a **compound pad** — a base coat four seasons old with a thinner dressing laid on it every year since, never replaced, all of it sitting on the scarwood by weight, with warm iron-smelling water coming up through the middle of it at the six o'clock position. Kellan's log records a proper seal. The warmth is written into the notebook as a guess, because he has no instrument and cannot defend a difference found with his palm. Marek also notes the pad beating at about one beat in four seconds, out of step with the pump. **Then he rebuilds the draw by hand**: the foot valve and gland are fouled with a season of iron off the wound, a Crown mechanic would be thirty shillings a day from Fennmere, and the cooperative has no such money. He clears it in his wet coat, runs the pit with the collar off, and fills the dry yard trough; four men drink from it and the water tastes of iron. The counter stays at 41/48 and he says why out loud: forty-eight was nine feet into good gravel. **That is the pride beat and the dread in one object.** Nia brings the season's board and tells him the third of the three things a charter can do is the one nobody in the yard says out loud, and will not say it either. Clerk Perrin Osse takes the walk-through, explains that a charter condition is a thing to be *shown* and not repaired, and records seventeen days. Marek announces, in front of the crew, that the fault is in the root line and he will prove it in front of an inspector rather than in a shed. Ends with the wrapped fieldbook's needle refusing to settle while the head is open. **Change:** the fault is located, the log is exposed, and the pump runs. **Cost:** a public promise, and the discovery that a perfect pump fixes the part of the problem that can be fixed with tools.

**2. The Loaned Steward** (day 1, seed house yard into evening, ~3,300 words)
Tova Reed of the Dryfoot Trust arrives at midday with eleven crates of the Longneck rye, the only dry-land line the Salt March has left. She has read the cooperative's filed numbers before she met anyone. In the yard, in front of the members, she makes Marek do the sum: the log claims 1,100 turns/day delivered to Lantern Reach ground, the ground can take 720, and 832,000 turns over six years went somewhere the cooperative does not own. She names four conditions: a measured draw she may witness and file alongside his; published numbers open to any member, stranger, or clerk; her own veto on any use of the seed vault; and a copy of everything to Cray whether or not it helps. She will look at the cooperative's records for two days and say nothing until they are out. She notices the mill-keeper's hand tally board and asks nothing about it. **Change:** a hostile, precise reader is inside the books. **Cost:** the fiction that the paperwork is clean.

**3. The Coupling** (day 3 afternoon, the closing hours of Tova's two-day look, ~4,200 words)
Marek's loading test on the seed house return line. Tova cites a witnessed, minuted condition in the seed house book; Marek denies the entry is there, and the prose marks the denial as deliberate, because it is — the entry is in the book and he will not concede it. She refuses; Ilsa refuses without understanding it. He starts it anyway. Cold iron water comes up through the vault's rootwood bed; three trays of the Longneck go translucent. Tova kills the draw and shears the coupling with his own spanner, then tells him to shut the rig down properly, which he does himself. **No rootwork occurs and none is available**: he wants the wood to say something and it is wood, wet and cold, doing nothing, patiently, for as long as he keeps asking. The **dial and the stroke tally are kept as two columns** — pressure and volume — and the result entered is *one hundred and thirty-four turns of the handle, needle at three-eighths under the mark*, a rate and not a day. Two records come out of it that agree without knowing about each other — his test says the return will not pass what the log claims, Tova's tape says the return is 22 rods of 8 inch against a specified 41 rods of 12 inch — and the missing margin is compost. Tova rules three trays spoiled and does not soften it. **The beat is shame, not relief**: he was right about the number and reckless about the cost, and both are true at once; and he discovers that respecting a refusal is not the same as agreeing with it, and had known that for eleven seconds and gone on anyway. Kellan's crew applies the last compound top-up to the pad that evening; Renn Tallow says, *you cannot close a thing with tar.* **Change:** Kellan's number is false for a physical reason, not a clerical one. **Cost:** the coupling is destroyed and unreplaceable before the eighteenth, part of a family's seed is gone, and two days of the season are gone and cannot be got back.

**4. The Vote and the Ledger** (day 3 evening, seed house, ~4,100 words)
Tova states in advance what the March will and will not lend and **withdraws nothing**: the vault veto stands, the cooperative broke it this afternoon, and the record is to show a written condition agreed and then broken. Marek owns the spoiled trays in the first thirty seconds. Dunmar Fitch will not enter a true cause without a licensed hand behind it, and when a member from the north row says *put the wrong number in the book and let the man in Fennmere end it*, the room agrees and Dunmar refuses. **The claim in the log is moved and lost, 9 for and 22 against with 4 abstentions, out of thirty-five hands in a room of forty.** Bram Oskell arrives in person with Kellan's offer: a free gasket and a proper seal in exchange for a two-year exclusive extension **and** the cooperative's confirmation of six years of maintenance. Tova explains that the offer buys a signature, not a seal, and refers to a paper on the shape of these offers that she refuses to describe in the room — **no number, no date, no place**, so that nobody in the room can start behaving like the people in it. Lissa refuses it in front of the room, then insists it be entered as a motion rather than left as a woman's speech; **Marek moves it himself, in his own name, and it carries 31–4**, with the second page annexed and unsigned. The trays motion is carried 34–0. He then orders the cooperative's own draw figures, log, work book and loading test opened to anyone who asks, with his own error on the same page in the same hand, as a standing act of the meeting and not a motion — so the open book is a private act and not a filing. Oskell mentions, in passing, that Sera Quill is on the fen road on the sixth day. **Change:** leverage traded for exposure, and the truth is kept out of the book by a vote. **Cost:** the open book, which is not a rule but a smaller and more dangerous thing, and a chamber that has just chosen the charter over the record.

**5. The Woman With the Old Book** (day 6, fen road, morning, ~2,800 words)
Sera Quill is walking to a one-day job at the salt cistern and will not stop. Marek offers a written scope instead of a favor. She names her terms: his raw notes, not copies; he does not touch the instrument unsupervised; she signs only what she has seen, with her license and her name behind it. Fee: fourteen pounds, paid personally, second visit another fourteen. She tells him one thing for free: a branch on this fen was cut on Crown money eight years ago while Aldren was in the ground, and she has spent six years failing to find out by whom. She has papers she will not show him. She has not asked for the fieldbook and tells him she will ask, in front of witnesses, when she asks. **Change:** a licensed witness with a fee, a scope, and her own agenda. **Cost:** fourteen pounds he will not get back, and the dread of being asked about the kit.

**6. Measured Water** (days 7–8, well head, the batch midpoint, ~4,800 words)
Two days of quarter-hourly logging. Sera brings two hundredweight of compost for the return and insists on it, because logging a wound without feeding it makes the wound worse. **It is £40 at the 4 s/cwt rate, which the cooperative cannot pay and which she pays out of her own fee money and enters in the open book under her own name** — consistent with her Ch 5 rule that a document with a document in it is a document with an opinion in it. The kit is unwrapped in front of the crew. The copper contact clip is the path; the fieldbook bead and the well's conduit are the anchor; the compost is the return, and she makes him say all three aloud. The brass gauge and the fieldbook's needle disagree, and the margin widens each hour; she forbids him to reconcile them. On the second afternoon the pressure comes up in events of two, three, four and five beats about four seconds apart, she is sent for a second dial, Gael goes to the sump, and in four seconds with nobody in the pit Marek gets a work sequence — valve, hand, wait, valve, lamp, valve — **with nothing looking back and nobody in it saying anything**; the well's own older rhythm runs under it, and *inside* it, between the second thing and the third, is a shaft: a lamp on a chain and his father's hands, older, with a torn cuff, on a wheel. The fieldbook's last page carries the line of figures, the column head and figure *confirmed — set — 4.*, and a year eight months after the disappearance. **No serial number is on that page.** He ends the session himself, writes three findings, records his own reading as UNVERIFIED before she can ask, states the impossible date is probably a copying error and must be treated as one, and hands her the raw notes instead of keeping them. Costs: nosebleed, disorientation, and a **memory bleed** — the term she gives him. No mark on his skin; she says it will not stay that way. **Change:** the fault is proven to be upstream, and the reading cannot be proved. **Cost:** the first private instrument, surrendered, and £40 of compost nobody in the cooperative can replace.

**7. Who Cuts a Branch** (day 9, Weir Row, all day, ~4,600 words)
Marek and Gael walk onto ground that the charter's fourth schedule puts under a maintenance order — a trespass against the cooperative's own charter. A Crown sign on the fifth post reads *BRANCH MAINTENANCE — DISTRICT SCHEDULE. NO LOCAL HOLDER. REPORT CUTS TO THE SUB-OFFICE, FENNMERE.* Cart ruts sit eight feet back on the soft berm. The branch was cut, healed, and cut again, with two die-struck serials: **N-2214/K** on the old healed face and **N-3318/K** on the fresh one. Marek dates it as a range, writes *both* possible dates side by side, draws it to scale with a scale bar, and has Gael Senn sign as a second witness. He finds a marker ninety yards away — **Ilsa Kern's** grandmother's, HANSA KERN, 1802, with a cup dish worn into the top and a later line about the short road to the weir — and does not tell Gael until he has to. Ilsa arrives at two, makes him put her grandmother's road in the margin in his own hand, and tells him to say her name to the inspector not because it helps him. Renn Tallow waits at the road fork with an offer: a full parts kit for the season for the notebook page. Marek refuses aloud, in front of Gael, because he wants it on paper. A boy rides in at dusk: the annual inspection has been brought forward to tomorrow. **Change:** the enemy is a schedule, not a man, and the ground is somebody else's. **Cost:** an enemy who now knows what he is looking for, and one day left.

**8. The Inspection Came Early** (day 10, batch climax, first half, ~4,900 words)
Inspector Ansel Bray arrives with Perrin Osse and the emergency-seizure clause: seal the head, take the seed stock, or refer for relocation. **In the open pit he times the pulse twice, does not feel it, and decides not to write it down** — he will not put an officer's pen to a thing one man felt and a district inspector did not, and the discretion is his and is recorded in the prose, so the clue is protected by a decision rather than by an oversight. He reads the open book for an hour and puts the district's own filing beside the cooperative's work book page. The pad, the trespass, and Marek's own signatures make this look like cooperative negligence — until the schedule extract. Bray identifies **N-2214/K** as a maintenance-schedule number, one item executed twice on a K three-year cycle, and a half-sheet of a table older than the cooperative's charter. The cut is not unregistered; it is on a schedule no local holder was told about, and notification is not in the schedule as drafted. Marek unwraps the fieldbook in front of the clerk; **Sera Quill puts her nine raw pages of quarter-hourly figures on the table first**, squared to the edge, with the page of four strokes past the refusal on top, and states that she took every figure from the operator by dictation and never opened the book — so the document he reads aloud is in the room. He reads Finding 3 aloud, states in the same breath that it is his own unreliable reading and cannot be proof of anything, and asks Bray to enter it as an unverified observation. Bray picks up the fieldbook, reads the last page and the impossible year twice, sets it down like something that has turned out not to be his, then takes **Marek's weir notebook** and reads N-2214 and N-3318 off the to-scale drawing where they were actually copied out. **Sera asks for the page, reads the two numbers twice, says she has seen the shape of that table, refuses to say where she saw it or what it is, and asks for both refusals to be entered with her name** — the payoff of Ch 5. She still names no branch and no office. Bray enters the serials as fact and not as inference, enters the finding, declines to seize, and moves the decision to the eighteenth — the charter's own expiry, so nothing is solved. Kellan arrives at noon, takes his hat off for the first time in six years, and says his crew pulls the compound kit out of the fen tonight. The glass has dropped two tenths. **Change:** the instrument is on a Directorate record and can be seized, and a licensed engineer has been drawn past her own six-year silence. **Cost:** eight days he already had, plus the only thing he owns that has ever told him the truth.

**9. The Thing at the Weir** (day 10 night, storm, batch climax, second half, ~5,300 words)
A storm comes in off the west. Gael Senn goes up the fen road with a hooked pole to save the north lamp; Ilsa sandbags the pit. **Ilsa reports the round trip in full**: the satchel to the third gate, the north post gone over with the bulb still in it, the lamp moved onto the wire and lit, a coat and most of a can of oil gone — and then the way back is shut, the drain ditch and the causeway both up, so Gael is on the far side of the race with the weir steps and the maintenance plank for ground. The pasture flock — 130 head, ninety ewes and thirty-six lambs and four rams — comes down the embankment crown with a drover's dog and something walking beside them, keeping them off a line on the ground. **Lissa Vale names it: a pathhound.** It herds, it does not hunt; it cannot be called off or tied up, and killing the thing it stands on does not get rid of it — she has known two men try that and both were still herding in the spring. The only thing that moves it is a change in where the water goes, and she has not said the word in this yard in thirty years. The animal stops 130 head dead on a twelve-foot crown. Gael is pinned on the maintenance plank over the race. Renn Tallow passes with the compound kit and does not stop, and says *I have got the load.* With no supervisor, no license, and no time, Marek puts his bare hands on the scarroot's cut face and gets **four seconds that are wrong** — the flow reads as going away instead of coming up. Damaged wood lies. He then does the actual work with a crowbar and his own graft shears, levers the healed seam open, and vents the dead branch's stored pressure into the old drainage channel. The animal loses the route it can feel, breaks off, and leaves on its own, going to look for the water. It is not commanded. Gael crosses whole. Marek collapses, is sick, cannot account for the last hour of the walk, and finds his own handprint on a stone he does not remember touching. He knows the name of what happened to him now: **memory bleed** — the same four seconds as the first and dearer than the first, because the length is not the price. The seam will not close and the branch bleeds from now on. No animal comes back. The storm drops nine tenths, the pit floods, the pad lets go all at once, and **four sacks of fiber and fortyweight of compound** are on a cart up the fen road. **Ilsa then says the second thing, at dawn, in the pit:** the flock has been the seed house's compost return for eleven years and there is nothing else to put in the bed. **Change:** the flock and the seed vault's compost return are gone, and the reason is now on the page in the climax chapter instead of surfacing in the aftermath. **Cost:** animals, a compost heap, a branch that will bleed for years, and the strongest evidence the Crown will later have against Marek.

**10. Seven Days** (day 11, seed house, morning into evening, ~5,000 words)
The cooperative makes a costed list on the back of a seed order. Tova gives the return arithmetic: 4 hundredweight a month of bought compost at 4 shillings is eighty pounds a month against a treasury of five pounds eleven, and a bed with no return kills seed in a fortnight. Lissa asks, in front of everyone, whether Marek intends to hand the fieldbook in. Tova's return arithmetic is **confirmation, not revelation** — the compost-return link is already on the page from Ch 9. Perrin Osse serves two documents: the district's filing note, and Kellan's second letter — the gasket and the seal offered free, exclusivity removed, with the accusation left in. **The cooperative votes four things and one refusal.** Four: a seal built by hand with the date, the hands, and a witness, signed by someone recorded as never having done the work; one shared draw with a written return plus a standing pressure log open to anybody and a monthly root-change report filed with the sub-office; a repair attempt Sera Quill can certify, on her signed scope, with *as it stands tonight* on the front of it; and Tova's seed annex, entered in the cooperative's book and offered to the district as a condition of renewal. The refusal: Kellan's letter, declined in front of the district's own clerk, who asks that the refusal be recorded. **The bypass is not a fifth vote** — Marek writes it in the margin with a figure against it and no money, and Dunmar rules under section nine that a note by an officer is evidence of what the cooperative intended and not of what it did, so it is carried as a note, unnumbered and unsigned by anyone else. On Tova's evidentiary rule — any reading taken by an unlicensed hand is logged in the seed house and may be checked by anyone who asks — the fieldbook goes into Lissa's custody under a written use log, and Marek does not argue and gets no speech about it. Sera signs the scope. Nia says the cart is booked for the twentieth and asks for a written list of what she takes and where it goes, with a column for destination and a column for who stood there when it went. **The chapter closes in a scene, not a ledger**: the room empties, his mother stays at the table with the book closed in front of her and makes him say out loud what he has, which begins *I do not have a plan* and then gives the list and then the fieldbook in her keeping and the fact that he has to come to this table and ask for it; she tells him a custody is not a forgiveness and would rather he learned the difference here than at a hearing, and leaves him the lamp, a warning about the compost page, and the night. **Change:** he is the man in the room who is not holding the instrument, and he says so out loud to the one person who will hold him to it. **Cost:** the fieldbook, and an obligation the cooperative voted for itself.

## Volume 01, Batch 0002 (days 12–20), files in `workspace/volume-01/batch-0002/`

**11. What Her Name On It Costs** (day 12, Thursday, well head, all day, ~3,300 words)
Sera Quill comes down the pit with a lamp on her belt and sends Marek up the ladder before he can get his boots off. **The examination produces two findings and only one of them is the cooperative's.** First, the pad is not a seal and the log is a lie — known since the first morning. Second, and worse for everyone: there is a **callus on the wound, a clean pale lip of new wood across the top third of the cut face, with a wet line on its lower edge**, because a man dressed four seasons of tar over it once a year and the coat kept the wood alive. Untreated, the wound would be a foot longer than it is; cut back and dressed in the first season it would have closed by the third. Kellan's log is a lie and Kellar's work is real, and Sera says she does not know how to write both in one line. **Then the dangerous job, priced out loud**: she cannot certify a repair, only supervise one; her certificate carries her personal liability because she is a freelancer with no employer, no district behind her, and no office that would stand in front of her at a hearing — "the charge is that a licensed engineer's name on a node is a promise that the node is somebody's to answer for, and the somebody is me." **Marek is refused the pit**, and told why at length: two contacts, four seconds each, the second dearer than the first, and a third would either give him something true that no clerk can use or take a piece of him he cannot identify. "I cannot let you do it, because you cannot be a witness at your own well." She signs first, he signs under her, and the order of the two names is the most expensive thing he does all day. The **second visit is £14, payable in advance, asked for on the fifteenth**. Ends: Ilsa tells him to tell the five people at a table, not at the pit, because a man told at a table has come to a decision and a man told at the pit has been informed. **Change:** the liability is named and the cost is priced. **Cost:** the instrument is his no more, and he cannot go in the pit again.

**12. The Column With Nothing In It** (day 13, Friday, the gate, the low acre, the well head, ~3,200 words)
The crew is assembled by asking, and three of the four say yes for the wrong reasons. **Jonas Byre** (the north-row man with the cough, named here for the first time) says yes because he is tired of being a man who is surprised, and asks that the reason be written down somewhere. **Anneke Roos**, forty-one, farms the low acre, and names the real objection: not that it is dangerous but that four unlicensed people will be the four names in a document about a thing that went wrong, while the two who arranged it are not in it. She will go, on one condition, spoken and then repeated: *I know less about this than the man who is asking me and I am going in anyway, and if I am wrong I want to be able to point at the hour.* **The first lesson is that a record is not a description** — four things in every entry, what was done and nothing that was not, a name against every hand, the hour and the day the work happened and not the day there was time to write it, and what a stranger could check without asking. "If you cannot date it, you write *cannot be dated* in those words and you do not guess." The **standing pressure log's first page** is ruled by Lissa Vale with eight columns; the last is *licensed person present*, and it is filled with the word **none**, underlined, because a record with a blank in it is a record with a hole in it. Anneke identifies it as the only part of the page that cannot be argued with. The log's first entry runs two to four o'clock. **The pulse arrives at 3:40**, five beats about four seconds apart, and is logged as *cause not established* on Sera's instruction — "do not look for one between now and the eighteenth, or you will find one and it will be the wrong one." **Change:** a crew that is not a queue. **Cost:** the day ends with a column full of the word *none*.

**13. The Line She Would Not Sign** (day 14, Saturday, the pit, ~2,500 words)
The pit is bailed out by hand — nine buckets a round, some three hundred rounds, the iron water tipped on the embankment and never into the return main, because Tova's veto on the vault is in writing. Then **the first refusal to sign, and it is correct.** The third hour's entry has been drafted as *head opened for examination of the wound. Wound inspected. No adverse condition observed.* **Anneke Roos will not sign it.** There was no examination: the pad is on and nobody has touched the wound. Four unlicensed people were within reach of a living conduit for the better part of an hour and the entry does not say so. She rewrites it at nineteen lines instead of four, including that she put her left hand to the elbow on the cradle, that the cradle moved, and that she did not like it. Sera Quill tells Marek it is the first entry in six years she would put in front of a hearing officer unaltered, and that a cooperative has never written one for her. The rule about **an entry made at the hour it describes** is written on the inside cover, attributed to Anneke Roos, signed by Gael Senn and initialled by Sera Quill, with Jonas Byre's name round the edge of the last page in pencil. Tova refuses the pit water for the trays on smell and on her veto, and the mill floor is written into the return column as labor twice a day with two names on it. **Marek closes the day's log himself, gets to the fourth hour, and does not improve a line that is nearly true.** **Change:** the rule that will convict him. **Cost:** he learns the lesson on Saturday and breaks it on Monday night.

**14. Four Seasons In Four Layers** (day 15, Sunday, the pit, all day, ~3,200 words)
The pad comes off in **four layers, not as a lump**, because it never bonded to anything and each season's coat ran down over the one below and set a lip. The top two come off whole in hard black shells about a foot across over six hours; the third has gone to soup in the flood and comes off with hot water and a flat knife and four hundred buckets; the base is still on. **Ilsa Kern holds the flat knife** and does eleven seasons' worth of it in a minute. **Jonas Byre goes down to take the bar and cannot get his leg**, and has to be hauled out on his hands and knees, and says he is not going down four feet again, and asks that it be written up as a man in a pit who should not have been in a pit rather than as a thing about his chest. **The crew is four.** The base coat — the fourth of the four — comes off at half past two in one piece, with a shallow bowl in it, and the bowl is the shape of the callus that has been growing over the top of the cut for four seasons. Sera Quill does not leave the rim all day and comes down the ladder herself to help haul Byre up, and the working notes for the fifteenth name her against the hour. Underneath: pale, clean, dry-looking scarwood with a ridge of new wood across the upper third and a wet line on it. **The material is the pad itself** — four baskets of the chopped fiber from the lower coats, which came out of the flood dry inside a black soup and snap rather than squash, and is the coarsest pack in the county. Sera's method, given in the shed: a seal is a fill *and* a restraint, and the restraint is what does the work; four green withies into **the four unused sockets in the wire cradle, which have been there since before anybody alive knew what they were for**, woven and tied, sacking over, tied down, nothing tarred because withies can be cut out in a year and a pot of tar cannot. The return is labor and compost and neither exists, so the return will be **monthly maintenance with a name on it**. Then she names the price: **£14, on the twentieth, in advance, asked in the shed in front of the four, and if it is not there she will not come.** **Change:** the material problem is solved by taking the dead pad apart, and the wound is a third closed. **Cost:** the crew is four, and there is no tar in this fen.

**15. A Letter With Somebody Else's Name On It** (day 16, Monday, the gate and the pit, ~4,000 words)
**Bram Oskell serves the district's monitored-repair offer at eight:** material, a two-person crew for six days, a district-retained licensed engineer, monthly inspection for twelve months — **for exclusive access to the head, the housing, and the node for the term.** Kellan has written to the sub-office disclaiming it, which Oskell notes is the first time in nineteen years a charter holder has written that sentence to him. Marek asks Oskell for **Kellan's compound book** — batch weights, pot temperatures, four seasons of dressing dates — and Oskell takes it back and is back the same day. **The refusal is taken at the pit, in front of the four, in the rain, at ten o'clock, and it is a struck-through clause and a marginal note in Marek's own hand, not a motion and not a table.** The material is not the reason; the condition is. Perrin Osse records it as the **third refusal this cooperative has entered since the third day**, and says the district does not admire it. Anneke Roos makes him write the cost where they will find it in February, initials it as an opinion rather than a record, and says: refusing the tar is why the seal is withies and sacking, and if it goes it goes because they said no. **The Undercommons courier comes at two** — **Marda Vole**, a mender of implements on the old peat roads, who will not be asked about anything and says the number unprompted: **N-2214 is die-struck into branches on four holdings in three other provinces, the same die, three of them from men who had never met.** She refuses to say whose branches, gives her own name unprompted, and — the actual gift — **the procedure: nobody gives a cooperative the table, but every renewal comes back with the district's works-schedule extract attached, because the form's first box asks what is already scheduled and a clerk will not send ninety forms with an empty box.** Her last line: fill the box as the number says, because that box is the only part of the form anybody in his position will ever be believed about. **Oskell returns at dusk with Kellan's own hand refusing the compound book**, and the reason is correct: the book is the record that there was never a seal in that pit, and handing it over would let a district officer read a holding repair as a Crown specification — "a man who has dressed a wound once a year for four years for nothing is not a man anybody should be able to turn into a witness for a coat, and you have been trying to do that to me since the fourth day." **Kellan has never apologized and nobody asks him to.** Ends with the levy: not a motion, a paper at the table at nine, sixteen households, **fourteen pounds does not buy a week of compost, compost is eighty pounds a month**, and **Anneke Roos signs first and makes him write the sentence: *it is a woman's fee, and the woman is putting her own name on top of his, and we are raising it out of sixteen houses to pay her.*** **Change:** Crown money refused, the courier's fact and procedure in hand, the levy open. **Cost:** the last tar in the fen, and the district's third refusal on file.

**16. Ninety Rows And No Headings** (day 17, Tuesday, the seed house table, ~3,700 words)
Dunmar Fitch files the renewal out at eight with the cause Marek gave him in a shed on the third day and would not enter in the cooperative's book: *return branch as built falls short of the specification; cause not established.* He states the difference between a minute and a filing and says he will write *we chose not to* anyway. **Perrin Osse rides out with the answer**: the application endorsed four days late, and attached to it a printed **works-schedule extract, six columns, no headings, ninety rows**, produced because the form's first box asks the applicant to list works already scheduled and no clerk sends ninety forms with an empty box. It is not to be kept, quoted, or copied. **Row forty-one is N-2214/K and then five more figures across: four dates, all marked, and a fifth figure that is also marked and is a hold nobody in the room can identify.** The two behind today are the two stamped cut faces; the next execution is the twenty-seventh, a Friday. Tova Reed works out what it is from the arithmetic rather than the shape: it is not a maintenance log, it is a **forward work programme with a horizon on it** — the two dates that are behind are the last two rungs of a ladder, and the schedule is not slipping, it is **on time**. **Sera Quill gives ground, and it costs her:** she says, unprompted and with the district's clerk in the room, *I have worked with district schedule forms.* Perrin Osse enters it with the hour. Then she reads the columns — first the item, second the **order** date, third, fourth and sixth executions, and the fifth **not a date at all but a hold, suspended until released** — and gives the batch's central discovery: **an order is not a permission. An order is a man in an office, on a date, deciding this cut is due, so the date it was done is not the date it was necessary, and the first order in that row is older than Marek's oldest cut.** She refuses a third time, by the same two names, and both refusals are entered. Marek's cost: **nine days building a case against carelessness, and the truth is that the cut was on time.** The pad is still a pad, the log is still a lie, the wound is still open; the wound is a *programme*, and it comes back in nine days. The second **section-nine margin note** goes in beside the bypass note — unnumbered, unfunded, signed by nobody but Marek, dated, *evidence of what the cooperative knows and not of what it has done.* Dunmar also confesses in the margin that he wrote *none known* in the first box of his own application at seven that morning, and that the cooperative's survival rested on Bray happening to be interested in the wrong thing. Perrin Osse returns the sheet and says she will report that the cooperative handed it back faster than she offered it. **Change:** the batch's discovery, and the twenty-seventh. **Cost:** the case he built was aimed at the wrong thing, and the district has a form on which he is now a liar.

**17. The Half-Of-An-Hour** (day 18, Wednesday, the yard, ~3,700 words)
Bray opens with the three things at the front and says he has no immediate-risk finding and that the tenth day's finding stands. **The seal is two days old, four pounds of willow, withy and seed-house sacking, a date cut into a stake and the five names that worked it under it — and the fifth name is not Marek's, and nothing on the stake says what comes next, because on the sixteenth nobody had written down yet what comes next.** It is the first thing of its kind he has seen in twenty-two years. He reads the working notes twice and finds on their first page the schedule of four seasons of compound pad in four hands and then a fifth line in a different hand that is not about the pad at all — *a confession with a time on it*, the first document of its kind he has had out of a cooperative. Then he reads the standing log, and **last the inside cover, and stops**, and says the rule there is his own practice and is going to cost them the morning. He asks one question. The work was finished at four on the sixteenth. **The completion entry was made at nine the next morning, alone, unwitnessed, and does not disclose it.** Marek answers: *because I wanted to know whether it held before I wrote down that it was done.* **Bray rules under the fourth part, second clause — a record made after the hour of the work is a recollection and not a record — that the completion is not evidenced as maintenance and that the node is not shown to be maintained, and says plainly that he is not the officer who determines the referred application and does not pretend his finding settles it. The charter lapses at midnight on its own terms whatever the referral does next, and after midnight there is nothing to refer.** He is explicit that the ruling is about a line of ink and not about four pairs of hands, that the district does not contest the workmanship, and that he is sorry to Mrs Roos **as a man and not as an officer**. **He does not seal the head, take the seed stock, or refer the charter** — there is no immediate risk — **but the renewal is referred rather than determined by him and the charter lapses on its own terms at midnight regardless, the emergency clause is neither spent nor extinguished, any report from anybody reopens it, the file does not travel with him, and a Crown-funded monitored repair sits in the cooperative's own file that the district does not let offers die.** Kellan Rusk, who came because he is charter holder of record and stood at the back for two hours, is asked by nobody and says two sentences. **Sera Quill puts a Crown seal specification into a district file as a thing to be compared against, at her own professional risk, and asks for it to be entered then and not after.** **Change:** the charter, from a date into a siege. **Cost:** everything, and the loss is entirely the cooperative's own hand.

**18. Eight Trays And A Letter** (day 19, Thursday, the seed house and the loading bench, ~3,300 words)
The thing that keeps Marek awake is not the charter: it is that **the pump still runs and there is no longer any document in Tarrow Fen that says it may.** The seed house has 1,100 turns a day because nobody has sealed the head, and any report from anybody — including a neighbor who tastes the water and finds iron — reopens the clause. Ilsa Kern's reduction: *it is not a siege, it is a watch, and a watch wants a person on it.* Sera Quill confirms the reading of the lapse: no charter means no right to draw, to work, or to enter anything in a district book, and the cooperative's standing procedure from today is that it **hands any district man its own keys and lets him do it on his own authority.** She also explains, unasked, why she has been in the yard six days past a fourteen-pound scope. The **levy is counted with the door open**: **£12 6** from sixteen households, fourteen names, nine contributors, one shilling with *and I am sorry* on the line entered separately, and **£1 14 made up by Marek out of his own money, both amounts entered.** The **seed annex is made real as eight trays on a loading bench**, read aloud line by line and then the whole page again, in columns — crate, quantity, condition, destination, who is standing there, witness. **Lissa Vale will not let the trust's tin go on a hired cart** and it goes into the press under the ledger, *retained at risk, and the trust knows where it is*, initialled by Tova Reed and signed by Lissa in that order. Tova names the condition out loud in a yard: the March's last dry-land line is going into the last place in the fen with a living comparison, and if the fen goes the March has nothing to compare for four years and nine thousand people eat what that crop is. At four Marek writes **the letter to Kellan Rusk** — he will not ask for a reduced draw, will not ask for a return, will not speak about an unmeasured log, and will not say publicly that the acceptance column was a lie, because Kellan's refusal was correct. The letter goes on Nia's list, because it must be delivered by hand in Fennmere and the cooperative has no business riding to a gate there. **Change:** the annex exists as objects, the money exists, the letter exists. **Cost:** £1 14 of Marek's own, and a condition accepted out loud.

**19. What He Went To The Table For** (day 20, Friday, the seed house table, the well house, ~3,900 words)
Marek asks for the fieldbook at his mother's table with five people in the room, and **asks it badly first.** Lissa says no twice, instantly, and then makes him say what he actually meant: he does not need it, he cannot read it, and the last two times he was alone in a pit when it happened. The use-log line is written in the seven-column form with the purpose, the licensed person, and *M. Vale will not touch the instrument and will stand outside the housing*, and **Anneke Roos reads the whole line aloud before she signs it.** The choice at the load test is between the cooperative's honest 720 turns and the log's 1,100, and Marek chooses 1,100 in front of the crew and on the record, because 720 is a lie about a load. **The single Fieldbook panel of the batch appears here, in the book, in Sera Quill's hands, at the seam, with Marek on the boards outside the collar: *The dressing is holding. / Full load will show you the wood. / Choose.*** She reads it out, because a reading taken in a pit is not a reading until somebody else has heard it, and enters that he asked her for it before he chose, which in thirty years is the fourth time and the first three men did not ask. The load runs in three stages from half past ten to one — 720, then 900, then **1,100 from about twenty past eleven, held to one, and nobody stops the pump**, with the load entered in the log before the reading. At 950 the frame hums; **the beat comes up about ten minutes into the full draw in runs of seven and eight**, counted by Gael Senn, who declines to give it a name because *pressure pulse* is the licensed word and a finding, entered by Sera Quill as *cause not established*. **The seal holds.** At half past twelve there is a **quarter of an inch** of seepage at the six o'clock corner — not a leak, a weep, the pack going in where it always went in — measured with a rule twice and written down before the rest of the sentence, with **half an inch named as where Sera stops calling it a weep**, and a warning that a leak under sacking at full draw goes into the housing and not into the pit. **Then the reading, and it is Sera's and not Marek's, and she reconciles it with Finding 3 herself before anybody else can:** the eighth gave valve, hand, wait, valve, lamp, valve, which is maintenance; the twentieth gives **cut, wait, cut**, with no well in it at all and nothing in the order that belongs to anything above ground. Her explanation is that a fieldbook reads what a node has recently had done to it. She refuses a third time where she saw it and what it is, and Dunmar enters all three refusals **and a fourth line in her words: *a licensed reader at the same seam did not obtain the sequence obtained on the eighth; both are logged; the difference is unaccounted for.*** **The certificate is worth one day's evidence about one wound and cannot be entered as a charter condition, and Sera says so before anybody builds a winter on it.** The return she writes into it is **monthly maintenance with a day, an amount and a name, on the first of every month, entered in the standing pressure log** — and when nobody volunteers, **Anneke Roos names Gael Senn and gives the reason, and Gael insists the entry read *nothing found* and not *all well*.** The certificate carries **three** names — Sera Quill's, then Gael Senn's, then Marek's, who signs without being asked, with Lissa entering the hour at the foot. Sera checks his arm for the third time, finds nothing, says the same thing, and says she will not say it a fourth time. **Change:** the seal is standing and suspect, and the person who holds it in January is not Marek. **Cost:** the one instrument in the world he may not open, and the largest name on every page of it is not his.

**20. The Last Line Of The List** (day 20, Friday, dusk, the gate, ~2,600 words)
The carter waits all day because the fen road is not a road after three in November, and Nia Vale keeps the list herself: **item, crate, quantity, condition, where it is going, who is standing there when it goes, witness.** Twenty-two lines, read aloud as they are entered and the whole page read again from the bench to about thirty people. The Longneck's eight living trays; the trust's tin, which Lissa Vale will not put on a hired cart and which goes into the press instead; a hundredweight of the thinnest bought compost, a fortnight; the March's two bundles of paper; and one sealed letter to Kellan Rusk to be delivered by hand. **The last line is Nia Vale: one person, one bag, one box, no crate, Cray by the Salt March road, and the witness is Lissa Vale.** Lissa signs the witness column and says nothing, and goes inside, and Nia tells her not to do it in a yard because she has spent nine days making a document a stranger can read in five years. Nia then says the only thing she is actually leaving behind, to about thirty heads: it is not a success, nothing worked, and what she is taking to Cray is a *documented bad season*, which is a different thing. Then the **first monthly root-change report**, written out by her by lamplight on the mill-keeper's board, 240 words, honest and entirely bad news, including that N-2214/K will be executed on the twenty-seventh and that the cooperative cannot object. The cart goes at ten past five; the gate shuts at half past five. **Ending type: departure.** Marek counts four crates he stacked against the seed house wall, and finds a seed-house lamp still hooked over one of them, and Anneke Roos tells him she is not the one who will be on the first of every month, and the volume does not get Nia back.
